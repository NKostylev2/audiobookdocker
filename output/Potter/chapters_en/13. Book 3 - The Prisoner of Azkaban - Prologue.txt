The Prisoner of Azkaban

The morning of the thirty-first of October, 1981, Harry Potter overturned a basket of freshly washed clothes and declared himself victorious.

The declaration consisted of one delighted shriek, several enthusiastic kicks and a stream of sounds that might have meant anything from I have conquered this household to I appear to be missing a sock. Whatever the intended message, it carried easily through the cottage.

James Potter opened one eye.

Beside him, Lily made the exhausted noise of someone who had slept in intervals no longer than forty minutes and had not been consulted about waking again.

A second crash came from the nursery.

"That," Lily murmured into the pillow, "was your son."

"Our son."

"He becomes yours whenever furniture is involved."

James pushed himself upright and searched blindly for his glasses. His hand found a book, the corner of Lily's abandoned pillow and something cold which proved to be a cup of tea neither of them remembered bringing upstairs.

"Where are my—"

Lily reached across him, plucked the glasses from his hair and placed them into his hand.

"You know," James said, settling them upon his nose, "most people wear these over their eyes."

"Most people don't fall asleep with them on their forehead."

"I was thinking."

"You were snoring."

"I think loudly."

Another sound came from across the landing: a wooden rattle, a soft thump and then silence.

James stopped smiling. The silence continued. He was out of bed before it had lasted three seconds.

The nursery door stood open. Thin autumn light entered through the window, touching the painted walls and the small collection of moving stars scattered across the curtains. Sirius had charmed them months ago. Lily had objected that a baby needed darkness in order to sleep. Sirius had explained that his godson required an early education in astronomy. The resulting compromise allowed the stars to appear only during daylight, which defeated their astronomical purpose but had preserved the friendship.

Harry sat in the middle of the floor wearing a yellow sleeping shirt, one sock and no trousers. An overturned laundry basket rested beside him. Clean clothes lay across the rug in every direction, and a knitted jumper had somehow landed over the toy dragon on the shelf.

Harry held a wooden rattle in one hand.

He stared at James.

James examined him in return.

"Were you attempting an escape?"

Harry struck the floor with the rattle.

"I see. And did the basket begin the hostilities?"

The rattle struck the floor again.

"Thought so."

James crossed the room and lifted him. Harry immediately caught hold of his father's glasses and pulled with impressive determination.

"Yes, excellent," James said through watering eyes. "Your mother has been trying to improve my face for years. Perhaps you'll have better luck."

Lily appeared in the doorway, tying the belt of her dressing gown. Her hair had escaped its plait during the night and now fell untidily around her shoulders. She took in the overturned basket, the trail of clothes and the absence of trousers.

"I left him asleep."

"He woke up."

"I had gathered that."

"He has ambitions."

"He has cold legs."

James looked down. Harry kicked both bare legs, apparently to demonstrate that temperature was a political concern beneath his notice.

"He seems comfortable."

"He also tried to eat a button yesterday."

"A visionary."

"A menace."

"Our visionary menace."

Lily attempted to remain stern. Harry smiled at her, revealing four small teeth and an expression of such shameless adoration that her resistance lasted less than a second.

"Oh, don't do that," she told him.

Harry reached for her.

James surrendered him with a sigh. "Betrayed in my own home."

"You were always temporary."

"I was here first."

"And yet he prefers me. Strange."

Harry buried one hand in Lily's hair. She kissed his forehead before checking it automatically for warmth. The gesture had become part of lifting him: one arm beneath him, one hand against his brow, a quiet count of breaths. James had seen her do it a hundred times. She never appeared aware of it.

"Hungry?" she asked.

Harry produced a hopeful noise.

"Traitor," James said. "I offered you intellectual conversation."

"You offered him an interrogation about a laundry basket."

"And he made several compelling points."

Lily carried Harry towards the stairs. James followed, gathering clothes from the floor and dropping them back into the basket with less care than their recent washing probably deserved.

The cottage was small enough that every room seemed to overhear the others. The floorboards creaked beneath familiar steps. Pipes knocked sleepily within the walls, and the narrow staircase complained whenever James placed his weight upon the third board from the top. They had been living there long enough to learn its habits but not long enough for those habits to feel permanent.

Nothing had felt permanent for some time.

Downstairs, the fire had nearly gone out. A red glow remained beneath the ash, giving the sitting room a stale trace of warmth. Last night's mugs occupied the low table beside an unfinished game of wizard chess. One white knight continued to accuse a black bishop of cowardice despite having spent nine hours facing the wrong direction.

Beyond the windows, Godric's Hollow lay beneath a veil of pearly mist. The church tower rose above slate rooftops, its clock face blurred by condensation. The stone lane below the cottage shone with rain from the night before. Yellow leaves gathered in gutters and against garden walls, trembling whenever the wind found them.

Someone had decorated the gate opposite with paper bats.

They fluttered upon their strings while two Muggle children argued on the pavement about whether a pumpkin required eyebrows. Their mother stood nearby holding a small carving knife and offering suggestions neither child appeared interested in accepting.

James watched them through the glass.

Halloween.

There would be costumes that evening, bowls of sweets beside doors and children racing between houses beneath masks. The Potters had carved a pumpkin of their own, although James had given it moving eyes and Lily had called the result deeply disturbing. It sat on the kitchen windowsill, glaring suspiciously at anyone who approached the sink.

They would not be opening their door.

Nobody would come to it.

That was the entire purpose of the cottage now.

Lily placed Harry into his high chair and fastened him in while James rebuilt the fire. He added two logs, nudged the embers with his wand and watched orange light travel slowly through the blackened wood.

"Porridge?" Lily asked.

James straightened. "Is that a question or a threat?"

"It's breakfast."

"It can be both."

"You cooked yesterday."

"And there were no survivors."

"The toast was burnt."

"The toast was strongly defended."

Lily opened a cupboard. "Porridge."

Harry slapped both palms against the tray.

"At last," James said, turning towards him. "Someone respects leadership."

Breakfast filled the kitchen with steam, warmth and the smell of oats. Lily mashed a banana into Harry's bowl while James sweetened his own portion until she took the sugar from him. The pumpkin on the windowsill followed the movement with suspicious orange eyes.

For ten minutes, the war existed somewhere beyond the cottage.

It remained in the folded copy of the Daily Prophet beside the bread bin. It occupied the names James and Lily avoided mentioning while Harry listened. It waited within the protective charms layered over the walls and beneath the unease that accompanied every unexpected sound.

But it did not sit at the table.

At the table there was only Harry refusing porridge from the spoon while attempting to seize the bowl with both hands.

"You cannot hold it," Lily told him.

Harry leaned forward.

"You'll wear it."

He made a frustrated sound.

James lowered his voice. "She's controlling, isn't she?"

"James."

"I warned you, Harry. Redheads."

Lily pointed her spoon at him. "Would you like to finish feeding him?"

"Desperately."

She handed over the bowl.

James approached the task with the confidence of a man who had mistaken enthusiasm for competence. Harry watched him lift the spoon, opened his mouth obediently and then struck James's wrist with one hand.

Porridge landed across James's glasses.

Lily closed her eyes.

Harry screamed with laughter.

James remained motionless, spoon suspended in the air.

"I have been ambushed."

Lily pressed her lips together.

"You saw that," he said. "It was planned."

"Of course."

"My own son."

"You encouraged his spirit."

"There are limits."

Harry struck the tray again, delighted with himself.

Lily turned away under the pretence of retrieving a cloth. Her shoulders shook.

"You're laughing."

"I am not."

"Look at me and say that."

"I'm searching for a towel."

"The towels are behind you, Lily."

When Lily finally turned around, she was openly smiling.

James removed his glasses and stared through the porridge coating them.

"I hope you both understand that this household would collapse without me."

"Possibly from relief."

The owl struck the kitchen window before James could answer.

Lily's smile disappeared.

The bird hit the glass with a frantic scrape of claws, recovered in the air and began beating one wing against the pane. Harry startled. James had his wand raised before the second impact.

Neither adult moved to open the window.

The owl clung to the narrow ledge, feathers soaked and crooked. A small roll of parchment was tied to one leg.

James and Lily looked at each other across the kitchen.

The protective charms should not have allowed an unfamiliar owl to find the cottage. Then again, a letter could reach them if its sender already possessed the Secret. The Fidelius Charm did not make them unreachable to the handful of people who knew where to look.

Lily lifted Harry from his chair. He protested at being removed from the remaining porridge, but she held him against her shoulder and stepped away from the glass.

"Do you recognise it?"

James studied the bird. Grey feathers. White patch beneath its throat. One wing bent oddly from exhaustion.

"No."

"Could be from Dumbledore."

"He uses a tawny."

"The Order has more than one owl."

"I know."

Lily's expression tightened. "Then why did you answer as though I didn't?"

James lowered his wand slightly.

"Sorry."

Rainwater slid from the bird's feathers and gathered along the sill. It tapped the window again, weaker now.

James approached. The warding runes beneath the frame remained quiet. No warning glowed across the wood. He tested the latch with his wand, released the outer layer of protection and opened the window no farther than necessary.

The owl fell inside.

James caught it against his chest before it reached the floor. Up close, he saw blood darkening the feathers around one leg.

"Hell."

Lily cleared a space upon the table. James laid the bird carefully beside the abandoned bowls while she fetched a cloth and a shallow dish of water. Harry twisted in her arm, reaching towards the owl with immediate interest.

"No," Lily said, shifting him away. "Gentle."

James untied the message. The parchment carried no seal, only a strip of ordinary brown string. He touched his wand to it and murmured the first identification charm.

The paper did not react.

A second spell revealed no curse, poison or concealed enchantment.

"James."

"I'm checking."

"The owl's hurt."

"I can see that."

Lily gave him a look.

He cut the string.

Only three lines had been written inside.

The Rosiers were seen near Bristol.

Two safe houses may have been compromised.

Remain where you are. Await further contact.

No name appeared beneath the warning.

James read it twice. Lily watched his face.

"What is it?"

He handed her the parchment.

She read without sitting. Harry had discovered the loose collar of her dressing gown and was attempting to pull it over his own head.

"Who sent this?"

"No idea."

"That handwriting looks familiar."

"It's been disguised."

"You're sure?"

"Letters slope differently halfway through. Whoever wrote it was concentrating at the beginning."

Lily looked towards the wet owl.

"Could be Caradoc."

"Caradoc has been missing for months."

"It could have been written before—"

"The owl wouldn't survive carrying it for months."

"No."

The single word quietened the kitchen.

James stroked two fingers carefully along the injured bird's back. Its eyes remained closed, breast lifting rapidly beneath soaked feathers.

The message told them nothing useful. They were already remaining where they were. Every hour of every day was spent remaining where they were. James had become extremely accomplished at it and resented every second.

"What about the signature charm?" Lily asked.

"Nothing."

"Can you trace where it came from?"

"Not without breaking the concealment wards around the house."

"Then don't."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know."

She said it quickly, but something in James tightened anyway. He folded the parchment along its existing crease.

Before Harry, he and Lily had argued like people throwing open windows. Loudly, dramatically and with the certainty that whatever entered could be weathered together. Since going into hiding, their disagreements had changed. Every question seemed to carry another beneath it. Every careless word risked touching the fear neither of them could afford to examine for too long.

Lily set Harry down inside the wooden playpen near the hearth. He immediately crawled towards the collection of coloured blocks Sirius had given him and selected the red one for destruction.

"Still nothing from Sirius?" she asked.

James looked at the window.

"No."

"He said he would write yesterday."

"He probably found something."

"He would write if he found something."

"Not if he thought sending an owl might expose us."

"He has sent an owl every other time."

"Then perhaps he hasn't found anything."

Lily folded her arms.

"You just said—"

"I know what I said."

"James."

"He'll be all right."

"That isn't what I asked."

James set the anonymous warning upon the table. "Sirius can look after himself."

"I know he can. That does not mean silence from him is normal."

It was not.

Sirius wrote exactly as he lived: suddenly, excessively and with no regard for whether anyone needed three pages describing a disagreement in a Cardiff pub. When delayed, he usually sent an owl apologising for the delay, followed by another retracting the apology on principle. His messages arrived at strange hours, covered in additions and arrows and complaints concerning the limitations of parchment.

He had not written for two days.

Two days was not long. Under ordinary circumstances it would have meant nothing.

Very little about their circumstances remained ordinary.

"He was going to check on Peter," Lily said.

James kept his expression still.

"That doesn't mean anything."

"I didn't say it did."

"You were thinking it loudly."

She gave him a tired look. "Apparently it runs in the family."

Inside the playpen, Harry bit one corner of the red block.

James dragged a hand through his hair. It stayed upright, achieving no visible change.

Peter had been nervous during his last visit. Peter was always nervous now, but James had attributed it to the war, the disappearances and the fact that Voldemort appeared to be closing his fist around Britain one household at a time.

Fear was not suspicious. Anyone who remained unafraid had either misunderstood the situation or belonged to the side causing it.

Still, Peter's hands had trembled while accepting tea. He had asked twice whether the defensive charms had changed and apologised immediately after the second question. When Harry cried upstairs, Peter had flinched hard enough to spill milk over his sleeve.

James had pretended not to notice.

Sirius had noticed everything.

That was the problem with Sirius. He could disregard six obvious dangers while detecting the seventh before anyone else entered the room.

"He said he'd make certain Wormtail was secure," James said. "That's all."

"Peter hasn't written either."

"Peter never knows whether he's allowed."

"He usually sends three letters asking."

A reluctant smile touched James's mouth.

Peter's messages tended to arrive in anxious clusters. The first asked whether they needed anything. The second apologised in case asking had endangered them. The third requested that they destroy the previous two and forget he had written.

Silence from Peter felt unnatural in an entirely different way from silence from Sirius.

Lily crouched beside the playpen. Harry offered her the damp block. She accepted it with appropriate solemnity.

"Thank you."

Harry grabbed another.

"I hate this," she said quietly.

James looked at her.

She was not speaking about the block.

"I hate sitting here while everyone else is still fighting. I hate waiting for letters and wondering whether the person who sent them is alive. I hate that every owl sounds like someone arriving to tell us another friend is dead."

"I know."

"I hate not knowing what's happening."

James crossed the room and sat upon the floor beside her. The rug compressed beneath his knees. Harry crawled towards the bars nearest them and pushed a blue block through the gap.

James caught it.

"We know what matters."

"Do we?"

"Yes."

Lily looked at Harry.

His hair had grown in three uneven directions, one of which seemed determined to reproduce James's own. There was porridge drying near his left ear. He had no understanding of safe houses, prophecies or names whispered after dark. His world consisted of familiar hands, warm food and the expectation that someone would come whenever he called.

James wanted him to keep that world so fiercely it frightened him.

"We keep him safe," he said.

Lily's fingers closed around Harry's hand through the bars.

"For how long?"

"As long as he needs us."

"That wasn't what I meant."

"I know."

Outside, the mist had begun to lift from the village. Roof tiles emerged in damp rows. A milk float rattled around the distant corner while someone opened the church doors. Ordinary life continued only a few gardens away, close enough to see and entirely beyond reach.

Harry shoved another block towards James.

James stacked it on top of the first.

"And after that?" Lily asked.

"After what?"

"After this ends."

"It will end."

"You're avoiding the question."

"I'm creating optimism."

"You're avoiding it optimistically."

James added a third block. The tower leaned.

"When this is over," he said, "we leave Britain for six months."

"Six months?"

"Possibly a year."

"With what money?"

"My parents left me enough."

"They did not leave you enough to finance every ridiculous idea you have until death."

"You underestimate their faith in my ridiculousness."

"Where would we go?"

"Somewhere warm."

"You hate hot weather."

"Somewhere moderately warm."

"Very adventurous."

"We'll take Harry to the sea."

Lily's expression changed.

James saw it and continued.

"Proper sea. Not the bit near Brighton where Sirius lost his shoes."

"He threw them at a gull."

"The gull had insulted him."

"The gull was eating a chip."

"With intent."

Harry pushed his hands beneath the tower and scattered every block across the rug.

James stared at the destruction.

"A critic."

Lily laughed.

It was not a large sound, but the room brightened around it.

James began rebuilding the tower. "We'll find somewhere quiet. A bigger kitchen, perhaps. I could put in one of those—"

"You are never altering another kitchen."

"That wall was structurally pessimistic."

"That wall was supporting the bedroom."

"It lacked ambition."

"And what will Harry do while you're attacking the architecture?"

"Develop character."

"He already has too much of yours."

"You like my character."

"In controlled amounts."

Harry reached through the bars and caught a fistful of James's hair.

"See?" James said. "He wants more."

Lily leaned against his shoulder.

For several minutes, they remained on the floor while their son dismantled everything James attempted to build. The injured owl drank from the shallow dish on the table. The fire strengthened, spreading heat across the cottage. Outside, the last of the mist withdrew from the lane.

It was easy, briefly, to imagine that they had been given a morning rather than merely loaned one.

By afternoon, the weather had turned.

Clouds gathered over Godric's Hollow in heavy layers, swallowing the weak sunlight and draining the colour from the village. Wind moved fitfully through the hedgerows. It set the branches scraping against one another and worried the garden gate upon its hinges until the latch began clicking at irregular intervals.

James repaired it three times.

The first attempt involved a screwdriver, frozen fingers and a refusal to admit that he had selected the wrong tool.

The second involved magic, a muttered threat and a disagreement over whether wood could be insolent.

The third required him to remove the gate completely, lean it against the stone wall and reconsider several choices which had led him to be defeated by a hinge.

From the kitchen window, Lily watched him lift it back into place.

"You could leave it," she called.

James turned, hair blown across his forehead. "And let it win?"

"It's a gate."

"That is precisely what it wants you to think."

She opened the back door. Warm air escaped around her, carrying the smell of bread and the faint sweetness of the pumpkin pie cooling beside the stove.

"You're using magic this time."

"I used magic last time."

"You threatened it last time."

"It responded to authority."

"It fell off."

"A temporary setback."

Harry sat upon Lily's hip, bundled in a blue jumper and clutching one of James's old Snitches. Its wings had been removed after he tried to swallow one, leaving it an ordinary golden ball with an inflated opinion of itself.

James fitted the final hinge into place and stepped back.

The gate remained upright.

"There."

A gust of wind struck it. The latch clicked.

James raised his wand.

Lily caught his wrist before he could cast.

"It's allowed to make noise."

"Not that noise."

"What is wrong with that noise?"

"It's smug."

"You need to come inside."

James lowered his wand. "You never support me."

"I married you."

"A lapse in judgement is not the same as support."

She kissed his cheek.

"Inside."

He followed her across the narrow garden, stamping mud from his boots at the threshold. Mud remained unimpressed by advanced concealment magic and crossed protected boundaries without the slightest difficulty.

The kitchen had grown golden with lamplight. Dusk pressed blue-grey against the windows. The carved pumpkin glared from the sill while its candle flame flickered behind pointed teeth.

Harry was placed upon a blanket with a collection of toys. He ignored all of them in favour of the wooden spoon Lily had been using.

James stole a piece of cooling crust.

She struck his knuckles with the cloth.

"That is for tonight."

"We aren't expecting anyone."

"That doesn't mean you eat the entire thing before dinner."

"I was testing it."

"You tested the last three pieces."

"I've got to be sure."

Lily moved the pie beyond his reach.

The wireless murmured softly from the sitting room. The presenter discussed heavy rain expected overnight, a closure on a road near Exeter and several unexplained disturbances which the Ministry had evidently decided could be disguised as gas leaks.

Neither James nor Lily remarked upon the final story.

James checked the mantel clock.

Half past four.

No owl.

He crossed to the front window and looked through the narrow gap in the curtains. Children had begun emerging from the houses farther down the lane, trailing adults and coloured fabric. A small witch in a pointed paper hat ran past a boy dressed as a vampire. Neither costume would have fooled anyone in the magical world, though the vampire possessed a remarkably convincing quantity of false blood.

Harry pulled himself upright using the edge of the sofa.

James turned at the soft grunt of effort.

His son stood unsteadily, one hand buried in the cushion, knees wobbling beneath him. Lily noticed at the same instant and froze beside the table.

Harry looked between them.

"Come on," James whispered.

Lily moved closer.

"Don't crowd him."

"I'm not."

"You're leaning."

"I'm encouraging."

"You're looming encouragingly."

Harry released the sofa.

For one perilous second, he remained upright. His face changed with the discovery, delight and alarm arriving together. Then he took one uneven step towards James.

His knee buckled.

Lily caught him before he struck the rug.

Harry stared at her, considered the injustice and began to howl.

"No, no." She lifted him. "You did it. You walked."

"He did," James said, grinning so hard his face hurt. "One whole step. Practically independent."

Harry cried against Lily's shoulder.

"You frightened yourself, didn't you?" she murmured.

James reached for him. Harry turned immediately and stretched both arms towards his father.

Lily handed him over.

James settled Harry against his chest and bounced him gently until the crying faded into damp, offended breaths.

"You were brilliant," he said. "Gravity cheated."

Harry gripped the front of his jumper.

"We won't count that round."

Lily wiped beneath Harry's eyes with her thumb.

"Your father has been blaming gravity for years."

"It has a documented bias."

Harry looked over James's shoulder towards the photographs upon the mantel. His attention fixed upon one near the end: James and Lily outside the cottage during summer, Harry held between them and Sirius leaning into the frame at the last instant. Every few seconds, the Sirius in the photograph pulled a ridiculous face behind James's head. Lily laughed. Photographic James noticed, turned and shoved him out of view. Sirius returned immediately.

Harry pointed.

"Pafoo."

James went still.

Lily looked at the photograph.

"Padfoot," James said.

"Pafoo," Harry insisted.

"That's right."

The photograph continued its happy loop. Sirius leaned into view. James shoved him away. Lily laughed without sound.

"He'll be unbearably pleased when we tell him," Lily said.

"When I tell him. I'm taking credit."

"For Harry learning Sirius's name?"

"I have mentioned it more."

"You called him an irresponsible fugitive yesterday."

"Affectionately."

Harry touched one finger to photographic Sirius's face.

The mantel clock ticked.

Five o'clock.

No owl.

James carried Harry away from the photographs.

"Bath?"

Lily glanced at the darkening windows. "Already?"

"He's covered in porridge."

"That happened at breakfast."

"I was leaving it to soak."

"You forgot."

"I was occupied by the gate."

"The gate you attacked for most of the afternoon."

James adjusted Harry higher against his chest. "You see how I'm persecuted?"

"Ba," Harry said.

"Exactly."

Upstairs, the bathroom filled with steam. James tested the water three times before Lily displaced him and declared it suitable. Harry sat in the shallow bath surrounded by charmed wooden boats which sailed in circles around his legs.

One wore miniature black sails.

Sirius's contribution.

It repeatedly rammed the other boats and shouted insults in a tiny voice. Lily had tried to remove the charm. The boat had bitten her.

Harry seized it and struck it against the water, sending droplets over James's shirt.

"That is your godfather's influence," Lily said.

James wiped water from his glasses. "He has excellent instincts."

"He enchanted a bath toy to swear."

"It says blast."

"It did not say blast when it bit me."

"The boat was frightened."

"The boat called me a—"

"Small ears," James reminded her.

Harry struck the water again.

When the bath ended, James wrapped him in a towel and carried him into the nursery. Lily followed with a clean sleeping shirt, pausing to retrieve James's wand from the windowsill before Harry could reach it.

"You left this here."

"I knew where it was."

"It was beside a one-year-old."

"A one-year-old who respects wand safety."

"He put a button in his mouth."

"That was an entirely separate department."

Lily placed the wand upon the dresser, farther from the cot.

"You'll forget it."

"I will not."

James forgot it within five minutes.

Harry resisted every stage of being dressed. He twisted free of sleeves, removed one sock while Lily attempted to fasten the other and treated trousers as a profound personal betrayal.

By the time he was ready for bed, James felt as though they had subdued a dragon using only knitwear.

"Story," he announced.

Lily sat in the chair beside the cot. "A short one."

Harry, now enclosed safely behind the wooden bars, bounced on the mattress.

James selected a book from the shelf. "This one?"

"No."

"You haven't seen it."

"You always choose that one."

"It has literary value."

"It has a dragon that sounds like Professor McGonagall."

"The resemblance is coincidental."

"It demands homework from the villagers."

"A reasonable expectation."

James opened the book.

The story concerned a dragon, three dishonest mice and a knight who had misplaced his sword somewhere between breakfast and destiny. James provided each character with a distinct voice. The dragon sounded exactly like Professor McGonagall. The mice sounded like Ministry officials. The knight sounded suspiciously like Sirius after several drinks.

Lily corrected him twice, then surrendered.

Harry's eyelids began to droop near the end of the third page. By the fourth, one hand had curled around James's finger through the cot bars. His breathing softened.

James continued reading at a whisper.

When the knight finally recovered his sword from beneath an unnecessarily judgemental goose, Harry was asleep.

James closed the book.

Neither parent moved.

Rain began tapping lightly against the nursery window. The sound gathered gradually, each drop joining the next until it formed a soft curtain around the cottage. Tiny charmed stars drifted across the blue fabric beside the cot, appearing and disappearing in patient patterns.

Harry's hand remained around James's finger.

Lily rested her head against his shoulder.

"He walked," she whispered.

"One step."

"It counts."

"Of course it counts."

"What if we forget?"

James looked at her. "Forget what?"

"Things like this."

"We won't."

"People forget."

"We're not people. We're exceptional."

She did not smile.

James turned back towards the cot.

Harry's face was round with sleep. Without his glasses, without the famous scar that did not yet exist, there was nothing about him anyone would consider historically significant. He was simply their son.

A boy who hated trousers, loved mashed banana and had taken one uncertain step across the sitting-room rug.

"We'll write it down," James said.

"Every little thing?"

"Everything important."

"How will we know what's important?"

"We'll write down all of it and let him decide."

Lily reached through the bars and brushed a curl away from Harry's forehead.

James imagined books filled with ordinary days. Harry's first sentence. His first accidental magic. The first time he mounted a broom without assistance. The name of his favourite teacher. The subjects he hated. The people he chose as friends. Arguments, holidays, broken bones, examination results and every small event which seemed insignificant until enough years had placed it beyond reach.

There would be time to record all of it. That belief didn't feel like hope anymore; it felt like fact.

"Come on," Lily whispered. "Let him sleep."

James eased his finger from Harry's hand. They left the door ajar and descended together. His wand remained upon the nursery dresser. Dinner passed without an owl.

They ate pumpkin pie afterwards, although James maintained that his earlier testing had reduced the available portions to an unfair degree. Lily accused him of creating the shortage. The argument continued into the sitting room, where she curled at one end of the sofa with a book and James attempted to finish the chess game.

The white knight called him a coward. James sacrificed it immediately.

Rain struck the windows with increasing force. Wind pressed beneath the eaves and travelled through the chimney in low, mournful breaths. The cottage settled around them, old timber contracting beside the fire.

At a quarter past seven, Lily lowered her book.

"Did you hear that?"

James looked up from the board.

Only the rain answered.

"Hear what?"

"The gate."

He waited. The latch clicked outside. Once, then once again. James listened more carefully.

"It's the wind."

"You fixed it, didn't you?"

"I attempted to fix it."

"Three times."

"I admire your confidence in me."

The latch sounded again. Lily placed her book aside. James reached automatically for his wand. His hand touched an empty pocket.

He frowned.

"Where did I—"

"The nursery."

Above them, a floorboard creaked. Both looked towards the ceiling. Harry shifted in his sleep. Nothing more followed.

James rose. A pressure touched the outer wards—a slight twinge through the magic as the concealment charms began to unfold.

James's blood turned cold. Only one thing could open the Fidelius Charm without force. Someone who possessed the Secret had brought the enemy to their door.

Lily was already standing.

"James?"

He lifted one hand. The front gate clicked for the final time. Footsteps crossed the garden path. They were slow, measured and entirely unhurried. The carved pumpkin upon the kitchen windowsill went dark.

James moved towards the stairs. The sitting-room candles narrowed until each flame became a blue point. The chess pieces stopped arguing. Rain continued to hammer against the glass, yet the sound seemed suddenly remote, separated from the cottage by something deeper than walls.

Lily's face had gone white.

"No," she whispered.

The front door opened. It did not splinter. No curse struck the wood. The lock simply turned, obedient to a power that had already been invited beyond every protection they possessed.

A tall figure entered the narrow hall. Water shone across black robes. Beneath the lowered hood, the face was pale and distorted, humanity stretched thin over something which had spent years abandoning it.

Red eyes found James. Understanding moved between them. James had time to feel the betrayal. He did not have time to grieve it.

"Lily, take Harry and go!"

She did not argue. James heard her running before he reached the hall. Voldemort raised his wand.

James overturned the narrow table between them. Wood struck the floor, scattering photographs and unopened post across the tiles. Voldemort's first curse shattered the wall where James's head had been.

James lunged.

He possessed no wand, no plan and no genuine belief that his body could stop what had entered the house. None of that mattered. Every second Voldemort spent upon him was a second Lily had with Harry.

James caught the dark wizard's arm.

For one startled instant, they collided like ordinary men. James drove his shoulder forward, forcing Voldemort back against the open door. Cold rain blew into the hall. A framed photograph broke beneath their feet.

Voldemort's expression changed. Not to fear. Offence. The wand twisted between them. James struck his wrist against the doorframe. Red light scorched the ceiling.

Upstairs, Harry began to cry. The sound passed through James with greater force than any spell.

He tightened his grip.

"You won't touch him."

Voldemort looked towards the staircase. James hit him. His fist connected with the side of that pale, inhuman face. Pain split across his knuckles. Voldemort staggered half a step.

It was not enough. It had never been going to be enough. The wand came level with James's chest.

James thought of Lily laughing in the kitchen. Harry taking one step. Sirius in the photograph, returning every time James pushed him from the frame. He tried to move once more. Green light filled the hall.

The curse struck before pain could arrive. James Potter was dead.

Lily heard him fall. There was no mistaking the sound. She had known James in laughter, anger, sleep and fear. She had known his footsteps racing along Hogwarts corridors and approaching her across crowded rooms. She had heard him land badly after falling from a broom, collapse beside her after Order missions and drop into chairs with theatrical exhaustion.

She knew the weight of him. The impact below was final. Lily reached the nursery with Harry clutched against her. His crying sharpened as she pushed the door closed behind them and turned the lock.

The lock would do nothing. She dragged the small chair beneath the handle anyway.

"Shh." Her voice broke. "Harry, please."

He reached for her face. James's wand lay upon the dresser. Lily saw it and crossed the room. Her fingers were inches from the wood when the nursery door shook in its frame.

Harry screamed. Lily seized the wand and turned.

"Protego!"

The door exploded inward. The Shield Charm caught the worst of the blast. Broken timber struck the walls and ceiling. The chair shattered beneath the force, one leg spinning beneath the cot.

Voldemort stepped through the smoke.

Lily cast the first lethal spell she knew.

He deflected it without slowing. The rebounding curse struck the ceiling. Plaster fell across the rug. Harry cried harder, small hands clutching the front of Lily's blouse.

She fired again.

Voldemort moved his wand. Lily's spell vanished. The answering strike hit James's wand and tore it from her fingers. It flew across the room, struck the wall and disappeared beneath the cot.

She was unarmed. There was nowhere to run.

The window was sealed against Apparition and too narrow to climb through while holding Harry. The roof beyond was steep with rain. Even if she reached it, she would have nowhere to go. The Secret was broken. Every hidden road had opened beneath their feet.

Voldemort's gaze settled upon Harry. Lily backed towards the cot.

"No."

He advanced.

"Stand aside."

She placed Harry behind her, shielding him with her body.

"No."

His face held no anger. That frightened her more. Anger would have acknowledged that she mattered. He regarded her as one might regard furniture obstructing a doorway.

"Stand aside you silly little girl, you needn't die tonight."

Lily thought of James downstairs.

Something tore open inside her, but she could not let herself look at it. Not yet. If she allowed herself to understand that James was gone, she would lose whatever remained capable of standing.

Harry's fingers caught in her hair. Lily reached behind her and touched his cheek.

"Stand aside," Voldemort repeated.

"You can have me."

"I already do."

"Kill me. Do whatever you want. Just leave Harry."

Voldemort's eyes narrowed.

"He is the reason I came."

"He's a baby."

"He is an obstacle."

"He doesn't even know who you are."

"And he never will."

Lily pressed herself more firmly between them.

Outside, rain ran down the window in silver streams. One of Sirius's charmed stars continued drifting across the curtain, unaware that daylight had ended. Harry's wooden dragon lay upon the floor with James's old jumper still caught around its neck.

The room contained every tomorrow Lily had imagined. Birthdays. School robes. Skinned knees. Books left open and muddy shoes abandoned near doors. Harry learning to fly. Harry asking about the war. Harry growing tall enough that she would need to look upward when scolding him. Harry bringing friends home. Harry becoming someone neither she nor James could yet predict.

An entire life waited inside a child too young to understand that anyone had come to take it. Lily lifted her chin.

"No."

Voldemort raised his wand. Behind her, Harry's crying quietened into frightened breaths. Lily did not look away.

"Harry," she whispered.

Green light entered the room.

Harry woke with his mother's name caught in his throat. He came upright so quickly that the narrow bed struck the wall. Springs groaned beneath him. His hands reached into darkness, searching for a child who had been behind him a second earlier and a wand which should have been beneath his fingers.

There was neither, only the smallest bedroom at Number Four, Privet Drive.

Heat lay heavily across the room. The window had been opened by less than an inch, the greatest distance Vernon considered compatible with security, and the curtains hung without movement. Moonlight passed through the gap and divided the carpet into pale bars.

Harry couldn't breathe properly.

He pushed both hands against the mattress. The room blurred around him: wardrobe, desk, peeling wallpaper, the shadow of Hedwig's empty perch. Ordinary shapes waited for him to recognise them.

He knew where he was but his body was still caught in the moments of the dream. Somewhere within the wall, a pipe shifted. Metal scraped against metal.

The sound became scales moving through ancient tunnels.

Harry was standing before he understood that he had moved. His shoulder struck the wardrobe. One hand reached for his wand again and found nothing. Vernon had locked it inside the cupboard beneath the stairs with the rest of Harry's school things.

He stared at the closed bedroom door. No green light appeared beneath it.

No footsteps crossed the landing. Vernon snored in the room farther along the corridor, each breath rattling through the pipes. A car passed outside, its headlights moving briefly across the ceiling before disappearing.

Harry remained braced against the wardrobe. His heart struck his ribs hard enough to hurt. The dreams had been impossible to shake since that night in the chamber.

He hadn't remembered his parents' house before then. He had been one year old when they died. He could not know the kitchen, the nursery or the particular sound his father's voice made when trying not to wake him. He could not remember walking towards James because he had not known James was his name.

Photographs had supplied the faces. Stories had provided the rest. His mind had assembled them during sleep and then destroyed them because it knew how the story ended.

Harry slid down the side of the wardrobe and sat upon the floor.

Sweat chilled against the back of his neck. The room smelled of warm dust, old carpet and the lavender polish Aunt Petunia used upon the landing. Nothing remained of rain, smoke or the faint sweetness of pumpkin pie.

He closed his eyes. Immediately, he saw Lily standing in front of him. Not the woman smiling from Hagrid's photograph album. Not the young girl whose green eyes everyone insisted Harry possessed. This Lily was terrified. Her hair clung to her face. Dust marked one cheek. She knew she was going to die but she had stood there anyway.

Harry opened his eyes. His nails had dug crescent shapes into his palms.

He had survived Quirrell. He had survived Tom Riddle, the basilisk and the Chamber beneath Hogwarts. He had stabbed a sword through the roof of a monster's mouth and remained conscious long enough to destroy the thing which had nearly taken Draconia from him.

A noise in a wall should not have been able to do this. The thought brought shame. Shame brought anger, although Harry could not decide whether he was angry with himself, the Dursleys, the Chamber or the dead woman whose voice his sleeping mind would not release.

He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until colour burst behind them.

"Stop."

The word sounded too loud. Vernon's snoring paused.

Harry froze.

Bedsprings creaked in the room across the landing. Vernon coughed, muttered something incomprehensible and rolled over. His snoring resumed.

Harry waited another minute before moving.

Beneath the mattress was a bundle of letters tied with thread pulled from an old school robe. He drew it out carefully and carried it towards the window, where the moonlight was strongest.

Hermione's handwriting covered the first envelope with disciplined neatness. His name and address sat perfectly centred, each line level enough that Harry suspected she had used a ruler.

Draconia's writing angled sharply across the second. She had addressed it simply to Potter, apparently trusting the postal service to recognise that no other Potter could possibly be relevant.

Harry loosened the thread.

For several weeks, the letters had been enough.

Whenever Privet Drive closed around him, he could unfold proof that somewhere beyond it existed two people who expected him to return. Hermione wrote about books, her parents' surgery and the increasingly serious moral failings of her local library. Draconia wrote about her mother, political dinners and Dobby's continued absence from Malfoy Manor. Both asked whether Harry was eating. Both attempted to disguise the question differently.

He selected Draconia's latest letter.

Moonlight caught the dark ink.

Potter,

That Organising charm you found is brilliant.

Mother insists that I ask whether you are eating enough. I informed her that your starvation would improve neither your height nor your conversational abilities, so she has decided I am insufficiently sympathetic and made me begin this letter again.

A second beginning had been crossed out so aggressively that the parchment was nearly torn.

Are you sleeping any better?

Beneath it, Draconia had written:

I dreamt about the Chamber again. If you say anything sentimental about shared trauma, I will hex you when we return to school. And trust me—it won't be like the hospital wing.

The corner of Harry's mouth lifted. It was not much of a smile. No one else would have noticed it in the dark. He heard her voice anyway. Precise, disdainful and careful around every word which might reveal more than she intended.

Harry folded the letter, then changed his mind and opened it again. His breathing began to slow.

The bedroom did not become pleasant. The Dursleys remained asleep beyond the door. His wand remained locked away, Hedwig remained trapped within her cage downstairs and morning would still bring chores, insults and whatever quantity of breakfast Aunt Petunia considered adequate for someone she preferred to forget existed.

None of that changed, but the nightmare withdrew by a small degree. Harry leaned his head against the wall and read the letter from the beginning.

He had escaped the Chamber months ago.

Some nights, it escaped with him. Tonight, at least, he did not have to sit with it entirely alone.
