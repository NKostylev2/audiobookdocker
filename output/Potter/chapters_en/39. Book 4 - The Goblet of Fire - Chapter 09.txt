9

By morning, someone had rewritten the banner.

Harry saw it as he came down from the dormitory, one hand sliding along the banister. Fred and George had folded the original away before midnight, but somebody had since retrieved it, turned the congratulatory side towards the wall and hung it above the fireplace again. The fabric sagged in the centre where the charm had been applied badly, pulling the letters out of line.

The gold letters no longer congratulated him. They now read:

GRYFFINDOR BELIEVES HARRY POTTER

The words occupied the wall above the fireplace with the solemnity of an official declaration. Beneath them, smaller writing had been added in several hands.

MOST OF US.

ASK HIM HOW HE DID IT.

CEDRIC IS HOGWARTS CHAMPION.

Someone else had drawn a dragon eating all three statements. Harry stopped on the final stair. The common room held fewer students than usual, and those who remained discovered a sudden interest in books, shoelaces and the early weather when they noticed him. A page turned without anyone looking at it. Grey dawn pressed against the windows. The fire had burned down to red seams beneath ash, and abandoned cups from the ruined celebration stood upon every table. Without the banners and sparks, the remaining food looked excessive rather than festive.

Hermione waited beside the portrait hole with two pieces of toast wrapped in a napkin and the expression of someone who had already argued with several people before breakfast. Her hair had been tied back without success, loose strands escaping around her face, and ink marked the side of one hand. She looked nearly as tired as Harry felt and considerably more prepared to make that everybody else's problem.

"Who changed it?" Harry asked.

"Fred and George removed your name. Dean wrote the first statement. Seamus added the second."

"And the Cedric one?"

"A Gryffindor first-year added it after a second-year Hufflepuff shouted the words through the portrait hole at half past six."

Harry looked at the dragon.

"Neville," Hermione said.

"Good dragon."

She handed him the toast.

"Eat."

Harry accepted it, though the thought of eating made his stomach tighten. He had slept in intervals measured by the mirror brightening beside his pillow: Draconia shortly after midnight, Sirius at one, Hermione at two to announce she had located three more books concerning coerced magical contracts. At three forty, he had woken convinced that blue fire encircled his wrist and rubbed the unmarked skin until it hurt.

Each time he surfaced, the canopy appeared wrong. Blue light from the Goblet seemed to move behind the red curtains. Once he woke with his wand in hand and no memory of drawing it. Another time Sirius's voice was still coming through the mirror, reading aloud from a Ministry regulation in the hope that boredom might succeed where exhaustion had failed.

By five, he stopped closing his eyes.

"McGonagall came up twenty minutes ago," Hermione said. "Dumbledore wants us in his office before breakfast."

"Us?"

"You, me and Draconia."

"Why you?"

"Because I asked."

"Of course."

They left Gryffindor Tower, and the castle watched them go. Portraits turned as Harry passed; ghosts slowed inside walls, their pale faces briefly emerging to look. Students who had never spoken to him whispered his name and stopped when he glanced towards them. Overnight, the fourth parchment had transformed him from a person into a question everyone believed they were entitled to answer.

Harry knew this kind of attention. He had lived inside versions of it since learning his own name mattered. Usually it arrived after danger, when people could admire survival without considering what it cost. This time the danger lay ahead, and the school was already deciding whether he had invited it.

Draconia waited outside Dumbledore's gargoyle in perfect robes and polished shoes, with shadows beneath both eyes. Harry recognised the effort behind that perfection: her hair had been brushed until every strand obeyed, and her tie sat precisely beneath her collar. Only the folded Daily Prophet shook when she held it out to him.

"Already?" Harry asked.

"Special edition."

The headline read:

POTTER ENTERS TOURNAMENT — FOURTH CHAMPION SHOCKS EUROPE

Below it, Rita Skeeter promised an exclusive examination of Harry's motives after the wand ceremony.

"She wrote it before asking me."

"Questions might complicate the result," Draconia said.

Hermione took the paper. "They call you Hogwarts's second champion."

"I don't belong to a school according to the contract."

"That is not as flattering."

The gargoyle stepped aside, allowing them onto the moving staircase. Harry climbed beside the girls while Hermione folded the paper shut, hiding the headline without making it any easier to forget.

Dumbledore's office contained too many adults.

Rain tapped against the round windows. Silver instruments turned upon spindle-legged tables, clicking and releasing curls of smoke which vanished before reaching the ceiling. The familiar clutter had been pressed to the edges so chairs could form an uneven circle around Dumbledore's desk. It looked less like a headmaster's office than a hastily assembled court.

Sirius stood first, crossing the circular room before Harry properly registered anyone else. His coat was still wet from travel, and the dark hair tied away from his face had escaped during whatever argument preceded their arrival. Harry had time to say his name before Sirius pulled him into both arms.

There was nothing careful about the embrace.

It closed around the entire night: the Goblet, the whispers and the moment Harry's name had ceased to belong solely to him. Sirius held tightly enough that Harry did not have to decide whether to return the embrace before his arms had already done it.

Harry pressed his face into the damp shoulder and breathed leather, rain and the smoke of home.

"You're here."

"I said I would be."

Sirius touched the back of Harry's head. His hands checked neck, shoulders and the wrist where the contract line appeared.

"Did it hurt again?"

"No."

"Truth."

"It felt warm once. I might have dreamt it."

Sirius examined him long enough to make dishonesty impossible before stepping aside for Remus. His hug was gentler and no less firm, one hand settling between Harry's shoulders.

"You look terrible," Remus murmured near his ear.

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Then the evidence is unusually cooperative."

Lucius and Narcissa stood near the windows. Draconia stopped when she saw them, her careful composure faltering for a moment before Narcissa opened her arms. She went into them without hesitation, her shoulders loosening at the first touch. Lucius looked towards Harry. His face remained pale from the World Cup wound, but his posture carried enough anger to support the entire room.

"Mr Potter."

"Morning."

"It is not."

"Right."

Professor McGonagall stood beside Dumbledore's desk. Snape occupied the shadow near a cabinet of silver instruments. Moody had taken the chair nearest the door, his magical eye observing the room's occupants in turn. It passed over Sirius's wand, Lucius's injured side and the transparent case upon Dumbledore's desk. When it reached Harry, it paused upon his wrist. Barty Crouch was absent.

"Where is Crouch?" Hermione asked.

Dumbledore indicated three chairs.

"The Ministry has declined to send a representative until its contract specialists complete their review."

"Which will occur?" Lucius asked.

"Within ten working days."

Sirius laughed once, without amusement. Beside Harry, the hand he had rested against the chair tightened.

"The first task is in twenty-three."

"I have informed the Minister that the delay is unacceptable."

"Did he form a panel?" Harry asked.

McGonagall's mouth twitched despite the room. Lucius placed one hand upon Dumbledore's desk.

"My solicitor has identified six legal challenges to the contract. The first concerns capacity: Harry is a minor under both wizarding and ordinary law. The second concerns fraudulent submission. The third—"

"Archaic magical contracts often recognise demonstrated power rather than civil majority," Snape said.

Lucius turned. "Then the second challenge should suffice. He demonstrated no intent to enter."

"I am describing the magic, Lucius. Not defending it."

Something old and difficult passed between them. Lucius held Snape's gaze for a moment, then inclined his head, accepting the distinction without surrendering his anger. Hermione sat straighter. Harry looked at her. She appeared momentarily surprised to agree with Lucius before deciding the cause justified the discomfort. Dumbledore raised the transparent box containing Harry's parchment. The nameless fourth-school border remained visible beneath the black ink, its faint light undiminished by the night. Harry kept his hands beneath the desk.

"I have spent the night examining the anchor. Severing it directly would risk Harry's ability to channel magic through his wand. Transferring it caused the contract to draw upon him more forcefully. Destroying the parchment produced the same result."

Harry stared.

"You destroyed it?"

"A duplicate."

"You could have mentioned that before starting."

Dumbledore's eyes lowered.

"Yes."

The apology did not make Harry less angry, which surprised him. He had spent years treating Dumbledore's regret as a kind of resolution: an adult admitted a decision had hurt him, and therefore the hurt became necessary, complicated or finished. Yet the blue line remained beneath his skin whether anyone regretted it or not. He could feel his pulse where it had closed around his wrist.

"So yesterday's answer hasn't changed."

"Not yet," Dumbledore said.

"I prepare because nobody knows what refusing will take from me."

"You prepare while we continue searching. If a safe method of refusal exists, the decision will be yours."

Harry felt anger rise with enough force to clear some of his exhaustion.

"The decision should have been mine before my name came out."

Sirius remained beside him. He did not interrupt or soften the accusation. Dumbledore's face looked older than it had in the champions' chamber.

"Yes," he said.

No justification followed. Harry had wanted one badly enough to fight. Its absence left nowhere for the anger to go. Harry turned towards Fawkes instead. On his golden perch, the phoenix watched with red feathers appearing almost black in the morning light, then offered one soft sound. It asked Harry for nothing, and explained nothing away. He accepted that comfort more easily.

"Until the contract is broken," Remus said, "we must prepare for the possibility that it cannot be."

Draconia left her mother and joined the table.

"Who entered him?"

Dumbledore's expression changed.

"The Goblet was placed under supervision from the moment it entered the Entrance Hall."

Hermione opened her notebook.

"By whom?"

"Professors Flitwick and Vector established the monitoring charms. Members of staff rotated through the Hall. The Goblet itself recorded forty-seven submitted parchments."

"Forty-eight names emerged in the audit," Snape said.

Harry looked at him. "Mine?"

"Your parchment did not register as an entry."

"How?"

Moody answered.

"Whoever Confunded it convinced the Goblet the fourth category existed before monitoring began. Your name wasn't added as part of the Tournament. It was hidden inside the terms."

Hermione's quill stopped.

"Before it was placed in the Entrance Hall."

Dumbledore nodded.

"The Goblet was stored in a sealed chamber beneath the Ministry until three days before the delegations arrived. Crouch transported it personally."

Harry thought of Winky in the World Cup woods, insisting that young master had taken Crouch's wand. He turned towards Sirius. His godfather's jaw had tightened. Across the table, Lucius glanced at Narcissa before both looked towards Moody.

"Winky is at the cottage," Harry said. "Did you ask her?"

"Before we left," Remus replied. "She confirmed Mr Crouch collected the Goblet. When Sirius asked whether anyone accompanied him, she became too distressed to answer."

"No family binding stops her now," Hermione said.

"No," Sirius answered. "Fear does not require one."

"Where is Crouch?" Sirius asked again.

"At the Ministry," Dumbledore said.

Moody's magical eye turned towards the office wall, as though stone could be made transparent through determination alone. Harry watched it instead of the box upon the desk.

"Not for long," Sirius said.

Narcissa approached Harry and touched two fingers beneath his chin, lifting his face towards the light. Her inspection used no magic and missed nothing; Harry had the uncomfortable impression that she could count every hour he had failed to sleep.

"You have not slept."

"There was a party."

"Did you attend it?"

"Briefly."

"Did you eat?"

"A sandwich."

Lucius's eyes narrowed.

Harry looked towards Draconia. "This is your fault."

"A tragic expansion of accountability."

Narcissa released his chin.

"You will eat breakfast."

"We're discussing attempted murder."

"Hungry people remain murderable."

Sirius made an approving sound. Harry surrendered.

The meeting continued around plans which never became solutions. Dumbledore would search for a method of suspension. Lucius would challenge the contract through the Wizengamot. Sirius and Remus would investigate Crouch's movements and every person who accessed the Ministry chamber. Snape would examine the ink. Moody volunteered to review castle security.

Harry would attend lessons, remain reachable and prepare. Every adult assigned themselves a task, with parchment passed across the desk and appointments settled in increasingly precise voices.

The only task Harry had not chosen was the one capable of killing him.

When the meeting ended, Lucius handed him an envelope.

"What is it?"

"A means of contacting my solicitor directly."

"I have a solicitor?"

"You have mine until Black acquires one capable of surviving a conversation."

Sirius looked offended. "Mine fled."

"Precisely."

Lucius lowered his voice.

"There is also a list of names Nott trusted during the first war. Most will be useless. Some may not."

Harry took the envelope.

"Why give it to me?"

"Because adults continue losing information shortly before you require it."

Lucius looked towards Dumbledore as he spoke. The Headmaster heard and did not object, leaving Harry to tuck the envelope safely inside his robes.

Breakfast was worse than the party.

Every table watched Harry enter between Sirius and Hermione.

Morning light had not yet reached the enchanted ceiling. Hundreds of candles floated over a Great Hall caught between night and day, illuminating open newspapers at every table. Harry's own face appeared upon several front pages. In the moving photograph, he stood outside the champions' chamber while Hermione and Draconia closed around him from either side.

The printed Harry looked trapped. His photographed gaze moved between the girls and the edge of the frame, searching for a way out the paper could not offer. The presence of an adult prevented direct questions and encouraged more creative staring. Gryffindors made room without agreeing who should sit near him. Hufflepuff conversation lowered. Ravenclaws examined Sirius with almost as much interest as Harry. Slytherin did not pretend. Warrington applauded slowly.

"Potter found a guardian willing to congratulate him."

Draconia stood before she had properly sat down, reaching the limits of Snape's instruction in a single movement. Her bench scraped sharply against the floor. Daphne caught the edge of her sleeve. Draconia removed the hand without looking, eyes fixed upon Warrington. At the Gryffindor table, Hermione set her bag down with equal care. Harry realised both girls were measuring the same distance and calculating different forms of damage. Sirius touched Harry's shoulder.

"Stay."

He crossed the aisle himself. Warrington's confidence failed as Sirius Black approached the Slytherin table, his slow applause ending before Sirius leaned down to speak.

"I spent twelve years in Azkaban for a crime I did not commit because people preferred an exciting story to asking inconvenient questions."

The Hall listened.

"You are welcome to repeat their mistake. But if you use it to trouble my godson, learn from Peter Pettigrew and become difficult to find."

Warrington went pale. At the staff table, Professor McGonagall closed her eyes while Snape lifted his cup, concealing whatever expression he considered inappropriate to the occasion. Draconia sat, and Sirius returned to Harry as though he had merely gone to fetch the butter.

"Subtle," Harry said.

"I learned from you."

"Tragic."

They ate breakfast. The Great Hall felt no less hostile, though direct cruelty became more difficult with Sirius beside him, one arm along the back of Harry's bench as he looked at anyone who stared too long. Remus spoke quietly with Hermione about the newspaper's timeline. Narcissa sent plates across the aisle with the efficient magic of someone establishing supply lines, while Lucius remained at Slytherin and made Warrington's entire year discover an urgent interest in their porridge.

For several minutes Harry existed inside a fortress constructed from worried adults and food he had not requested.

Sirius stayed until Harry had finished the toast Narcissa placed upon his plate and most of the eggs Hermione added when Narcissa looked away. Questions collected beyond their uneasy barricade. Harry could feel them in every glance directed over a goblet, every conversation which fell apart when he turned his head.

He wondered how long it took a school to decide what it believed. Less than one night, apparently. Changing its mind seemed likely to take longer, and Harry was too tired to imagine how he might begin.

Remus checked his watch. "We need to leave if we mean to reach the Ministry before Crouch discovers an urgent engagement elsewhere."

"He's already discovered one," Sirius said. "That's why he isn't here."

"Then we ought to arrive before it becomes an illness."

Sirius did not move. Harry concentrated on cutting a mushroom he did not intend to eat, pushing the pieces apart with the edge of his knife.

The idea of the doors closing behind Sirius tightened something low in Harry's chest. He had spent the night wanting his godfather to arrive. Now Sirius had, the school had not become safer, and leaving was required almost immediately because the adults who loved Harry needed to chase the danger elsewhere.

"I'll be all right," he said.

"That phrase should be banned from your vocabulary."

"You'd have to find something else to say as well."

Remus looked down at his tea. Sirius's mouth tightened. Then he reached inside his coat and withdrew a small square mirror. The frame was newer than Harry's, its silver still bright at the corners.

"Keep yours on you."

"I do."

"Not in your trunk. Not beneath six textbooks. On you."

"It was four textbooks."

"Constantly, Harry."

The humour left Sirius's voice upon the final word. Harry nodded.

"Constantly."

Sirius squeezed the back of his neck. It was not quite an embrace, but it contained the promise of one and the reluctance of a man who had only recently been given the right to worry in person. Harry leaned into the hand for half a second before remembering the Hall. Sirius felt it anyway. His thumb moved once beneath Harry's hair.

"I'll call tonight."

"You'll be investigating."

"I possess the astonishing ability to do two things in a day."

"Since when?"

"Cruel. Entirely deserved, but cruel."

Remus rose. "I'll make certain he remembers."

They left together, Sirius looking back twice before the doors closed behind him. Lucius and Narcissa followed soon afterwards. Lucius paused beside Draconia long enough to murmur something against her hair. She answered with a small, irritated nod which meant she had been given an instruction she considered unnecessary and intended to obey precisely. Narcissa came to Harry last.

"The Manor remains open to you," she said. "Whatever the papers decide to print."

Harry looked at her. People were listening, their breakfasts momentarily forgotten. Narcissa knew it, and did not lower her voice.

"Thank you."

Her fingers briefly enclosed his shoulder. Then the Malfoys crossed the Hall, pale heads held high beneath several hundred interested eyes. Draconia watched them go.

"What did your father say?" Harry asked.

"That I am not to duel anyone before luncheon."

Hermione glanced at the enchanted ceiling. "How generous."

"I thought so."

"And the part you agreed to?"

Draconia collected her bag. "Mother told me to remain with you."

Harry attempted a smile. "Were you planning to leave?"

"No."

She said it without emphasis, her hand already resting upon the back of Harry's chair. The certainty struck harder for its simplicity. He picked up his bag, and they rose for lessons together.

The first badge appeared outside Transfiguration.

It occupied the front of Ernie Macmillan's robes in yellow and black and read SUPPORT CEDRIC DIGGORY — HOGWARTS'S TRUE CHAMPION. Several Hufflepuffs wore them. Most looked uncomfortable when Harry noticed. One boy pressed the badge with his thumb and caused the words to change.

POTTER STINKS.

Hermione stopped. Draconia kept moving, crossing the corridor with such purpose that the students between her and the badges stepped aside without being asked. Harry caught the strap of her bag just before she reached them.

"Your father said luncheon."

"It is eleven fifty-eight."

"The clocks might be slow."

"Release me."

"Cedric's coming."

Cedric descended the marble staircase two steps at a time. He still wore his Hufflepuff scarf from breakfast, though the end had slipped loose over one shoulder. A conversation died around him as he reached the landing. Cedric looked at the badges, his gaze moving over the words before finding Harry. His expression lost every trace of the conversation he had been having on the stairs.

"Take them off."

Ernie's hand rose defensively to his chest. "Cedric, they're supporting you."

"The first half is."

"He took your place."

"No, he didn't."

"There's meant to be one Hogwarts champion."

"And I am that champion." Cedric's voice remained even. "Harry never said otherwise."

The younger boy with the changing badge looked at the floor.

Ernie reddened. "You don't know he didn't enter."

"I asked him."

"And you believed him?"

"Yes."

The answer carried down the corridor. Harry had spent the morning being defended by people who loved him, people whose loyalty could be dismissed as blindness or obligation. Cedric had every reason to resent what had happened. His belief left fewer excuses behind. One by one, the Hufflepuffs removed their badges, slipping them into pockets or closing their hands around the pins. Ernie did not. Cedric looked disappointed rather than angry, and that appeared to affect him more. Professor McGonagall opened the classroom door.

"If the corridor has become the site of a political demonstration, Mr Macmillan, I suggest you conduct it elsewhere. If not, you are delaying my lesson."

Ernie removed the badge at last. Draconia looked faintly cheated as Harry released her bag, allowing the strap to settle against her shoulder. Cedric stepped aside to let the younger students enter, then caught Harry just before he crossed the threshold.

"Sorry," he said quietly.

"You didn't make them."

"They're doing it for me."

"People keep doing things for me too. Apparently, I'm responsible for all of those."

Cedric gave him a tired smile.

"If they start again, tell me."

"I can handle a badge."

"That isn't what I said."

Harry recognised the difference. He nodded.

"Mr Potter," Professor McGonagall called from inside.

Harry entered. Cedric continued down the corridor towards his own lesson.

The badge disappeared from public view for three days.

The opinion behind it remained, requiring no pin or coloured lettering to make itself known.

It followed Harry into lessons and waited outside bathrooms. It took the form of laughter from behind suits of armour, unsigned notes among his books and conversations conducted just loudly enough for him to hear. Some students believed he had cheated because cheating made more sense than someone penetrating protections established by Dumbledore. Others believed danger followed Harry so reliably that entering himself seemed inevitable.

The Gryffindors defended him with enthusiasm and little discipline. Fred and George charmed one of the Hufflepuff badges to shout ASK A BETTER QUESTION whenever pressed. Angelina hexed a seventh-year Ravenclaw's shoelaces together. Lee Jordan began narrating Harry's entrances into rooms until McGonagall threatened to remove his voice for a week.

It should have helped, and sometimes it did. At other times, Harry found himself wishing he could enter a room without anyone announcing which side they stood upon. Support felt too much like another form of watching.

By Wednesday, Harry had discovered an abandoned Charms classroom behind a tapestry of Gregory the Grasping. Dust lay thick upon the teacher's desk, undisturbed except where he rested his bag. The windows overlooked the empty Quidditch pitch, and a broken cabinet clicked whenever the wind changed. Nobody had reason to enter it.

Harry ate half a roll there at lunch and left the rest untouched beside him. The silence helped until footsteps paused outside. His wand was in his hand before the door opened. Hermione entered first, carrying enough books to justify the journey only if the library had caught fire. Draconia followed with three apples and no explanation.

Harry lowered his wand. "How do you keep finding me?"

"You choose rooms with a view of the pitch," Hermione said.

Draconia placed one apple upon his book. "And you are less unpredictable than you imagine."

"I'm becoming mysterious."

"You brought the same bag."

Neither asked him to return to the Hall. Hermione claimed the nearest desk and began revising Arithmancy, while Draconia opened a Potions text with her apple untouched beside her hand. Pages turned; the cabinet clicked in the draught. Their silence asked Harry for neither conversation nor gratitude, and made no demand that he recover quickly enough to reassure them.

After several minutes, he picked up the apple.

Harry learned which staircases were empty before breakfast. He took the long route to Herbology and ate lunch at the edge of the grounds when the weather permitted. His answers in class grew shorter. Humour remained available when somebody else needed it, but he seemed to misplace it whenever the quiet belonged to him.

Hermione named the pattern on the second day. By the time Harry recognised it himself, Draconia had already begun appearing at the end of his lessons.

Neither confronted him. Hermione simply changed where she studied, establishing herself in whatever abandoned classroom Harry selected and behaving as though seventeen pounds of books had happened to follow him there. Draconia never said she had crossed half the castle to meet him after lessons.

Harry never thanked either of them. The gratitude was too large and too close to fear, tangled with the memory of a summer in which letters had stopped and silence had quickly become evidence that he had imagined being wanted. Now, whenever he left a room alone, one of them eventually found him. He began to understand that this was deliberate, and that neither required him to ask before choosing to stay.

On the eighteenth of November, four narrow tables replaced the desks in an unused classroom beside the Great Hall.

That morning's wand-weighing ceremony had already been held up twice while the Ministry debated whether examining Harry's wand might strengthen the contract. Lucius's solicitor insisted upon an independent witness. Dumbledore insisted upon three. Rita Skeeter insisted the public could not be expected to endure such secrecy.

She arrived in robes the colour of poisonous lime, her blonde curls arranged into a shape which appeared structurally ambitious. Jewelled spectacles perched upon her nose, and a crocodile-skin handbag swung from one arm. Beside her, the Quick-Quotes Quill scratched across a pad before anyone spoke, evidently finding facts an unnecessary delay.

Harry leaned towards Hermione.

"What's it writing?"

Hermione glanced down.

"'Harry Potter cowers behind two devoted companions.'"

Draconia, on Harry's other side, looked at the empty space behind them. "It lacks a reliable understanding of direction."

The quill added something.

"Now it says your voice was cold with aristocratic menace," Hermione reported.

"Better."

Rita swept towards them.

"Harry, darling."

"We've never met."

"And already so modest."

"That wasn't modesty."

The quill raced. Rita's eyes moved to Hermione and Draconia, and recognition sharpened her smile as though she had discovered a more promising angle.

"Miss Granger. Miss Malfoy. How fascinating."

"For you?" Hermione asked.

"For everyone. The Boy Who Lived, accompanied by a Muggle-born prodigy and the sole heir of one of Britain's oldest families. Such unusual loyalties always interest my readers."

"Your readers should consider a hobby," Draconia said.

Rita laughed as though she had been given permission, her quill hovering expectantly beside Harry's shoulder.

"Could I borrow Harry for a moment?"

"No," Hermione said.

"I did ask Harry."

Harry considered the open classroom, the officials arranging documents and the cupboard Rita had already indicated with one lacquered fingernail.

"Then you heard her answer."

Rita's smile became more genuine. Resistance appeared to interest her far more than cooperation would have done.

"Oh, this will be fun."

She led all three of them into the cupboard.

There was barely space for two buckets, a mop and Rita's perfume. Harry remained beside the door, close enough to feel the cool brass handle against his sleeve. Hermione stood by a shelf with her notebook open, while Draconia took possession of the only chair and looked as though it had been brought for her.

Rita's quill hovered.

"Harry, how did it feel to hear your name called?"

"Confusing."

"Terrifying?"

"Mostly confusing."

"Exciting?"

"Still confusing."

"You must have wondered whether this was your opportunity to prove yourself beside older and more experienced students."

"No."

"To step beyond your famous past?"

"I was trying to finish a treacle tart."

The quill wrote for several seconds. Hermione leaned over.

"It says you were 'haunted by a hunger no feast could satisfy.'"

Harry faced Rita. "It was a very good tart."

Draconia covered her mouth.

Rita tapped the quill. "A little colour is necessary."

"Then use green ink," Hermione said. "Lying is not colour."

Rita ignored her.

"The Tournament presents considerable danger. You have, of course, faced death before. Do thoughts of your parents return at moments like these?"

The cupboard seemed to lose what little air it contained. Harry's attention returned to the quill, its point trembling above the parchment as though the question had brought it nearer to the story it wanted. Eager for grief.

"Sometimes."

"Do you imagine they would be proud?"

"I imagine they'd want to know who entered me."

"But you must feel their absence especially keenly now."

"I have a guardian."

"Sirius Black." Rita tilted her head. "A man whose instability has been extensively documented."

Draconia rose.

"By you."

The cupboard no longer appeared large enough to contain her anger.

"Among others," Rita said lightly.

"Those reports helped keep an innocent man hunted after his escape. My father's solicitors retained copies."

"Is that a threat, Miss Malfoy?"

"No. Threats require uncertainty."

The Quick-Quotes Quill paused, leaving a dark speck of ink upon the parchment. Harry glanced down to see whether it had managed to keep up.

"Did it write that properly?"

Hermione checked. "For once."

Rita snapped her handbag open and directed the quill inside.

"Young people often mistake devotion for judgement. Tell me, Harry, is it comforting to have two girls arranging your life?"

Harry felt Hermione become very still beside him. Draconia's expression emptied, the anger disappearing behind a composure he knew was more dangerous. There it was: the shape Rita wanted. Not friendship, because three people caring for one another would not sell enough papers. A rivalry would. A scandal might.

Until Rita named it, Harry had not considered how they looked from outside. Hermione stood close enough for her sleeve to brush his whenever she wrote. Draconia had claimed the only chair as though guarding the space between Harry and the reporter. Both had followed him into a cupboard without waiting to be invited.

The idea of choosing between them felt absurd. The idea that Rita's version was entirely absurd should have felt easier. Harry refused to examine either thought while a quill waited to turn it into a headline. Harry opened the cupboard door, letting the classroom's cooler air reach them.

"Extremely," he said. "They're arranging for me to leave."

He stepped out before Rita could offer the quill another version of his answer. Hermione followed with her shoulders rigid. Draconia came last and let the cupboard door strike the wall hard enough to make Rita flinch.

"That woman," Hermione said beneath her breath, "should not be permitted to own punctuation."

"I liked the part where my concern for Harry became aristocratic possession," Draconia said. "It was almost inventive."

Harry glanced between them.

"You both know I'm comforted by your arrangements."

Hermione's anger broke around a reluctant smile. Draconia adjusted one cuff, making a careful business of straightening fabric which was already perfectly smooth. "Good. Your schedule is exhausting."

At the front of the room, Garrick Ollivander called the champions forward.

The wandmaker appeared unchanged from the first time Harry entered his shop: pale eyes, silver hair and fingers so long they seemed designed for instruments more delicate than bone. He stood behind four velvet cushions while the judges and witnesses formed a half-circle around him.

Dumbledore occupied one end. Madame Maxime towered at the other. Karkaroff smiled without warmth beside a Ministry witch Harry had never met. Ludo Bagman wore wasp-striped robes and the distracted expression of a man hearing several races at once. Lucius's solicitor, Octavius Brindle, sat behind Harry with three rolls of parchment and no visible capacity for joy. Rita emerged from the cupboard as though no one had left her inside it. Ollivander began with Fleur.

Her wand produced a spray of pale gold flowers which drifted to the floor without wilting. Krum's conjured a flock of black birds that circled once beneath the ceiling before vanishing into smoke. Cedric's sent silver rings expanding through the air, each one chiming when it passed through another.

Then Harry stepped forward, and the room quietened in a different way. Rita leaned towards her pad. Ollivander accepted his wand with both hands, giving the familiar piece of holly an importance Harry found unsettling outside his own grip.

"Eleven inches. Holly. Phoenix feather." His moon-bright eyes rose to Harry's scar. "I remember every wand I have sold, Mr Potter. Yours has given me particular reason not to forget."

Harry could think of several replies and trusted none of them near Rita's quill. Ollivander turned the wand beneath the light. He examined the grain, bent it carefully and measured it from pommel to tip. His fingers paused halfway along the wood.

"Has it been exposed to binding magic?"

Brindle's quill stopped, its point held just clear of the parchment. The solicitor watched Ollivander's hands.

"The contract," Harry said.

"Perhaps."

Ollivander raised the wand.

"Orchideous."

Flowers burst from the tip. For a moment, they appeared ordinary: white petals, green stems, drops of water clinging to their leaves. Then a thin blue flame travelled across them. It did not consume the flowers. It traced every vein in every leaf before collecting around Harry's wrist. The line beneath his skin answered. Pain tightened through his hand, and Harry drew breath between his teeth, fighting the instinct to seize his wand back. Sirius was not there, but Hermione and Draconia were. Both moved towards him at once.

Brindle blocked them with one extended arm while Dumbledore extinguished the fire. Ollivander released Harry's wand at once. Harry caught it before it reached the table. The pain vanished as his fingers closed around the holly, leaving a tremor which he could not immediately stop.

"Mr Potter?" Madame Maxime asked.

"Fine."

Draconia made an angry sound.

"Capable of standing," Harry amended.

Ollivander leaned closer without touching him.

"The wand is uninjured. The connection remains clear. Whatever has attached itself to your magic has not entered the core."

"Can it?" Hermione asked.

"A wand is not a passive object, Miss Granger. It may resist. It may also interpret the binding as part of its wielder, given time."

"How much time?"

"I cannot say."

Another answer without certainty. Harry curled his fingers around the holly. It felt the same. Warm from his hand, familiar in weight, a little rough where his thumb had worn the polish across four years of use. The sameness reassured him more than Ollivander's examination.

"May I?" the wandmaker asked.

Harry returned it. Ollivander cast again. This time the wand produced a fountain of clear water, then a ribbon of red sparks. No blue fire appeared.

"In excellent condition," he announced.

The witnesses signed, their quills moving one after another down the waiting sheets. Rita's scratched furiously, with no pause to ask Harry how he felt. The photograph took twenty minutes. Fleur stood with her chin raised. Krum endured the camera. Cedric attempted to make room for Harry when the photographer repeatedly positioned him at the outer edge. Bagman insisted upon several arrangements: champions with judges, foreign champions together, Hogwarts champions together, Harry alone. In the final picture, a flash exploded just as the blue line beneath Harry's skin warmed again.

His expression in the following morning's paper looked appropriately haunted.

The headline was worse.

HARRY POTTER: THE BOY WHO NEEDS TO BE CHOSEN

Rita described him as slight, lonely and driven by an "aching need to equal the parents he scarcely remembers." Sirius became an "infamously volatile guardian." Hermione was an "earnest Muggle-born admirer," while Draconia was the "beautiful pure-blood heiress whose possessive vigilance hinted at passions beyond friendship."

Rita omitted the contract, the fourth school and Cedric's belief that Harry had not entered. All three would have complicated the loneliness she had assigned him.

She included the treacle tart.

"Haunted hunger," Harry read aloud at breakfast. "At least she got that right."

Hermione seized the paper.

"She made me sound like I follow you carrying books and sighing."

Harry looked at the twelve books beside her plate.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"Your face lacks discretion."

"She called you a prodigy."

Hermione paused.

"That does not improve the article."

It appeared to improve something, though Harry judged it wisest to look away before she noticed him noticing. Draconia read the paragraph concerning herself twice. A faint patch of colour appeared high upon her cheeks.

"Possessive vigilance," she said.

"It could be worse," Harry offered.

"How?"

"Aristocratic menace."

"That would at least be accurate."

Harry took the paper from her before she could fold the paragraph out of existence.

"She got beautiful right."

The words escaped before he decided to say them. Hermione's cup stopped halfway to her mouth, and Draconia stared at him, the colour beneath her cheekbones deepening. When she answered, however, her voice remained perfectly controlled.

"A competent recovery, Potter."

Harry lowered his eyes to the article and discovered that the photograph required his complete attention. Draconia folded the paper with care, though the edges did not quite meet.

Around them, students read copies of their own. Glances acquired a new quality. The Tournament had made Harry a liar. Rita made him ridiculous, which proved easier for the school to enjoy.

Worse, she had made Hermione and Draconia available for inspection. Students who once stared only at Harry now watched the girls to see whether either reacted when the other sat closer, touched his arm or spoke first. Every ordinary gesture acquired an audience and therefore a meaning none of them had chosen.

By lunch, Lavender Brown wanted to know whether Draconia had really threatened a journalist for him. A Slytherin third-year asked whether Hermione intended to challenge her. Fred offered to take bets. George offered to hex Fred if the odds were poor. Harry laughed at the correct moments, answering enough questions to keep anyone from asking why he had gone quiet. Then he left before dessert, while the others were still looking towards the arriving dishes.

He climbed until the voices faded and found a window ledge in an unused passage overlooking the grounds. November had taken most of the colour from the hills. Bare branches scraped at a white sky. Far below, Hagrid crossed towards the Forbidden Forest with Fang at his heels and a coil of rope over one shoulder.

Harry drew Sirius's mirror from his pocket, turning the familiar frame between his fingers.

He did not call.

Sirius had spent the previous four days moving between the Ministry, Hawthorn Cottage and people who remembered Barty Crouch's household. Remus said he had slept once. Harry did not want to become another demand. The instinct felt sensible because it was old. At Privet Drive, needing anything had always worsened the cost of asking. Hunger became ingratitude. Loneliness became attention-seeking. Harry had learned to make himself smaller around exhausted adults and call the result independence. Sirius had never asked him to do that. Still, Harry rested his thumb against the mirror's corner without tapping it, watching the reflection of the pale sky.

The glass brightened anyway, and Sirius's face appeared.

"Why are you sitting on a windowsill?"

Harry nearly dropped him.

"Why are you calling me to ask?"

"Because you look miserable and apparently believe altitude makes it private."

Harry angled the mirror away from the view.

"The article came out."

"I know. I sent a formal complaint."

"To the Prophet?"

"To Rita's hairdresser."

Harry smiled before he could prevent it. Sirius's expression softened.

"There you are."

The smile disappeared.

"Everyone keeps looking at them now."

"Hermione and Draconia?"

Harry nodded.

"Because of me."

"Because a grown woman decided three friends caring about each other was less interesting than inventing something ugly."

"It still happens because they're near me."

Saying it aloud revealed the thought's shape. Harry was not only afraid the girls would be hurt. He was preparing to withdraw before either could decide his friendship cost too much.

"Most worthwhile things in my life happened because I stood near your father when one of us had a terrible idea."

"Which of you usually had it?"

"James. Unless Remus asks."

The lie was comforting because they both recognised it. Sirius shifted. Harry saw shelves behind him and the edge of a lamp. He was at home, or somewhere arranged to resemble it.

"You don't protect people by deciding they'd be better without you," Sirius said. "You ask what they want. Then you believe their answer."

Harry said nothing. Sirius's face remained in the glass. He waited until Harry looked at him again before continuing.

"And you do not protect me by deciding you are one more task on my list."

Harry's eyes rose.

"Remus told you."

"The mirror told me. You've been holding it for three minutes like an apology."

"You're busy."

"I am your godfather. Worrying about you is not an interruption to the job. It is the job."

Harry rubbed his thumb along the mirror's frame.

"What if they get hurt?"

"Then you help. You apologise when it's your fault. You don't claim every cruel thing in the world merely because it reached them through you."

Footsteps sounded at the far end of the passage. Hermione and Draconia came around the corner together, the Prophet rolled like a weapon in Hermione's hand and Harry's abandoned bag hanging from Draconia's shoulder.

"Found," Sirius said.

"Apparently."

"Believe their answer."

The mirror darkened. Hermione stopped in front of the ledge.

"You left your books."

"I thought you liked books."

"Yours are badly annotated."

Draconia held out his bag. "We are going to the library."

Harry looked between them.

"Do I have a choice?"

"Yes," Hermione said.

Draconia said, "No."

Harry took the bag.

"Comforting."

Hagrid appeared towards the end of dinner the following evening. He did not enter the Great Hall. His face appeared between the doors while pudding was being served, beard damp with mist and eyes moving until they found Harry at the Gryffindor table. Then he winked. Hagrid's winks involved most of his face and had never communicated secrecy successfully. Half the table turned, following a signal which could have guided ships.

"Subtle," Harry said.

Hermione followed his gaze. "He wants you."

"Or he's developed something in his eye."

Hagrid jerked his head towards the Entrance Hall and disappeared. Draconia, seated at the end of the Slytherin table, had also noticed. Her questioning look crossed the distance between them. Harry gestured vaguely towards the doors. Draconia rose at once, apparently considering that an adequate explanation. Five minutes later, all three stood beneath the shelter of the stone steps while rain sifted across the grounds. Hagrid waited beside a column in an enormous moleskin coat.

"Harry," he said, then noticed the girls. "Ah."

"You winked at the entire Hall," Hermione told him.

"Did not."

"A Ravenclaw first-year followed you into the Entrance Hall."

"Thought he wanted directions."

"He did not."

Hagrid looked at Draconia.

"Thought I said Harry should come."

"You did not technically say anything," she replied.

Harry drew the Invisibility Cloak from beneath his robes.

"Whatever it is, they're coming."

Hagrid's gaze travelled over the three of them. Concern fought with whatever secret had drawn him to the castle, making his beard twitch as he considered arguing. The secret won.

"Bring a warm coat," he said.

"We're wearing coats."

"Warmer ones."

"That isn't ominous," Harry said.

They met him beside his hut at eleven.

Rain had thickened into a cold, needling sheet that silvered the dark windows and turned the path to mud. The castle rose behind them in broken towers of light. Ahead, the grounds vanished into a night so complete that the Forbidden Forest appeared less like trees than the edge of the world.

Fang whined from inside the hut, his claws scraping briefly against the door, but Hagrid did not invite them in. A crossbow hung over one shoulder, and the shutters of his lantern had been closed until only a thin line of yellow escaped.

"Cloak on," he whispered.

Harry unfolded it. At eleven, the three of them had fitted beneath the Cloak with room to spare. Three years later, concealment required negotiation, bent shoulders and a careful agreement about where everyone intended to put their feet.

"Your elbow is in my ribs," Hermione whispered.

"That is Draconia."

"It is not."

"Someone's standing on my foot."

"Both of you be quiet," Draconia breathed. "And Potter, move your head."

"I can't remove it."

"Consider trying."

Hagrid set off, and they followed as a single badly designed creature with six feet, progressing in short, awkward steps whenever his long strides threatened to leave them behind.

The route curved away from the Forest and descended towards a part of the grounds Harry had never visited. Wet grass brushed their ankles. Twice, Hermione's shoe sank deep enough to nearly pull the Cloak from Harry's shoulder. The air changed as they walked. Rain no longer smelled only of earth and leaves. Smoke entered it. Then came something sharper: scorched metal, singed wool and the heavy animal scent of a stable enlarged beyond reason.

Harry heard the first roar before he saw the fire. It travelled through the ground and brought every muscle in his body to a stop: a grinding growl, deep enough to be felt in his teeth, climbing into a shriek which tore across the hills. The sound belonged to no creature he knew. Birds erupted from the Forest's edge, scattering above the dark treetops as a second roar answered, then a third.

Orange light unfolded above the trees, turning the rain to glittering threads. Beneath the Cloak, Hermione caught Harry's sleeve.

"Keep moving," she whispered.

He did.

The field beyond the hill had been enclosed by a ring of upright posts and chains thick as Hagrid's wrists. Dozens of wizards moved between them in heavy hides, faces protected by iron masks. They shouted in several languages. Spells crossed the rain like coloured wire.

Inside the enclosure were four dragons. The nearest reared against its chains, scales shining an impossible blue-white whenever fire touched them and long horns curving from its head with the translucent colour of ice. Beside it, a green dragon clawed trenches through the mud. The third lay low and broad, coppery wings folded close while the spines along its back rose and fell with each breath. None appeared willing to be where the handlers had placed them.

"Swedish Short-Snout," Hermione whispered beneath the Cloak. Her gaze moved with each identification. "Common Welsh Green. The red one is a Chinese Fireball."

"You memorised them?" Harry breathed.

"There were books on the train."

Draconia leaned past him. "Naturally."

The fourth occupied the far end of the enclosure alone.

Black scales armoured its body from skull to tail. Its wings, half-open in agitation, seemed to cut pieces from the night. Bronze horns crowned its head, and shorter spikes bristled along a tail that struck the ground hard enough to shake water from the grass. A clutch of pale stone-coloured eggs lay within the hollow between its forelegs.

The dragon lowered its head over the eggs. Yellow eyes opened beneath its bronze horns, and Harry forgot the rain. He remembered green stone. A skin moving through pipes. The rasp of scales across the Chamber floor and the wet, ancient breath of something made to kill whoever met its eyes. The dragon turned towards the hill, its pupil narrowing. Harry knew it could not see through the Cloak, but his body did not believe him. His knees locked, and the breath he had taken would not leave his chest.

Stone seemed to close above him. His scar prickled with remembered heat rather than fresh pain. He heard Riddle's voice where there was only rain, calm and curious as Draconia raised her wand against him in the hospital wing. Harry stepped back, pulling the Cloak tight across their shoulders. Draconia's hand found his unmarked wrist and pressed once, giving him something to feel beyond the memory of scales.

Here.

Hermione moved until her shoulder rested against his, steady beneath the wet fabric.

Now.

Harry forced air into his lungs. Rain struck his hair where the Cloak had slipped. Mud gave beneath his shoes. Draconia's hand was cold. Hermione was whispering numbers, too quietly for anyone else to hear.

Five things.

Harry looked at the handler nearest the fence. The red patch on his coat. The silver chain. Hagrid's shuttered lantern. Hermione's wet braid. The dark green stone in Draconia's ring.

Four things he could feel.

He reached the third before the enclosure returned to its proper size. The Horntail was not the basilisk, and the hill was not underground. Draconia stood beside him, her fingers cold and alive against his wrist, instead of pale and empty beneath Tom Riddle's control. Harry swallowed, concentrating upon the pressure of her hand.

"I'm here," he whispered.

Her grip loosened without leaving him.

Below them, Hagrid had reached a group of officials beside the enclosure. Madame Maxime stood among them in a sable coat, too tall to conceal even in darkness. Bagman's yellow robes flashed beneath a rain cape. Karkaroff was nowhere visible, though one of his students' fur hats briefly moved behind a distant hedge.

"The champions aren't meant to know," Hermione breathed.

"Then security is going well," Harry said.

His voice sounded almost normal. Draconia watched him for another second, checking the answer beneath the joke, before turning back towards the field. A handler raised his wand.

"Together!" he shouted.

Eight jets of red light struck the blue dragon. It fought them, claws ripping at the earth, neck twisting against restraints that blazed white with magic. One spell glanced from its scales and shattered a fencepost. The watchers scattered.

The dragon inhaled, and every handler dropped. Fire passed over their heads in a blue sheet, striking the hill with heat fierce enough to turn rain to steam. Harry flinched as his damp fringe dried instantly; beneath the Cloak, the air became hot and difficult to breathe. When the fire ceased, the shattered post continued burning.

"Those," Hermione said carefully, "are for the first task."

"I'd hoped they were decorative."

"The eggs matter," Draconia said.

Harry followed her gaze. Every dragon guarded a nest.

"They'll make us take one."

"Or approach one," Hermione said. "The distinction may matter."

"Lovely."

The Hungarian Horntail's tail smashed sideways, splitting a boulder with a crack which carried up the hill. Below them, Hagrid beamed at the dragons as though someone had presented him with four unusually charming puppies. Harry looked from him to the broken stone.

"He thinks he's helping," Harry said.

"He is helping," Hermione replied. "He is also appalling at rules."

"One of his better qualities."

They remained long enough to watch the dragons secured in separate enclosures. An official drew brass discs from a leather case and fastened one to each gate, establishing the lots the champions would draw later. Distance hid which number belonged to which beast. The Horntail reacted to its new chains by sending a burst of fire across the mud. A handler rolled behind a trough with his sleeve burning.

Harry watched the flames. Fear settled differently once it had a shape. For weeks, the first task had been an empty space large enough to contain anything; now it had wings, teeth and a temper, and he could begin to think about what it might do.

He preferred it. That concerned him.

Hagrid returned after midnight.

They followed him back towards the hut before removing the Cloak, emerging red-faced and crooked from walking pressed together. Cold air reached Harry's neck as he straightened. Hermione immediately checked his pupils by wandlight, while Draconia examined the hand she had been holding as though an injury might appear there now they could properly see it.

"I'm all right," Harry said.

Neither responded.

"I knew what was happening this time."

That made Hermione look at him.

"This time?" Hagrid asked.

"The Chamber," Draconia said.

Hagrid's happiness faded. His gaze moved from Harry's face to the girls still standing close on either side of him.

"Harry, I didn' think—"

"You showed me what I'm facing. It helped."

"Nearly gave yeh a fit."

"Not the helpful part."

Hagrid dragged one hand down his beard.

"Should've told Dumbledore. Shouldn' have brought yeh down like this."

"If you told Dumbledore, he'd have stopped you."

"Exactly."

"Which is why you didn't."

Hagrid looked miserable. Harry touched the enormous sleeve of his coat.

"Thank you."

"Don' thank me till after the task."

"That's encouraging."

Hagrid glanced towards the castle. "Suppose yeh'll need ter tell—"

He stopped himself. Harry knew the name anyway.

"Cedric."

"Didn' say that."

"Maxime knows, so Fleur knows. Someone from Durmstrang was in the hedge."

"Could've been anybody."

"Anybody wearing a Durmstrang hat."

Hagrid found the roof fascinating, examining its wet thatch as though the answer might be hidden beneath it.

"Cedric deserves the same warning," Harry said.

Draconia's expression sharpened. "He is your competition."

"He believed me in front of his entire House."

"That does not make the Tournament safe."

"No. It makes telling him fair."

She studied Harry through the rain. For a moment he expected an argument, but Draconia glanced towards the enclosures and nodded once, accepting what he had chosen.

"Tell him privately."

Harry found Cedric the next morning outside the Owlery. The older boy was fastening a small parcel to the leg of a tawny owl. He looked over his shoulder when Harry entered, then finished the knot before speaking.

"If this is about the article, Cho says Rita writes about everyone eventually and survival is possible."

"How reassuring."

The owl launched into the grey morning. Harry closed the door.

"The first task is dragons."

Cedric stared. Wind moved through the open arches, stirring straw across the floor while the owls shifted and rustled upon their perches. Harry waited for the word to settle.

"Dragons," he repeated.

"Four of them. Each one has a nest. I don't know what we're taking, but we're going near the eggs."

"How do you know?"

"Hagrid has never met a dangerous creature he didn't want to introduce."

Understanding reached Cedric's face. He turned towards the arches and stood silent for several seconds, looking out at grounds which concealed rather more than either of them had expected.

"Are you supposed to tell me?"

"Probably not."

"Why did you?"

Harry leaned against the wall.

"Maxime saw them. Someone from Durmstrang did too. If everyone cheats except Hogwarts, it becomes less noble and more stupid."

Cedric's mouth moved at one corner.

"That sounds like something Hermione Granger would say."

"She used more words."

"And Malfoy?"

"Suggested making you sign a confidentiality agreement."

"Did she?"

"She was tired."

Cedric looked back at him.

"Thank you, Harry."

The sincerity made Harry uncomfortable. It was easier to talk about cheating than admit how much he needed Cedric to survive it too.

"Don't die. It'll make telling you seem badly planned."

"I'll do my best."

Harry reached the door.

"And Cedric?"

"Yes?"

"They breathe fire."

Cedric stared at him.

"That part," Harry said, "was mostly for my own enjoyment."

He escaped before Cedric found anything to throw.

The library campaign began before Harry returned from the Owlery.

Hermione had claimed the largest table at the back and surrounded it with books from three separate sections. Their titles formed a history of increasingly poor decisions: Dragons of the British Isles, Defensive Magic Against Armoured Beasts, When Pets Become Problems and a scorched volume called A Healer's Guide to Magical Burns.

Draconia sat at the centre of the arrangement with a sheet of parchment divided into four columns.

"Did he believe you?" Hermione asked.

"Yes."

"Did anyone hear?" Draconia asked.

"Only the owls."

"Unreliable creatures."

Harry took the chair between them and looked over the columns: BODY, FIRE, TEMPERAMENT and AVOID. The final list extended onto a second page, which Draconia had already begun filling.

"Encouraging," he said.

"We are planning for the Horntail," Draconia said.

"I haven't drawn one."

"If the plan survives the worst dragon, the others become improvements."

Hermione turned a page. "Statistically, only slight improvements."

She pushed the book towards Harry. In the moving illustration, a wizard struck a dragon's belly with a curse, only for it to rebound and send him disappearing from the frame in flames. A moment later he reappeared, ready to make the same mistake.

"Dragon hide resists most direct spells."

"I gathered that from the screaming."

"The eyes are vulnerable," Draconia said. "A Conjunctivitis Curse could impair it long enough for you to reach the nest."

"Could."

"Yes."

"I dislike that word."

"Then you are going to hate the literature."

Hermione pushed another book across the table. "A dragon's underside is less heavily armoured, but getting beneath one would be—"

"Short-sighted?"

"Fatal."

"That too."

They worked through the morning.

Every possible advantage demanded proximity Harry did not want. Freezing charms failed against internal fire. Stunning spells required at least seven trained wizards. Transfiguration became unstable within the magical field surrounding mature dragons. Their hearing was excellent, their sense of smell better, and their eyesight—apart from the narrow angle behind the skull—gave Harry little hope of approaching unseen.

Hermione proposed distraction, Draconia controlled blindness, and Harry moving to France. Neither girl wrote down his suggestion, despite the considerable distance it would place between him and a dragon.

At midday, Madam Pince removed their ink after Hermione dripped it across a description of Romanian nesting grounds. They relocated to an empty classroom on the third floor and continued with borrowed chalk.

By two, a dragon occupied most of the blackboard. Hermione had drawn the body, Draconia added measurements and angles of attack, and Harry supplied fire around the tail. Chalk dust whitened their fingers and gathered beneath the beast's badly proportioned feet.

"Fire does not emerge from that end," Hermione said.

"This one is special."

"This one is anatomically offensive."

"It represents my feelings."

Draconia stood before the board with her arms folded.

"You need distance."

"Yes."

"And speed."

"Ideally."

"Your fastest form of movement is a broom."

Hermione looked up, and Harry stopped drawing. For a moment, the scratch of chalk ceased and the same thought moved across all three faces.

"Only our wands are permitted into the arena," Hermione said.

"That does not mean only wands may be used," Draconia replied. "The rules state each champion enters carrying a wand. They do not prohibit summoning an external object."

"The Firebolt will be in Gryffindor Tower."

Harry looked out of the classroom window. The Quidditch pitch stood beyond the grounds, its hoops thin and dark against the sky.

"Not if I move it before the task."

Hermione's eyes narrowed in concentration. "The Summoning Charm works across distance provided the caster has a sufficiently precise sense of the object."

"And sufficient power," Draconia said.

"Harry has power."

"Harry also has less than four days."

He lowered the chalk.

"I'm in the room."

"We know," they said together.

Harry added two enormous wings to Hermione's dragon.

"Then let's see if I can summon something."

He could not.

The cushion Hermione placed at the far end of the classroom trembled when Harry cast, then settled exactly where it had been. On the sixth attempt it tipped forward; on the ninth, the chair beneath it slid three inches and fell with a scrape and a thud. The cushion landed triumphantly upon the floor.

"Progress," Harry said.

"You summoned the wrong object," Hermione replied.

"The chair seemed more committed."

Hermione corrected his wand movement while Draconia adjusted his stance, nudging one foot into place with hers. When Harry cast again, the cushion leapt from the floor, struck a desk and knocked a jar of quills into the air. Feathers exploded around them, drifting more gently than anything he had managed to summon.

Harry grinned.

Hermione removed one from her hair. "Better."

Draconia plucked a white quill from Harry's fringe, her fingers pausing there. The touch lasted no longer than necessary, which was long enough for him to notice how the classroom's winter light turned her grey eyes almost silver and forget the spell he had just cast. She looked away first, turning the quill between her fingers.

"Again," she said.

The cushion hit him in the face.

Moody stopped Harry after Defence that afternoon. The other students had already left. Hermione waited beyond the open door. Draconia stood beside her, pretending not to watch while watching with considerable skill. Moody extinguished the dark-detector spinning on his desk.

"Preparing, Potter?"

"For next week's essay?"

The magical eye revolved towards the door.

"For what comes first."

Harry's hand tightened around his bag.

"Do you know what the task is?"

"Wouldn't be much use as a security adviser if I didn't."

"Are you allowed to tell me?"

"No."

Moody's scarred mouth bent.

"Wouldn't need to. You're Hagrid's friend. He has the restraint of an enthusiastic landslide."

The answer was plausible, and Harry distrusted how quickly it arrived. Moody had a way of closing questions before Harry had properly decided why he was asking them. Moody came around the desk. His wooden leg struck the floor in deliberate beats.

"Danger makes people stupid. They learn one defensive spell and convince themselves every problem wants to be fought. You ever fight a dragon before?"

"No."

"Then don't."

"That simplifies things."

"Get what you need. Get out." Moody's ordinary eye fixed upon him. "Use what you do better than the rest of them."

"Sarcasm?"

"If you can ride it."

The magical eye returned to the door. Harry felt the shape of the advice settle around the plan already forming.

"Professor," he said, "did you know my name was in the Goblet before it came out?"

For the first time, Moody became completely still. Even the magical eye stopped. It faced Harry while the ordinary one remained slightly to the left, leaving him with the uncanny impression that one part of Moody had been caught before the rest decided how to react.

"No."

The answer sounded clean, without a pause Harry could use to judge it. That did not make it true.

"You watched the Goblet all evening."

"I watch every old object capable of binding children to dangerous promises."

"Before my name came out?"

"Especially before." Moody leaned closer. "A trap does not become a trap when it closes."

The words were sensible. Harry had begun to distrust how often Moody gave him sensible words at precisely the moment he needed them.

"Right."

Harry left. Hermione waited until they reached the stairs.

"What did he want?"

"To tell me not to fight a dragon."

Draconia glanced back towards the classroom. "How did he know you knew?"

"He blamed Hagrid."

"Reasonable," Hermione said.

"Convenient," Draconia replied.

"He also said the Goblet was always a threat," Harry added.

Hermione considered. "That is consistent with everything we found."

"And with someone wishing to explain why he watched it," Draconia said.

Harry agreed with both.

Dumbledore set aside the following day for training and gave Sirius and Remus permission to visit. Permission came with a classroom beneath the Astronomy Tower, three monitoring charms and Professor McGonagall appearing every forty minutes to verify that Sirius had not damaged the building.

"She has no faith in me," Sirius said after the second inspection.

Remus examined the smoking remains of a practice target. "An inexplicable prejudice."

The room had once been used for advanced Charms. Tiered benches faced a broad square of empty floor whose walls carried old scorch marks and the faint outlines of spells removed by years of cleaning. Rain struck the high windows. Sirius had brought padded targets, a bag of objects for summoning and enough food for a small siege.

Harry picked up one of the wrapped sandwiches.

"Narcissa?"

"She intercepted us at the gates," Remus said.

"Threatened us with bodily harm if you missed lunch," Sirius added.

Draconia checked the contents. "That sounds like Mother."

Hermione spread their research across the first bench.

"The plan is to summon the Firebolt, mount before the dragon reaches him and remain above the effective range of the flame."

Sirius looked delighted, his gaze already moving towards the practice floor. Remus kept his attention upon Hermione's calculations.

"How far is the effective range?" he asked.

"It varies."

"How far?"

"Between twenty and fifty feet."

"That is not a small variation."

"Dragons are not uniform," Hermione said defensively.

Sirius moved a model of the Horntail into the centre of the floor. It was hardly larger than a cat, but it snapped at his fingers with convincing hatred.

"Flying is the right instinct. The dragon owns the ground. Harry owns the air."

"The dragon has wings," Remus pointed out.

"It'll be chained near its eggs."

Draconia looked at Sirius. "If a chain breaks?"

His delight faded by a degree.

"Then he needs a second plan."

"And a third," Hermione said.

"And a fourth," Draconia added.

Harry unwrapped the sandwich.

"I'm beginning to miss the basilisk. It had the courtesy to stay underground."

No one laughed. Harry took a bite, discovering too late that mentioning the basilisk had given all four of them something else to worry about.

"That was a joke."

"We know," Sirius said.

He crossed the floor and rested both hands on Harry's shoulders.

"Before anything else: you are not fighting what happened in the Chamber. You are not proving you can endure it again. You take the egg, and you leave."

Harry looked at him. Sirius's eyes were steady. Beneath the steadiness lived the same fear Harry had seen at breakfast, given a task so it would not become panic.

"I know."

"Say it back."

"I'm not fighting the dragon."

"And?"

"I'm taking whatever they want and leaving."

Sirius squeezed once, then released him.

"Good. Now summon the ugly boot."

An ugly boot sat upon the highest bench, its cracked leather sagging around a bent buckle. Harry raised his wand and fixed his attention upon it.

"Accio boot."

The boot rose, travelled six feet and fell into Remus's lap.

"It likes you," Harry said.

Remus moved it to the far end of the room.

"Again."

They trained for seven hours.

Distance changed the spell. At ten feet, Harry could pull almost anything towards him. At thirty, weight mattered. Beyond the classroom door, walls interfered less than uncertainty: if he could picture the object precisely, it responded; if his attention slipped towards the dragon, the spell caught whatever occupied the nearest approximation.

He summoned three books, a brass candlestick and Sirius's left glove. The boot did not move.

"You're asking," Sirius said.

Harry lowered his wand. "Spells care about manners now?"

"Some do. This one doesn't. You know where the thing is. You know it belongs in your hand. Don't request it. Make the world correct the distance."

Hermione's quill slowed as she wrote that down. Remus set the boot behind a stone partition. Harry pictured the split sole, the cracked brown leather and the bent buckle. He thought of the object not as something elsewhere but as something prevented from reaching him.

"Accio boot."

There was a pause. Then the boot shot around the partition and struck Sirius in the chest, making his coat flap against him. He caught it before it fell.

"Excellent."

"It still prefers other people."

"We'll correct its judgement."

Sirius made the next attempts harder. He hid objects inside cabinets, beneath cushions and behind wards designed to imitate the magical interference of the arena. Remus taught Harry to hold a Shield Charm with his left hand while summoning with his right. Hermione fired soft practice jinxes at irregular intervals. Draconia used harder ones after accusing Hermione of making them polite.

One caught Harry behind the knee, dropping him to the floor just as the brass candlestick he had summoned flew towards his face. Draconia deflected it with a sharp movement of her wand, sending it ringing against an empty bench.

"The dragon will not pause because you lose your balance," she said.

"You enjoyed that."

"A little."

"Again," Remus said.

By late afternoon, sweat darkened Harry's collar and every muscle in his wand arm trembled. The contract line had warmed twice but had not flared. He summoned while running, while turning, after being knocked down and once while Sirius shouted contradictory instructions directly into his ear.

The last exercise placed Harry at the bottom of the Astronomy staircase. His Firebolt waited inside the training room six floors above. Hermione and Draconia stood beside the broom. Sirius leaned over the rail at the top landing. Remus remained halfway between them and Harry, prepared to stop the broom if it left the staircase badly.

Harry closed his eyes and pictured the Firebolt: more than polished ash and iron fittings, more than the outline of a broom. He remembered the shallow scratch near its tail from a poorly judged landing in third year, the place where his right hand rested, the vibration through the foot grips as it gathered speed. Cold air cutting tears from his eyes above the pitch. The peculiar stillness which arrived whenever the ground fell away and his body remembered what freedom felt like.

The broom knew him. He knew it. Harry opened his eyes.

"Accio Firebolt."

Nothing happened for one second, then two. Harry kept his wand raised, holding the shape of the broom in his mind until wood struck stone above him and Sirius shouted. The Firebolt shot into view around the top landing, descended the spiral in a blur and skimmed past Remus's outstretched hand. Harry held his ground.

At the final turn, the broom tilted and its handle struck his palm. Harry's fingers closed around the wood. For a moment, the entire tower went silent; then Hermione's cheer travelled down the stairs, bright enough to make him grin before he had properly caught his breath.

Draconia did not cheer. She reached the bottom fast enough to suggest she had run, her gaze moving from the broom to Harry's hand before she looked at his face.

"Again," she said.

Harry laughed.

"I just did it."

"You took too long."

"It crossed six floors."

"The Horntail can breathe fire in less than two seconds."

Sirius and Remus descended behind her.

"She's right," Sirius said.

Harry stared at him. "Whose side are you on?"

"The side where you live."

Sirius took the Firebolt, carried it back up the stairs and made Harry summon it until darkness filled the windows. On the seventh successful attempt, it reached him before Remus counted four. On the tenth, before three. The eleventh failed entirely because exhaustion broke Harry's concentration. He stood at the bottom with his wand extended and nothing came. Sirius ended the session, and Harry's objection did nothing to alter his decision.

"You don't improve a spell by teaching your body to cast it badly," Remus said. "Rest is part of the work."

"The task won't stop when I'm tired."

"Which is why you do not arrive tired," Draconia said.

Hermione came down the final steps carrying Harry's coat and settled it around his shoulders before he could tell her he was not cold. His hands shook as he fastened it. He was cold after all, and the warmth beneath the heavy fabric made him realise how tired he had become. Sirius saw.

The others began clearing the room. Sirius drew Harry towards the window and sat with him on the broad stone ledge. Below them, the castle grounds had disappeared into rain. The glass reflected them instead: Sirius all dark edges and restless hands, Harry pale with weariness beside him.

"You were brilliant," Sirius said.

Harry looked down at the wand across his knees.

"I failed the last one."

"You succeeded ten times before it."

"The dragon only has to succeed once."

Sirius leaned back against the window frame.

"Do you know what James did before our Defence practical in fifth year?"

"Declared he hadn't studied because talent was hereditary?"

"That was before every practical. Afterwards, he locked himself in a bathroom and was sick."

Harry turned.

"Dad?"

"Your father was terrified of failing whenever it mattered. He simply considered fear a private insult and refused to let anyone witness it."

"That sounds healthy."

"We were fifteen. Health was for other people."

Harry smiled faintly. Sirius looked towards the room, where Remus had involved Hermione and Draconia in dismantling a ward.

"You don't have to perform courage for me."

"I'm not."

"You're doing it now."

Harry's denial stopped. The glass was cold against the back of his head.

"I know what it sounds like," he said. "The dragon."

Sirius waited.

"Not exactly. But when it moved, I was back there. I knew I wasn't, and it didn't matter. What if that happens inside the arena?"

"Then you find the present."

"What if I can't?"

"You found it on the hill."

"They helped."

"They'll be there."

"In the stands."

"Then borrow them from the stands." Sirius touched two fingers against Harry's chest. "You know what Hermione will say. You know what Draconia will tell you to notice. Keep their voices here until you can hear your own."

Harry looked through the room. Hermione was arguing that a ward should be unwoven methodically. Draconia had already drawn her wand. Remus stood between them with the resigned patience of a man who had once supervised James Potter and Sirius Black.

"They're loud," Harry said.

"Useful, isn't it?"

Sirius placed an arm around Harry's shoulders, and he let his head rest against him. For several minutes neither spoke. Rain softened the windows' reflection, and the voices from the practice floor became an ordinary murmur. No revelation arrived. The Tournament did not become fair. The dragon remained beyond the dark grounds, breathing fire over its eggs. But Sirius was warm beside him, and somewhere within the castle Hermione and Draconia had already planned tomorrow. Harry allowed that to be enough for one evening.

Rain returned on the day before the task, striking the castle windows throughout breakfast and continuing through every lesson until the sky had turned the colour of iron. Before lunch, Bagman reminded the students that classes would end at noon the following day. His description of the first task included the phrases "historic spectacle" and "perfectly manageable risk."

Professor McGonagall snapped the handle from her teacup. No one commented.

After lessons on the last day before the task, the remaining hours disappeared into repetition.

Harry summoned the Firebolt from behind doors, around corners and across the width of the Quidditch pitch. He practised mounting before it fully stopped, rolling beneath its path and catching the handle with either hand. The first attempt spun him sideways into the grass. The fourth tore the wand from his fingers. On the ninth, he mounted cleanly and rose twenty feet before Hermione shouted that he had forgotten to secure his left foot.

Draconia recorded every failure, her quill moving briskly as Harry brushed grass from his robes. She recorded every success as well, though those received less commentary.

"Two-point-eight seconds," she called from the ground.

Harry circled overhead. "You said three was enough."

"I have revised my standards."

"Of course you have."

"Again."

He dived.

The cold stripped every thought from him except distance and motion. The Firebolt responded before pressure became instruction. He cut across the empty pitch, rolled between two practice Bludgers Sirius had left behind and pulled out of the dive close enough to the grass for its wet tips to brush his shoe.

For several minutes, fear could not keep pace. Then Harry landed and found Hermione holding a letter.

"From Sirius."

The parchment contained four lines.

Crouch is officially unwell. No one at the Ministry has seen him since the selection, but instructions continue to arrive in his handwriting. His house is sealed from inside. Winky recognised the outer wards but became frightened when Remus asked who could have changed them. We are checking the World Cup records again. Do not investigate. Yes, that sentence is aimed at all three of you.

Draconia read it over Harry's shoulder.

"He understands us poorly."

"He understands us exactly," Hermione said. "That is why it is underlined."

Harry folded the message.

The Tournament pressed against one side of his mind, Crouch the other: a locked house, a dismissed elf and official instructions issued by a man no one saw. Somewhere between them lay the hand which had entered Harry's name. He slid the letter safely into his pocket.

He could do nothing about it before the first task. For once, accepting that did not feel like surrender. It felt like choosing which danger deserved his attention first. They practised until sunset.

Harry's final practice took place after curfew. The Firebolt remained in his dormitory. Harry stood in the entrance courtyard beneath an arch, separated from it by seven floors, a portrait hole, several corridors and most of Gryffindor Tower. Rain blew across the flagstones. Hermione held the timer. Draconia watched the castle windows for movement.

"Ready," Hermione said.

Harry tightened his fingers around his wand. He pictured the broom.

"Accio Firebolt."

The spell left him with enough force to lift his hair. For two seconds, nothing answered. Then distant shouts echoed from within the castle, followed by something striking a wall and the unmistakable crash of a suit of armour. Harry kept the broom firmly in his thoughts.

Harry winced. "That sounds expensive."

"Concentrate," Draconia said.

A window opened six floors above, and the Firebolt burst through it. Swift and unwavering, it descended through the rain until Harry caught its handle before Hermione's timer reached three. He mounted in the same movement, barely feeling the broom steady beneath him. One push carried him above the courtyard. Another took him over the roofline, where the castle spread beneath him in wet black stone and lighted windows. He flew a single circle around the Astronomy Tower before returning.

Hermione waited beneath the arch with rain across her face and an expression so relieved it bordered on anger. Draconia looked at the Firebolt rather than Harry.

"Again," she said from habit.

Hermione lowered the timer. "No."

"One more would confirm consistency."

"One more could exhaust him."

"Three seconds remains too long."

"Two-point-seven."

"The dragon does not round down."

Harry dismounted.

"I'm going to bed."

Both fell silent. Harry looked from one to the other, his hand resting upon the broom handle while rain dripped from its tail.

"That wasn't a request."

"Good," Hermione said. "For once."

They returned the broom and walked to the fourth floor together. At the junction where the route to the dungeons divided from the stairs to Gryffindor Tower, Draconia stopped. The castle had quietened around them. Rain whispered against a narrow window. Somewhere below, water moved through ancient pipes. Harry expected her to say goodnight. Instead, Draconia reached up and straightened the collar of his robes, looking at the fabric rather than his face.

It was a Narcissa gesture, familiar from King's Cross, hospital corridors and moments when emotion threatened to become visible before other people. Draconia's fingers were less steady, catching briefly upon the edge of his collar.

"You do not need to impress them tomorrow," she said.

"I know."

"The judges. The school. Anyone."

"Draco—"

"Take what the task requires. Then leave."

Sirius's words, returned in her voice. Harry's answer softened as he understood how carefully she had been carrying them.

"I will."

Her hands remained at his collar for a moment.

"Promise properly."

Harry understood what she meant. Promises had become dangerous between them—not because they did not trust one another, but because they did. Last year, he had promised not to disappear inside himself. In the summer, they had promised to answer. Each promise created a place where failure could hurt all three.

He looked at Hermione.

She stood close enough that her sleeve touched his. Worry had hollowed shadows beneath her eyes. There was ink along the side of her hand, as always, and a small burn upon one thumb from testing a flame-resistant charm she would not be allowed to cast from the stands.

Harry reached for that hand. Then he took Draconia's.

"I promise I'll come back to you."

Draconia closed her eyes, and Hermione's fingers tightened around his. In the silence, Harry could hear rain gathering against the narrow window.

"That is not entirely within your control," she whispered.

"Most promises aren't."

"Then promise what is."

Harry thought.

"I promise I won't stay because I think leaving makes me a coward. I won't fight it longer than I have to. If something goes wrong, I'll choose getting back over winning."

Hermione searched his face.

"All right."

Draconia opened her eyes.

The control had returned, but fear remained visible beneath it. Harry did not ask her to hide it better. He raised their joined hands and pressed his forehead briefly against her knuckles, feeling her breath catch at the touch. The sound reached somewhere he was not prepared to examine. Then he did the same to Hermione's.

Hermione stared at him, cheeks colouring in the dim corridor.

"What was that?" she asked.

Harry lowered their hands.

"I don't know."

"You generally should before doing things."

"It seemed right."

Draconia's thumb moved once across his fingers.

"It was," she said.

None of them let go immediately.

The moment carried no name. Friendship remained too small for some parts of it and the other available words felt too large, too early and too easily damaged by examination. It was fear, loyalty and the steady accumulation of choosing one another. For now, that was enough.

Footsteps sounded on the staircase, and all three separated with remarkable speed. Filch rounded the corner carrying a lantern, its swinging light finding their faces as his narrowed eyes moved between them.

"Out after curfew."

Harry glanced at the clock above Filch's shoulder.

"Only by twelve minutes."

"Rules don't stop bein' rules for champions."

"That's the best news I've had all month."

Filch appeared uncertain whether he had been insulted. Hermione used the delay.

"Professor McGonagall authorised late preparation."

She produced a signed note, smoothed its fold and held it beneath the lantern. Filch read it twice before returning it with visible disappointment.

"Straight to your common rooms."

Draconia inclined her head. "A pleasure as always."

They separated at last. Harry climbed towards Gryffindor Tower alone, though he could still feel the warmth of their hands against his fingers.
