5

Snow had begun falling during the night.

It lay across the castle roofs in smooth white layers and gathered along the ledges outside the Gryffindor tower, brightening the dormitory with the pale, colourless light of early morning. Frost feathered the windows. Beyond the glass, the mountains had disappeared behind a bank of cloud, leaving Hogwarts suspended between a white sky and grounds that had not yet been disturbed by footprints.

Harry woke to an empty room.

The other boys' curtains had already been pulled back. Their trunks stood closed at the ends of their beds, and the usual morning debris—discarded socks, half-fastened schoolbags and books abandoned wherever their owners had finished reading—remained behind. Nobody had woken him before leaving.

Harry lay still beneath the blankets and listened to the wind press softly against the windows.

Perhaps they had gone down early. Perhaps Dean had remembered an unfinished essay or Neville had wanted extra time to find whichever object he had misplaced before breakfast. Any one of those explanations might have been true.

All of them leaving without making enough noise to wake him required more imagination.

Harry dressed slowly. His reflection in the small mirror above the washstand looked much the same as it had the previous morning: untidy hair, crooked glasses and a lightning-shaped scar which had suddenly become interesting to everyone again. He pushed his fringe across it, watched the hair spring back into place and decided there were battles even magic could not win.

The common room was almost empty. A few younger students occupied the chairs nearest the fire, speaking quietly over their homework. Their conversation stopped when Harry descended the staircase.

Hermione waited beside the portrait hole with her bag already over one shoulder. She had chosen a position from which she could see the boys' staircase, although the book in her hands allowed her to pretend this was accidental.

"You're late," she said.

"Good morning."

"It stopped being morning three minutes ago."

"I don't think time works like that."

"You would if you had read A Beginner's Guide to Magical Chronometry."

"Has anyone?"

"I have."

"Of course you have."

She closed the book and examined him. Her attention travelled over his face, his unevenly buttoned robes and the tie he had apparently tied without involving a mirror.

Harry began correcting the buttons. "They left early."

"I noticed."

Neither of them said who they were.

Hermione reached up and straightened his tie. "They may be avoiding the crowds."

"At breakfast?"

"It is possible."

"So is spontaneous human transfiguration."

"That has never been reliably documented."

"Then I'll try not to worry."

The portrait swung open.

Cold air drifted through the passage beyond, carrying the distant noise of hundreds of students making their way towards the Great Hall. Hermione fell into step beside him. She began explaining an inconsistency she had found in their Transfiguration reading, speaking with more energy than the subject deserved and never allowing enough silence for the whispers around them to become distinct.

The castle appeared altered beneath the winter light. Snow shone through the tall corridor windows and softened the grey stone, while draughts moved beneath doors and stirred the flames in the wall brackets. Suits of armour gleamed beside wreaths of holly the house-elves had begun hanging for Christmas. Portraits leant from their frames to discuss the weather, their conversations changing whenever Harry passed.

On the second-floor landing, a pair of younger Ravenclaws saw him approaching and stepped hurriedly into an alcove.

Harry glanced at the narrow recess. "Perhaps they've discovered a secret passage."

"They haven't."

"They seem committed to it."

Hermione's voice sharpened. "You shouldn't have to pretend this is amusing."

"I'm not pretending. If they stay in there much longer, they'll miss breakfast."

She looked back at the alcove. One of the Ravenclaws had attempted to hide behind a decorative vase which reached only to his waist.

"That might improve the conversation," she admitted.

The smell of toast and woodsmoke met them outside the Great Hall. Inside, thousands of candles floated beneath a ceiling heavy with enchanted clouds. The windows had misted from the warmth, and wet scarves hung over benches wherever students had already ventured outside. Golden plates clattered, owls called from the rafters and four long tables overflowed with the usual morning confusion.

Harry and Hermione entered.

The Hall did not fall silent. It came close.

A fork scraped across a plate at the Hufflepuff table. Someone whispered Harry's name near the doors, followed by the word snake. At the Slytherin table, Pansy Parkinson leant towards Draconia and said something which made two older students laugh.

Draconia had already seen Harry.

She sat very straight among the green-and-silver uniforms, one hand curved around a goblet of pumpkin juice. Her gaze moved from him to the students watching his entrance. Whatever tiredness had been visible in the library the previous evening disappeared beneath the cool composure she wore whenever Slytherin demanded it.

Pansy spoke again, louder this time.

"Perhaps Potter can ask the sausages whether they know who opened the Chamber."

The laughter around her was uncertain.

Draconia placed her goblet on the table. "Why would he ask the sausages?"

Pansy faltered. "It was a joke."

"It wasn't funny the first time either."

"At least I'm not defending him."

"No," Draconia agreed. "You would first need to understand what happened."

Pansy's cheeks coloured. "Everyone knows what happened."

"Then everyone knows Potter ordered the snake away from Finch-Fletchley."

"He spoke Parseltongue."

"And?"

Pansy glanced around the table, searching for support. "Only Dark wizards speak it."

Draconia's voice remained level. "You weren't there, Pansy. I was. He stopped it."

Several students abruptly lowered their heads.

Harry found himself smiling despite the knot beneath his ribs.

Hermione guided him towards the Gryffindor table. "Don't encourage her."

"I haven't done anything."

"You're smiling."

"I'm allowed."

They found two spaces near the middle. The students opposite continued eating, although their concentration upon their plates became almost scholarly. Harry reached for the toast while Hermione filled both their plates and began discussing the day's timetable.

Across the Hall, Draconia resumed breakfast.

The separation between their tables had always seemed larger when other people noticed it. Draconia belonged among the students who were now wondering why she defended him; Harry belonged among the Gryffindors who had left the dormitory rather than walk downstairs beside him. A year earlier, the distance had been inconvenient.

That morning, it felt deliberate.

The post arrived before Harry could finish his first piece of toast.

Owls poured through the high windows in a confusion of wings, shaking loose snow across the tables. Parcels landed among plates. Letters struck students on the head, and a large tawny owl narrowly avoided Professor Flitwick before depositing the Daily Prophet in Professor McGonagall's tea.

One pale envelope fell into Draconia's waiting hand.

She recognised the handwriting at once. After reading the first few lines, she rose, collected her bag and carried the letter across the Hall.

Every head she passed turned with her.

Draconia stopped at the Gryffindor table. "Move."

A third-year occupying the place beside Hermione stared at her. "This is the Gryffindor table."

"Yes, I noticed. Move over."

He gathered his plate and shifted down the bench.

Draconia sat opposite Harry and laid the letter between them.

Hermione looked towards the Slytherin table. "You could have asked him."

"He moved, didn't he?"

"You displaced someone."

"He survived."

Harry reached for the envelope. "Who is it from?"

"Mother. She addressed it to all three of us because she has correctly concluded that none of us can be trusted individually."

Narcissa's precise handwriting covered three sheets of cream parchment. The letter began with ordinary news from the Manor. Draconia had left her winter gloves at home. Lucius continued complaining about Ministry searches while insisting they did not inconvenience him. Narcissa wished to know whether Harry had received the thicker blankets she had arranged to send and whether Hermione's parents preferred magical or Muggle post over Christmas.

The second page concerned the Duelling Club.

News of Harry's Parseltongue had already reached them. Lucius wanted an exact account of the incident before deciding whether Lockhart's supervision constituted negligence or required a more imaginative description. Narcissa instructed them not to allow frightened people to decide what they believed about one another.

Beneath that, she had added an update about Dobby.

The house-elf had not returned. None of the Manor's other elves admitted knowing where he had gone, although Narcissa suspected that loyalty might be limiting the usefulness of their answers. Dumbledore had been informed again. The promise that Dobby would not be punished for attempting to prevent harm remained unchanged.

Draconia folded the page before Harry could read the final lines twice.

"There was more," Hermione said.

"It concerned my gloves."

"You have never hidden information about gloves."

"My relationship with winter clothing is private."

Harry reached for the letter. Draconia moved it beyond his fingers.

"What did Narcissa actually write?"

Draconia hesitated. "She said she hoped you were eating."

Harry looked down at his plate.

The piece of toast in his hand had gone cold. Most of the breakfast Hermione had served him remained untouched.

"I've eaten."

Hermione examined the single missing corner of toast. "Technically."

Draconia pushed the marmalade towards him. "Mother will ask."

"Do you report everything I eat?"

"Only the disappointing meals."

"That must require a great deal of parchment."

"I'm running out of parchment."

Harry spread marmalade across the toast and took a bite.

Around them, the Great Hall gradually recovered its noise. Students still glanced in their direction, but Draconia remained seated in Slytherin green at the Gryffindor table, reading the remainder of her mother's letter while Hermione corrected the way Harry had packed his schoolbag.

The rumours had not disappeared.

They merely had to compete with the two people who knew him better.

The attention followed him into every lesson.

In Herbology, Ernie Macmillan switched places with Hannah Abbott rather than work at the bench opposite Harry. In Charms, a second-year waiting outside the classroom pressed himself against the wall when Harry approached. Even the suits of armour seemed to turn their helmets after him, although Harry suspected Peeves had something to do with that.

The only people who behaved normally were the professors, which was not necessarily comforting.

Professor McGonagall called upon him twice during Transfiguration and deducted five points when his beetle escaped beneath Neville's chair. Professor Flitwick congratulated him on a successful Freezing Charm. Lockhart attempted to reassure the class that Parseltongue, while "dramatic to the untrained ear," was nothing an experienced duellist could not counter.

Snape said nothing about it at all.

That silence proved worse.

Harry entered the dungeon for Potions on Thursday afternoon and felt every Slytherin eye turn towards him. The usual mixture of damp stone, crushed herbs and badly cleaned cauldrons seemed heavier in the cold. Greenish light entered through the high windows, wavering whenever the lake shifted beyond them.

Snape swept between the worktables.

"You will prepare a Deflating Draught," he said. "Instructions are on the board. If anyone manages to dissolve a cauldron, poison a partner or begin a discussion unrelated to the assigned potion, I shall assume they are volunteering to clean the storeroom until Easter."

Whispers died.

Harry set out his ingredients. Hermione took the place beside him before anyone could object.

Across the aisle, Draconia unpacked her scales with movements that were slightly too careful. Dark shadows remained beneath her eyes, and the cuff of her left sleeve had been pulled halfway over her hand. She had missed breakfast the previous day and arrived late to Charms that morning with no explanation beyond having lost track of time.

Pansy lowered her voice. "Perhaps Potter can ask the snake scales what they think."

Draconia's knife stopped against the chopping board.

Harry continued measuring lacewing water. He did not need a fight beginning over him, particularly when Snape was close enough to hear their breathing.

Pansy glanced towards Millicent and smiled. "He might discover which of them opened the Chamber."

"Parkinson," Draconia said quietly.

"What?"

"If you intend to be tedious, at least attempt originality. Half the school made that joke before lunch yesterday."

Pansy's smile thinned. "I didn't realise you had appointed yourself his guard dog."

"Someone ought to be on his side."

The knife came down through the shrivelfig root with enough force to split the board beneath it.

Snape appeared beside their table.

His gaze moved from the cracked wood to Draconia's hand. "Miss Malfoy."

"The board was defective."

"The board survived six years of Longbottom."

Neville looked up from the next table, uncertain whether he had been insulted.

Snape lifted the broken pieces. "Ten points from Slytherin. You will remain after class."

Pansy stared. Draconia's face revealed nothing.

Harry expected her to object. She merely took another board and resumed cutting.

When the lesson ended, Hermione packed slowly, watching Draconia remain beside the teacher's desk.

"We could wait," she said.

"Snape will notice."

"He has noticed."

Harry glanced back.

Snape stood with his hands braced upon the desk, speaking too quietly to be heard. Draconia answered without meeting his eyes. Her fingers had tightened around the strap of the green satchel she now carried everywhere.

She seemed smaller from a distance.

"Let's give her five minutes," Harry said.

They waited near the dungeon staircase.

Students passed them in clusters, leaving an empty circle around Harry. Nobody told him to move. Nobody came close enough to risk touching him.

Harry leant against the stone and folded his arms.

He had forgotten how quickly a crowd could make a person feel alone.

At Privet Drive, loneliness had been built into the house. It lived in the cupboard, the locks and the photographs which proved three people formed a family without him. Hogwarts had been different from the first night. It had been crowded and noisy and frequently dangerous, but he had never needed to wonder whether he belonged inside it.

Now students lowered their voices when he entered rooms. They watched his mouth, waiting for another language to emerge.

The castle had not changed.

Only the way it held him had.

Draconia came through the dungeon door alone.

"What did Snape want?" Hermione asked.

"To remind me that breaking school property is an inefficient method of correcting stupidity."

"He took points from his own house," Harry said. "I think Pansy's head nearly exploded."

"A small price."

Draconia began climbing the stairs. Hermione joined her, but Harry remained where he was.

They reached the landing before noticing.

Draconia turned. "Harry?"

"I need to go somewhere."

"Where?"

"Hagrid's."

Hermione studied him. "Do you want us to come?"

Harry nearly said yes. The word rose automatically, driven by the knowledge that neither girl would hesitate.

Then he pictured the quiet of Hagrid's hut, the fire and Fang and a place where nobody would examine every sentence he spoke.

"I think I want to go alone."

Neither protested.

Hermione nodded. "Come back before dinner."

"Are you planning to count me?"

"I am considering it."

Draconia descended one step. "If anyone bothers you—"

"I'll ask them to hiss on my behalf."

"That is not what I meant."

"I know."

Her hand tightened upon the satchel.

Harry gave them both a small smile and headed towards the entrance hall.

Harry crossed the grounds with his hands in his pockets.

The cold helped. It gave him a reason for his shoulders to draw inward and his head to remain lowered. By the time he reached the hut, the noise of the castle had faded behind the wind.

Fang announced his arrival before Harry knocked.

The boarhound hurled himself against the door, barking with such force that the hinges rattled. Hagrid shouted from inside, followed by the crash of metal and an accusation involving somebody's enormous paws.

The door opened.

"Harry!" Hagrid's face brightened beneath his beard. "Get in, then. It's freezin'."

Warmth rolled from the hut. A kettle steamed above the fire, and a collection of thick socks hung from a line stretched across the ceiling. Bundles of drying herbs obscured one window. The other had fogged around the edges, leaving only a wavering circle through which the castle could be seen.

Harry wiped his shoes and stepped inside.

Fang pressed his wet nose into Harry's chest.

"Hello to you too."

"He's missed yeh." Hagrid cleared a chair by moving a bucket of what appeared to be dead rats onto the floor. "Where're Hermione an' Draco?"

"Studying."

Hagrid paused.

Harry sat.

"Together?"

"They're probably arguing first."

"Right."

Hagrid poured tea into a mug large enough to wash socks in and pushed it across the table. Beside it he placed a rock cake. Harry examined the cake, then the fogged window, and decided the possibility of breaking a tooth could wait.

Hagrid took the chair opposite him.

The table creaked beneath his elbows. "So."

"So."

"How's things?"

"Good."

"Good?"

"Fine."

Fang dropped his head onto Harry's knee.

Hagrid watched him scratch behind the dog's ears. "Yeh know, Harry, I'm not very clever with people."

"You're better than most of the clever ones."

"Not sayin' much, is it?"

"No."

A smile moved briefly through Hagrid's beard and vanished.

"What happened at that duellin' thing?" he asked.

Harry traced a crack along the side of the mug. "You heard?"

"Whole school's heard. Had a third-year ask me whether snakes live in the forest because they're waitin' fer orders from you."

"What did you tell him?"

"That if he went lookin', he'd deserve whatever bit him."

Harry laughed despite himself.

The sound felt unfamiliar. He had spent the past two days making jokes for Hermione and Draconia because both girls watched him too closely when he became quiet. Laughing without an audience felt different.

Hagrid leant back. "Yeh told a snake ter leave someone alone."

"I think so."

"Seems decent enough ter me."

"It didn't sound decent."

"What's that got ter do with anythin'?"

Harry looked into the fire. "They all think I'm the Heir of Slytherin."

"Rubbish."

"You didn't even think about it."

"Didn't need ter."

"The Sorting Hat considered putting me in Slytherin."

Hagrid's brows rose.

Harry had not meant to tell him. The admission had escaped before he decided whether it was safe.

"It said I might do well there," he continued. "I didn't ask it not to. It chose Gryffindor anyway, but everyone acts like wanting anything connected to Slytherin proves you're dangerous."

Hagrid rubbed both hands over his beard.

"Yeh remember what I told yeh when we went ter Diagon Alley?"

Harry did.

The memory returned with the taste of melting chocolate-and-raspberry ice cream and the crowded brightness of his first day in the magical world.

"People are usually more than one thing," he said.

"Still true."

"That doesn't mean one of the things isn't bad."

"No. Means yeh decide what ter do with it."

Fang shifted closer. Harry rested his wrist across the dog's broad head.

"What if I don't know what it is?"

"Then yeh keep decidin' until yeh do."

The answer was frustratingly simple.

It was also the first one Harry had heard all week which did not make him feel contaminated.

He drank his tea. Hagrid looked towards the fire, allowing the silence to remain without trying to fill it.

After a while, Harry asked, "Do you know anything about the Chamber?"

Hagrid's chair gave a sudden creak.

"Only stories."

"Professor Binns said it was opened fifty years ago."

"Binns says plenty."

"Was anyone caught?"

Hagrid's eyes moved to the window. Outside, the snow had begun to cling to the garden fence.

"They thought they caught someone," he said.

Harry sat straighter. "Who?"

"Long time ago, that."

"That isn't an answer."

"Only one I'm givin'."

There was no anger in Hagrid's voice, but there was strain. His hands closed around his mug until it appeared dangerously small between them.

Harry considered pressing him. Hermione would have asked three more questions, each more precise than the last. Draconia would have watched until the silence became an interrogation of its own.

Instead, he said, "They were wrong, weren't they?"

Hagrid looked at him.

"About whoever they blamed."

The fire cracked. Fang lifted one ear.

Hagrid's face changed in a way Harry could not name. "People get frightened," he said. "An' frightened people like an answer more'n they care whether it's the right one."

Harry thought of the empty spaces around him at breakfast.

"Did anyone believe them?"

"A few."

"Was that enough?"

Hagrid's gaze dropped to the table. "Sometimes a few have ter be."

The words carried more history than he was willing to explain.

Harry did not ask again.

Hagrid pushed the plate of rock cakes closer. "Yeh've got people, Harry."

"I know."

"Knowin' an' rememberin' aren't always the same."

Harry picked up the smallest cake. "Is that why you keep feeding me things too hard to eat?"

"That one's fresh."

"It's dented the plate."

"Strong plate."

Harry knocked the cake lightly against the table. The wood answered.

Hagrid laughed, and this time Harry joined him.

The weather worsened in time for Saturday's Quidditch match.

Snow had stopped falling before dawn, but the clouds remained, pressing low over the castle and turning the grounds a muted mixture of white, grey and frozen green. Wind swept down from the mountains in fierce, unpredictable gusts. It rattled the shutters, dragged loose snow from the battlements and sent it twisting across the lawns in pale ribbons.

From the windows of Gryffindor Tower, Harry could see students making their way towards the pitch. Scarves streamed behind them. Red and green banners snapped above the stands, their painted lions and serpents distorted whenever the canvas folded in the wind.

Harry watched them for several minutes before Hermione pushed his gloves into his hands.

"You need to leave."

"I know."

"The team went down ten minutes ago."

"I know."

"And Wood said he would come back for you if you were late."

Harry pulled on the gloves. "That threat is why I'm considering the window."

Hermione followed his glance. "We're seven floors above the ground."

"I have a broom."

"Your broom is already in the changing room."

"Then my plan has encountered difficulties."

Hermione lifted his Quidditch robes from the bed and held them out. She had inspected every fastening that morning, repaired a loose seam near the shoulder and performed three different charms to ensure the fabric would not retain water.

Harry had stopped asking how she entered the boys' dormitory. The staircase appeared to have accepted that arguing with Hermione was unlikely to improve matters.

"You don't have to play," she said while he pulled the robes over his jumper.

He glanced at her. "You sound like Draco."

"She said the same thing?"

"Nearly."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

"And you ignored her?"

"I planned to ignore both of you equally."

"That is not fairness."

"It's the closest I can manage before breakfast."

Hermione did not smile. She had spent the week pretending not to watch him whenever students whispered in corridors, and Harry had spent the same week pretending not to notice her doing it.

"You haven't slept properly," she said. "You nearly walked into a suit of armour yesterday."

"It moved."

"It was bolted to the floor."

"The helmet turned."

"Peeves was inside it."

"That still means it moved."

Hermione tightened the scarf around his neck with unnecessary force. "If you feel ill, you land."

"I will."

"Immediately."

"Yes."

"You are saying that because you want me to stop asking."

"It's working."

She released the scarf and examined him.

Harry picked up his glasses from the bedside table. "It's only Quidditch."

Hermione's eyebrows rose.

"All right," he amended. "It's only flying at dangerous speeds while people strike iron balls at me."

"That is not better."

"It was more accurate."

They left the tower together.

The castle grew colder as they descended. Draughts curled around the staircases and slipped beneath Harry's robes. Students stood aside to let them pass, though it was difficult to tell how much of the movement came from respect for a player before a match and how much came from their reluctance to stand near him.

A group of Hufflepuffs fell silent on the second-floor landing.

Harry kept walking.

By the time they reached the entrance hall, noise from the pitch rolled faintly through the open doors. Hermione stopped beneath the stone archway.

"I'll be near the front of the Gryffindor stand."

"Where you can shout instructions?"

"Where you can see me."

The distinction caught Harry unprepared.

Hermione looked towards the students crossing the snowy lawn. "There will be a great many people watching you today."

"There usually are at matches."

"You know what I mean."

Harry did.

Quidditch had always made being watched feel different. On a broom, attention became noise rather than judgement. Nobody cared where he had grown up, what language he could speak or what the Sorting Hat had once considered. They cared whether he saw the Snitch before the other Seeker.

For an hour, that had usually been enough.

"I'll be fine," he said.

Hermione's mouth tightened. "You always say that."

"So does Draco."

"You're both irritating."

"Something else we have in common."

She hugged him before he could object.

Harry's arms rose a second too late. By the time he thought to return it properly, Hermione had already stepped away and begun walking towards the stands.

"Land if anything feels wrong," she called.

"That describes most of my life."

"Harry."

"I'll land."

She did not appear convinced, but she continued across the grounds.

Harry waited until the crowd swallowed her red-and-gold scarf before heading towards the changing rooms.

The wind struck harder once he left the shelter of the castle. It tore at his robes and carried the sharp smell of snow from the mountains. Wooden walkways groaned beneath the spectators climbing into the stands, and every gust sent a tremor through the high towers.

Inside the Gryffindor changing room, the team had gathered around Oliver Wood.

Steam rose from wet gloves arranged near the small iron stove. Seven broomsticks rested against one wall, their polished handles shining in the firelight. The room smelt of damp wool, leather polish and the sharp herbal ointment Wood insisted prevented stiff fingers.

A diagram of the pitch had been pinned above the benches. Coloured counters marked the expected positions of both teams, though several of the Slytherin pieces had acquired horns and tails in dark ink.

"We have one advantage," Wood announced as Harry entered.

Fred studied the diagram. "They're uglier?"

"That isn't an advantage," George said. "It's a public service."

Wood ignored them. He had the strained appearance of someone attempting to plan a military campaign with soldiers who found war entertaining.

"Their new brooms are faster in a straight line," he said, striking the diagram with one finger, "but they're heavier through tight turns, and this wind will punish anyone who relies entirely on speed. Angelina, Alicia and Katie, keep the Quaffle low whenever you approach their hoops. Force their Chasers to turn into the crosswind."

Angelina tightened her ponytail. "Flint will foul us before we reach them."

"Then make sure Madam Hooch sees it."

"She watched him elbow Katie in the throat last year."

"She called a penalty."

"After he elbowed me in the throat."

Wood moved quickly onwards. "Fred and George, I want the Bludgers kept high whenever Gryffindor has possession."

Fred lifted his bat. "And when Slytherin has possession?"

"Hit Flint."

"We've been waiting for that order," Fred said.

"Happy to help," George added.

Wood turned to Harry.

"And you keep Malfoy away from the Snitch."

Harry sat on the bench to fasten the guards around his shins. "How?"

"You're the Seeker. Work it out."

"That's very useful."

"I have faith in you."

"That makes one of us."

"You have beaten her before."

"I've also lost to her before."

Nobody in the changing room suggested Draconia held her position because of the brooms Lucius had bought the Slytherin team. She had earned the place before the Nimbus Two Thousand Ones arrived and spent enough time practising to make accusations of favouritism sound foolish. Harry knew exactly how good she was because she had spent part of the summer proving it to him.

Draconia possessed none of his instinctive recklessness in the air. She flew the way she approached most things: precisely, efficiently and with the expectation that preparation ought to defeat improvisation. Harry had won their first race around the Manor grounds by flying through a gap between two trees she considered too narrow.

She had won the next four by anticipating that he would attempt it again.

Wood moved the counters across the board. "Malfoy prefers the northern half of the pitch because the sun usually sits behind her there."

Harry glanced towards the grey window. "What sun?"

"The principle remains."

A knock sounded against the changing-room door.

Nobody answered.

It opened anyway.

Draconia stood at the threshold in green-and-silver Quidditch robes. Her hair had been braided tightly away from her face, and the loose end rested against one shoulder. A dark leather guard encircled her left wrist, covering the faint marks Harry had noticed in Potions.

Several Gryffindor players reached instinctively for their brooms.

Draconia surveyed the room. "Relax. If I intended to steal your strategy, I would find one worth taking."

"We're discussing how best to hit Flint," Fred told her.

"I withdraw my criticism."

From the Slytherin corridor came Marcus Flint's muffled shout.

"Malfoy!"

Draconia ignored it. Her attention found Harry at the back of the room.

"I need to speak to you."

Wood stepped between them. "The match begins in twelve minutes."

"Then I shall return him in eleven."

"You will return him now."

Harry collected his broom from the wall. "I'll meet you outside."

Wood opened his mouth.

"It's either that or she stands there until Flint comes looking."

As if summoned by the suggestion, another roar came from the corridor.

"MALFOY!"

George peered around Draconia. "He sounds friendly."

"He has a limited emotional range," she replied.

Wood pointed at Harry. "Eight minutes."

"You said twelve."

"You spent four arguing."

"That seems unfair."

"Seven."

Harry followed Draconia into the corridor before Wood began counting aloud.

She led him beyond the changing rooms and around the rear of the stands, where the rising structure sheltered them from the worst of the wind. Canvas banners snapped overhead. Hundreds of boots pounded the platforms as students found their seats, shaking frost and old dust through the gaps between the boards.

Draconia stopped beside a support beam.

"You shouldn't play."

Harry rested his broom against one shoulder. "That's an inspiring beginning."

"I'm serious."

"So is Wood. That's why he's terrifying."

"You haven't slept properly in days. Half the school is trying to decide whether you're a murderer, and yesterday you nearly walked into a suit of armour."

"It moved."

"It was bolted to the floor."

"Hermione has already made that point."

"Then listen to one of us."

Harry studied her.

The cold had sharpened the colour in her cheeks, but it could not hide the exhaustion around her eyes. Draconia looked better than she had in the library, though better had become a worryingly flexible word. She held herself with her usual control, yet the hand resting against her broom flexed repeatedly around the handle.

"Did Hermione send you?" he asked.

"No."

"Has she trained you?"

Draconia's mouth tightened. "I am capable of worrying independently."

"I'm fine."

"You always say that when you aren't."

"You say it too."

"That is different."

"How?"

"That sounds more promising."

"It was enchanted."

"We know," Hermione replied. "Bludgers do not select one player and ignore everyone else."

"Someone tampered with it before the match."

"Flint?" Harry asked.

Draconia's eyes narrowed. "Flint would have told half the common room by now. He can't resist boasting."

"Someone wanted Harry injured," Hermione said.

"Not necessarily killed," Draconia replied. "If murder were the intention, there are easier methods than attracting an entire stadium's attention."

Harry looked between them. "It tried very hard to disagree."

"The pattern matters," Hermione said. "It chased you constantly, but most attacks came from angles that would force you towards the ground. Whoever enchanted it may have wanted you removed from the match."

"Or from Hogwarts," Harry said.

The words silenced them. All three thought of the same small figure, the same enormous green eyes and the same warning delivered in Harry's bedroom during the summer.

Hermione shut the textbook.

"Dobby."

Draconia's gaze moved towards the hospital-wing doors. "He has not returned to the Manor."

"Your mother said the other elves didn't know where he was."

"They claimed not to know. House-elves are capable of avoiding a direct answer if they believe it protects their family."

"Could he enchant a Bludger?" Harry asked.

"House-elves can perform magic wizards cannot. Whether Dobby could interfere with school equipment depends upon the protections around it."

Hermione leant closer. "You said he was unusually powerful."

"All our elves are powerful."

"That sounded like boasting."

"It was."

Madam Pomfrey's footsteps approached.

Hermione reopened the textbook with remarkable speed. Draconia placed the essay parchment across her lap.

The matron inspected Harry's arm and ordered both girls to leave before the bones began growing in earnest.

Hermione reluctantly gathered her books.

"We'll return first thing tomorrow."

"With food," Draconia added.

"The hospital wing provides food."

"Harry dislikes porridge."

"I can speak," Harry said.

Neither acknowledged him.

Hermione tucked the blanket more firmly around his shoulders. Draconia rose, but remained beside the bed after Hermione had stepped away.

"Land next time," she said.

"Catch the Snitch next time."

"I was occupied preventing your funeral."

"You still lost."

Her eyes flashed.

There she was.

Harry smiled.

Draconia squeezed his uninjured hand once, quick enough that he might have imagined it, then followed Hermione from the ward.

The pain began again shortly after midnight.

It went as a deep pressure inside Harry's shoulder and spread downwards, branching through flesh which had contained nothing an hour before. Every new inch of bone brought a grinding ache. Sweat dampened his hair despite the cold ward, and the blankets trapped too much heat against his skin.

Madam Pomfrey had given him a sleeping potion. It had not worked.

Harry counted the ceiling beams. He counted footsteps in the corridor, panes in the nearest window and breaths between the clock's slow chimes. When none of that helped, he imagined Hermione explaining the biological processes involved in bone growth until the invented lecture became more painful than his arm.

A floorboard creaked.

Harry opened his eyes.

Moonlight stretched across the empty ward. Privacy screens stood folded against the wall, and glass jars shone faintly inside the locked medicine cabinet.

A narrow shape moved at the foot of his bed.

Harry reached for his wand.

"Harry Potter must not be frightened."

He froze.

Dobby emerged from the shadow between the beds.

The elf wore the same stained pillowcase Harry remembered from Privet Drive. His enormous eyes reflected the moonlight, and both ears drooped almost to his shoulders. A woollen sock had been tied around one hand like a bandage.

Harry pushed himself upright. Pain surged through his new bones.

"Dobby."

"Please, sir, not loudly."

"Where have you been?"

Dobby twisted his bandaged fingers. "Dobby has been watching."

"Watching what?"

"Harry Potter."

"That isn't comforting."

The elf flinched.

Harry lowered his voice. "Did you enchant the Bludger?"

Dobby's ears sank further.

The answer was written across his entire body.

"You broke my arm."

"Dobby meant only to injure Harry Potter enough to send him home!"

"You removed the bones too?"

"No, sir. That was the smiling wizard."

"At least we agree on something."

Dobby's eyes filled with tears. "Dobby is very sorry. Dobby thought if Harry Potter fell, Hogwarts would send him away. Dobby tried the letters. Dobby gave warnings. Dobby went to the station to close the barrier, but Harry Potter's friends came with their parents. Dobby could not keep them all away."

"You were at King's Cross?"

Dobby nodded miserably. Harry remembered standing alone on the Muggle side for a moment, everyone he wanted to follow already through the wall. He had never known there was someone else watching.

"And you stopped our letters all summer."

Another nod.

Harry's anger came slowly.

It had spent too long buried beneath other feelings: the empty windowsill at Privet Drive, the weeks when no owls came and the quiet certainty that Hermione and Draconia had forgotten him. Dobby's interference had given Uncle Vernon the excuse to lock him behind bars. It had turned the magical world silent when Harry needed proof that it still existed.

"You made me think they didn't want me."

Dobby's face crumpled. "Dobby did not understand."

"No. You didn't."

"Harry Potter has friends who came for him. Dobby did not expect—" He stopped, hands flying over his mouth.

Harry watched him.

"You didn't expect them to come because you don't think people come back?"

The elf's fingers trembled.

Harry understood more of that than he wanted to.

The anger remained, but its edges changed.

"Did the Malfoys tell you to do any of this?"

Dobby shook his head so violently that his ears struck his cheeks.

"Master Lucius does not know. Mistress Narcissa does not know. Young Mistress must never—"

He clapped both hands over his mouth again.

"Draconia already knows you warned me."

Dobby swayed.

"She knows you stopped the letters too," Harry continued. "Your family spent half the summer looking for you."

"Family," Dobby whispered.

"Yes. Yours."

The elf's knees buckled. He caught the bedframe before falling.

"Dobby cannot return."

"Why?"

A thin, strained noise escaped him.

Harry had heard that sound in his bedroom—the noise Dobby made when magic and fear closed around an answer.

"You're not allowed to tell me."

Dobby's eyes pleaded.

"Is someone at the Manor responsible for what's happening?"

"No!"

The answer came at once.

Harry believed it.

"Did something come from the Manor?"

Dobby seized the water jug from the bedside table and raised it over his head.

Harry caught it with his good hand.

"No punishing yourself."

"Dobby spoke too near."

"You barely spoke at all."

"Too near," Dobby repeated desperately.

Harry lowered the jug to the floor. "Narcissa said you wouldn't be punished for trying to prevent harm."

Dobby stared at him.

"She made Lucius promise."

The elf's eyes overflowed.

"Dobby does not deserve Mistress's promise."

"That seems like her decision."

A bell sounded somewhere in the castle.

Dobby turned towards the doors.

"Harry Potter must leave Hogwarts."

"I can't."

"The danger did not end when Harry Potter returned. It has entered the school. It is close to people Harry Potter loves."

Harry's new fingers curled painfully against the blanket. "What is it?"

Dobby backed away.

"Tell me."

"Dobby cannot."

"Who opened the Chamber?"

The elf's whole body went rigid.

Harry heard it then: footsteps beyond the ward, quick and uneven.

Dobby snapped his fingers.

The water jug vanished from the floor. The curtains lifted around the bed, though no wind had entered. By the time the doors opened, only a shallow depression remained where the elf's feet had pressed into the rug.

A small figure slipped into the hospital wing.

"Harry?"

Colin Creevey stood between the beds, wearing striped pyjamas beneath his school robes. His camera hung from his neck. Under one arm he carried a bowl containing three oranges and a bruised apple.

Harry sank back against the pillows. "Colin?"

"I heard you were here."

"So did Madam Pomfrey. She put me in a bed."

Colin hurried closer. "I wanted to visit, but Percy said nobody was allowed in after eight, and then I thought I could come when everyone was asleep."

"That is usually why rules specify a time."

"I brought fruit."

He placed the bowl on the bedside cabinet with the solemnity of an offering.

Harry eyed the apple. "Did you steal that from dinner?"

"I saved it."

"It has teeth marks."

"That was before I decided to save it."

Harry moved his arm and immediately regretted it.

Colin dragged the chair closer. "Does growing bones hurt?"

"No. I'm sweating for entertainment."

"Can I see?"

"My arm?"

"The bones."

"They're inside."

"Oh." Colin appeared disappointed. "Could I take a photograph in the morning?"

"Why?"

"To remember it."

"I'm trying not to."

Colin laughed more loudly than the joke deserved, then clapped a hand over his mouth.

"Sorry."

Harry glanced towards Madam Pomfrey's office. No light appeared beneath the door.

"What are you doing wandering around with your camera?"

Colin lifted it. "I thought I saw something in the corridor."

The last trace of humour left Harry.

"What?"

"I don't know. It moved past the corner."

"Did you follow it?"

"Only a little."

"Colin."

"I didn't see anything."

Harry pushed himself upright again. "Go straight back to your dormitory."

"But—"

"Now."

Colin blinked at the sharpness in his voice.

Harry softened it with effort. "Take the main staircase. Stay where the torches are. If you hear anything, don't follow it. Find a professor."

"What would I hear?"

"I don't know."

That was the worst part.

Colin hesitated, then nodded. "All right."

He reached the doors before turning.

"Gryffindor won, by the way."

"I know."

"You caught the Snitch with a broken arm."

"Yes."

Colin's grin returned. "Brilliant."

The doors closed behind him.

Harry listened to his footsteps fade.

Sleep remained impossible.

Dobby's warning circled his thoughts. It is close to people Harry Potter loves.

The new bones pressed against his skin. Wind moved faintly around the tower, making the windows rattle in their frames.

Then, from the corridor below, came a distant crash.

Harry threw back the blankets.

His feet touched the floor before pain drove him against the bedpost. He dragged his wand from beneath the pillow.

The hospital-wing doors opened.

Professor McGonagall entered backwards, guiding one end of a stretcher. Dumbledore held the other. Madam Pomfrey emerged from her office in a dressing gown, wand already lit.

A small body lay between them.

The camera came first.

It hung over the side of the stretcher, the lens cracked and the metal casing twisted. Smoke rose from the opening at its back.

Harry stopped breathing.

Colin lay rigid beneath his striped pyjamas. His eyes were wide, fixed upon whatever he had seen in the corridor. One hand remained curled around the camera strap.

"Move back, Potter," Madam Pomfrey ordered.

"Is he—"

"Back."

Harry obeyed.

They placed Colin upon the nearest bed. Madam Pomfrey bent over him, casting spells in rapid succession. Blue light passed across his body and vanished into his chest.

Dumbledore lifted the camera carefully.

He opened the back.

A foul smell filled the ward. The film had melted into a smoking black mass.

"He has been Petrified," Madam Pomfrey said.

McGonagall closed her eyes.

Harry gripped the bedframe.

Dumbledore turned.

His gaze rested upon Harry's face, then upon the wand in his hand.

"Did you hear anything tonight, Harry?"

"No, sir."

The answer felt like a failure.

Dumbledore studied him for another second. "Did you see Mr Creevey before he was found?"

"He came here. He said he thought he saw something moving in the corridor."

"When?"

"Only a few minutes ago. I told him to go back."

McGonagall's face tightened, but Dumbledore remained calm.

"You did correctly."

"Not correctly enough."

"Harry," he said, "you did not cause what waited outside this room."

Dobby's warning seemed to echo behind the words.

Harry looked at Colin's camera.

The glass lens had cracked from the inside.

Morning entered the hospital wing slowly.

At first, there was only a thin strip of grey light beneath the curtains. It widened across the stone floor as dawn reached the high windows, catching upon the rims of potion bottles and turning the ward's white sheets faintly blue. Frost obscured the lower panes, and beyond them the castle roofs lay beneath fresh snow. The wind had weakened during the night, leaving Hogwarts wrapped in a silence broken only by the crackle of the stove and the occasional clink of glass from Madam Pomfrey's office.

Harry had not slept enough to appreciate any of it.

The pain from the Skele-Gro had finally subsided shortly before sunrise, retreating from the grinding pressure of the night into a deep ache that followed every movement. His arm had returned in full. It lay outside the blankets, pale and tender, the skin stretched over thirty-three new bones which had not existed the previous evening. After hours of feeling each one rebuild itself, Harry thought he ought to have been introduced to them individually.

Madam Pomfrey had no interest in ceremony.

She stood beside his bed, holding his wrist in one hand and bending each finger with the other. Her dressing gown had been replaced by immaculate healer's robes, though the shadows beneath her eyes suggested Colin's arrival had cost her as much sleep as it had Harry.

"Move your thumb."

Harry moved it.

"Again."

He obeyed.

"Make a fist."

His fingers curled slowly. Stiffness caught in the joints, and the newly formed muscles trembled with the effort, but the hand closed.

Madam Pomfrey inspected it from several angles. "Tenderness?"

"Everywhere."

"Sharp pain?"

"Only when you do that."

"That is why I'm doing it."

She pressed the inside of his wrist again.

Harry winced. "Still sharp."

"Good."

"How is that good?"

"It means the nerves have regrown correctly."

"I liked them better when they were quiet."

Madam Pomfrey ignored him. She produced a narrow wooden spoon from the pocket of her robes and placed it in his hand.

"Lift."

Harry attempted to raise it. The spoon wobbled several inches above the blanket before his arm began trembling and dropped back to the mattress.

"There," Madam Pomfrey said, apparently satisfied. "Physically capable of leaving after breakfast."

"I can barely lift a spoon."

"You are leaving a hospital, not competing in a weightlifting tournament. Avoid Quidditch, duelling and incompetent celebrities for forty-eight hours."

"The last one may be difficult. He teaches here."

"Then run."

"With the new arm?"

"With whichever limbs Professor Lockhart has not removed."

She reclaimed the spoon and crossed to the far side of the ward.

Her shoes made little noise against the polished floor. The soft tread faded behind a row of beds before a curtain whispered along its rail.

Harry turned his head.

Screens had been drawn around Colin's bed, enclosing it on every side. They concealed the boy himself, but the gap beneath the nearest curtain revealed the toes of his trainers and one untied lace resting against the floor. Neither had moved since Professor Dumbledore carried him into the ward.

The memory of the previous night returned in fragments: Colin's striped pyjamas, the bowl of stolen fruit and the cheerful certainty with which he had called Harry's fall brilliant. Less than ten minutes later, the same small body had lain rigid upon a stretcher while smoke rose from the ruined camera.

Harry could still smell the melted film.

The odour had worked into the clean air of the hospital wing, bitter beneath the sharper scents of healing potions and disinfectant. Every time it reached him, he remembered the crack in the camera lens and Colin's wide, unseeing eyes.

Madam Pomfrey did not tell Harry how he was doing.

She did not need to.

Footsteps approached outside the ward. They were hurried, uneven and accompanied by two voices conducting separate conversations through the closed doors.

The doors opened hard enough to strike the wall.

Hermione entered first with four books pressed against her chest and a fifth wedged precariously beneath her chin. Cold air followed her into the ward, lifting several loose pages from Madam Pomfrey's desk. Draconia came immediately behind her, carrying a wicker basket covered by a dark blue cloth. Snow had melted across the shoulders of her robes, and her pale hair remained slightly damp around her face.

Both began speaking before the doors swung closed.

"Are the bones back?"

"Did you sleep?"

"Can you move your fingers?"

"What happened to Creevey?"

Harry raised his restored hand. The movement required more effort than he allowed his face to show.

"In order: yes, no, mostly, and he was Petrified."

Hermione stopped so suddenly that the top book slid from her pile. Draconia caught it against the basket before it reached the floor.

Neither girl spoke.

Harry lowered his hand.

Hermione placed the remaining books on his bedside cabinet. Their weight made the water glass tremble. "Petrified?"

"He came here after midnight," Harry said. "He brought fruit."

Hermione's eyes moved to the bowl on the neighbouring table. The three oranges remained untouched, arranged around the apple with Colin's bite missing from one side.

"He came to visit you?"

"He heard about my arm. He left a few minutes before they found him."

Draconia's attention had gone to the curtained bed.

The basket remained clutched against her body. "Where?"

"The corridor below the ward."

"Was he alone?"

Harry nodded. "He said he thought he saw something moving and followed it."

Hermione turned back to him. "He saw the attacker?"

"I don't know. His camera was destroyed. The glass cracked and the film inside melted."

She went very still.

Harry recognised the change. It was not fear disappearing, exactly, but fear being forced aside while her mind rearranged what she knew. Hermione looked towards Colin's bed once more, then pulled the nearest chair closer without sitting.

"The camera was around his neck?"

"Yes."

"Was the lens facing forwards?"

"I think so."

"And his eyes were open?"

"Hermione," Draconia said quietly.

"I need to understand."

"So do I, but he is not evidence on a desk."

Hermione flinched.

Regret replaced the sharp concentration in her face. She looked towards the curtains again. "I know."

Harry rested his new arm across the blankets. "Dumbledore opened the camera. The film had melted completely."

Hermione's gaze dropped to the floor between the beds.

"When Mrs Norris was attacked, there was water in the corridor," she said.

"Yes."

"From Moaning Myrtle's bathroom?"

"Probably. The whole passage was flooded."

"And Mrs Norris was hanging above it."

"From the torch bracket."

"But which direction was she facing?"

Harry tried to reconstruct the corridor. He remembered the dark shape ahead of them, Hermione's hand closing around his sleeve and the cat's eyes reflecting the flames.

"Towards us," he said. "Towards the water."

Hermione reached into her bag, discovered she had left it by the doors and crossed the ward to retrieve it. Parchment appeared first, followed by an ink bottle and the notebook containing their list of attacks.

Draconia set the basket at the foot of Harry's bed. "You have been here for less than two minutes."

"That does not make the question less important."

"You brought him five books."

"Three concern dark creatures. One is about defensive enchantments, and the last is his Transfiguration text."

Harry stared at the pile. "You brought homework?"

"You missed Friday's assignment."

"I lost an arm."

"It has returned."

"I'm beginning to regret that."

Hermione opened her notebook. Beside the entry for Mrs Norris, she added several lines in small, rapid handwriting.

Draconia lifted the blue cloth from the basket. Warm air carrying the smell of cinnamon and fresh bread escaped into the ward.

Harry leant slightly closer. "What did you bring?"

"Fruit."

He looked inside.

Two bread rolls, three sugared pastries, a small treacle tart and several slices of ham had been arranged around a single apple.

"Some of it is shaped unusually," Draconia added.

Hermione paid no attention. She had drawn a line between two sections of her notes.

"The water could have reflected whatever attacked Mrs Norris," she said. "Not clearly, but enough for her to see it."

Harry looked towards Colin's bed. "And Colin had the camera."

"The lens is curved glass."

"Most cameras have those," Draconia said while transferring the food to the bedside table.

"That is the point. Neither victim necessarily looked directly at the attacker."

Harry tried to follow the connection. Exhaustion made the thought slippery.

"Why does that matter?"

"I don't know yet," Hermione admitted. "But it may explain why they were Petrified instead of killed."

The ward seemed to grow colder around them.

Draconia's hands stopped above the basket. "Instead of killed?"

"If the Chamber's monster is meant to purge the school, these attacks are strangely ineffective. Mrs Norris and Colin are alive. That may not be what was intended."

Harry glanced once more at the curtains.

Colin's trainers remained motionless beneath them.

Being alive did not look particularly reassuring from here.

The door to Madam Pomfrey's office opened.

Hermione closed the notebook so quickly that a corner of parchment remained trapped between the pages. Draconia dropped the blue cloth over the food, concealing everything except one bread roll.

Madam Pomfrey emerged carrying a tray. A bowl of porridge steamed beside a glass containing a thick purple restorative.

Her gaze travelled from Hermione's books to Draconia's basket and then to Harry's face.

"Investigation is not an approved hospital activity."

"We were discussing his recovery," Hermione said.

"With a book entitled Serpents of the Ancient World?"

Hermione turned the top book over.

Madam Pomfrey placed the tray across Harry's lap. "Potter will eat his breakfast. You will refrain from exciting him, interrogating him or encouraging him to leave this bed before I permit it."

"I'm sitting here," Harry said.

"You have demonstrated an unusual talent for becoming injured without changing location."

She examined the uncovered bread roll.

Draconia pulled the cloth over it.

Madam Pomfrey pointed towards her office. "I shall be within earshot."

They waited until the door closed.

Hermione counted to ten.

Draconia uncovered the basket again.

Harry stirred the porridge with his newly regrown hand. The spoon felt heavier than it had during Madam Pomfrey's examination, but he managed to lift it without dropping anything onto the blankets.

"There's something else," he said.

Hermione's notebook reopened immediately.

"No list yet."

Her quill stopped above the page.

Harry lowered his voice. "Dobby came here last night."

Draconia's head snapped towards him.

The colour drained from her face so quickly that Harry regretted not finding a gentler way to say it.

"Dobby was here?"

"At the end of my bed."

"Where is he now?"

"I don't know. He disappeared when Colin came in."

Draconia moved into the chair beside him. The covered basket was forgotten.

"Was he hurt?"

"One hand was bandaged. I don't know what happened to it."

"Did he say where he has been?"

"He said he's been watching me."

Hermione shifted onto the edge of the neighbouring bed. "Since the summer?"

"I think so."

"Why?"

"The same reason he stopped the letters. He wants me to leave Hogwarts."

Draconia leant forwards, her hands clasped so tightly that the knuckles whitened. "What exactly did he say?"

Harry began with the rogue Bludger.

He told them how Dobby admitted enchanting it, believing an injury would force the school to send Harry home. Hermione's face moved from shock to anger while Draconia became unnaturally still. By the time Harry reached the elf's warning, she was no longer looking at him. Her attention had fixed upon the folds of the blue cloth covering the basket.

"He said the danger had entered Hogwarts," Hermione repeated.

"Something close to that."

"Close how?"

"He said it didn't end when I came back. That it was inside the school and near people I care about."

Hermione's quill remained suspended above the parchment. "Those exact words?"

"Nearly. I had spent most of the night growing an arm."

"This matters, Harry."

"I know."

Draconia had not moved.

Harry watched her fingers close around the edge of the cloth.

"And he denied that your parents ordered any of it," he told her. "Straight away. I asked whether someone at the Manor was responsible, and he said no before I had finished."

"I know they aren't responsible."

The force of her answer carried across the ward.

Madam Pomfrey's office remained closed.

Harry kept his voice quieter. "I believed him."

Draconia's shoulders lowered slightly.

"He would not lie about that," she said. "He might avoid answering. House-elves can twist instructions until the words become almost useless, but he would not falsely accuse the family to protect himself."

"He tried to punish himself when I asked whether something came from the Manor."

Draconia's gaze lifted.

Hermione stopped writing.

"What did you ask?" Draconia said.

"Whether the danger came from your house."

"And what did Dobby say?"

"Nothing. He grabbed the water jug and tried to hit himself with it."

Draconia looked towards the windows.

The winter light revealed every sign of exhaustion she had been trying to conceal. There were shadows beneath her eyes, and a faint tremor moved through the hand resting upon her knee. She appeared to be searching the snowy grounds for an answer the castle could not provide.

"He said he couldn't return," Harry continued.

Pain flickered across her face. "He cannot, or he will not?"

"I don't know."

"There is a difference."

"He wasn't able to explain."

Draconia drew a careful breath. "Mother bound every house-elf to the Manor's protection. If Dobby believes returning would endanger the family, the magic may prevent him from entering. He may also believe that disobeying us has broken his service beyond forgiveness."

"Narcissa promised he wouldn't be punished," Harry said. "I told him."

Draconia's eyes closed briefly.

"Did he believe you?"

"I think he wanted to."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

"Dobby does not always understand kindness when it is offered," she said. "He understands instructions, punishment and service. Kindness has usually made him suspicious."

Harry remembered the elf's face when he called the Malfoys Dobby's family. He remembered how the word appeared to frighten him more than any threat of punishment.

"I don't think he knows what to do with it," Harry said.

Draconia looked down at her hands. "Neither do most people."

Hermione's attention moved between them, but she did not ask what Draconia meant.

Instead, she closed the notebook and rested it on her lap. "Harry asked whether something came from the Manor."

"Yes," Harry said. "Dobby couldn't answer."

Draconia reached instinctively for the green satchel beside her chair.

The movement lasted less than a second.

It was enough.

Harry's gaze dropped to her hand.

Draconia realised what she had done and withdrew it, but her fingers remained close to the clasp.

"You know something," Harry said.

Her face closed. "There are things at the Manor that shouldn't be anywhere near a school."

"What sort of objects?" Hermione asked.

"Old ones."

"That is not a category."

"It is when one's family refuses to throw anything away."

Harry set the spoon beside his bowl. "The notebook."

Draconia's fingers touched the satchel again.

"Is irrelevant."

The answer came too quickly, spoken with the clean certainty of a sentence prepared before anyone asked the question.

Hermione and Harry exchanged a glance.

Draconia saw it.

"Do not."

"We didn't say anything," Harry replied.

"You made the face."

"What face?"

"The one you both make when silently agreeing that I am lying."

Hermione folded her arms. "Are you?"

"No."

"Draco."

"What would the notebook have to do with a Bludger? Or an elf who ran away before term even began?"

"That wasn't the question," Harry said.

Her eyes met his.

Beyond the high windows, snow slipped from one of the roofs and fell in a glittering sheet. Its shadow passed over Draconia's face, briefly darkening her features before the winter light returned. Harry saw the strain in the line of her mouth and the fine tremor she could not entirely suppress.

This was not the irritation she usually employed when avoiding a subject. Draconia was frightened.

The discovery did not ease Harry's suspicion. It made it worse.

"What happened to your wrist?" he asked.

Her hand disappeared into the fold of her robes. "Nothing."

"You were wearing that guard before the match."

"It prevents strain."

"You have bruises beneath it."

Draconia's chair shifted backwards. "You were unconscious from pain for most of last night. Your observations may be unreliable."

"I wasn't unconscious. That was the problem."

Hermione lowered her voice. "If the notebook has done anything unusual—"

"It hasn't."

"Then show it to us."

Draconia looked towards her satchel.

For one suspended second, Harry believed she might open it. Her hand rose, hovered over the clasp and curled into a fist instead.

The curtains around Colin's bed moved.

All three turned.

Madam Pomfrey stepped through the opening and drew the fabric closed behind her. Her face disclosed nothing about what she had found inside.

"You are finished," she announced. "Potter may leave once he has eaten more than three spoonfuls of porridge. Miss Granger, if those books concern dark creatures, remove them from my ward. Miss Malfoy, whatever you have concealed beneath that cloth had better not contain sugar."

Draconia pulled the blue covering firmly over the basket.

"It is fruit."

Madam Pomfrey lifted one eyebrow.

"Some of it is shaped unusually."

"Out."

Hermione gathered her books, arranging them into a pile which immediately began sliding sideways. Draconia rose and reached for the green satchel.

Harry caught the sleeve of her robes.

The weakness in his new hand prevented him from gripping very tightly, but Draconia stopped.

Her gaze dropped to his fingers.

"You nearly fell off your broom for me yesterday," he said quietly. "You don't get to decide that we only worry in one direction."

Her throat moved.

Around them, morning light filled the hospital wing. It shone across the polished floor, the untouched fruit beside Colin's bed and the metal clasp of the satchel hanging from Draconia's hand.

"I am handling it," she said.

Harry released her sleeve.

"You sound like me."

Draconia stood beside his bed with the answer she would usually have produced nowhere in reach.

For once, she had no reply.

Hogwarts responded to Colin's attack by becoming a prison.

Students were forbidden from walking the corridors alone. Professors escorted groups between lessons, and prefects patrolled every landing after dinner. The castle doors were locked before sunset. Even the ghosts had been asked to report unusual activity, a request Peeves treated as permission to invent it.

The restrictions made Harry safer and more visible.

Every journey through the school now occurred in a cluster. If he entered a classroom, twenty students saw him enter. If he left, twenty students watched him go. Conversations bent around him like water around stone.

Justin Finch-Fletchley continued to avoid him.

Ernie Macmillan no longer bothered whispering. Harry heard the words Dark wizard outside Charms and Slytherin's heir near the greenhouses. When Hermione rounded on him, Ernie claimed he was merely considering the evidence.

"The evidence," Hermione repeated, "is that Harry stopped a snake from attacking Justin."

"The evidence is that he spoke to it."

"Yes. In order to stop it."

"How do we know that's what he said?"

"Because the snake stopped."

Ernie's face flushed. "He could have been ordering it to wait."

"For what?"

"A better opportunity."

Harry touched Hermione's arm. "Leave it."

"He's accusing you of plotting snake appointments."

"He'll be disappointed when I don't attend."

Ernie moved away.

Hermione watched him go. "You cannot keep making jokes whenever someone says something cruel."

"Why not?"

"Because it lets them believe it doesn't hurt."

Harry shoved his hands into his pockets. "Why should I want them to know that?"

Hermione fell silent.

Draconia waited a short distance away beneath an arched window. She had been granted permission to accompany the Gryffindors to Charms after informing Professor Snape that the alternative was allowing Harry to be murdered between lessons.

Whether Snape believed this or merely wanted five minutes without arguing was unclear.

When Harry and Hermione reached her, Draconia said, "Macmillan is an idiot."

"Hermione already gave the longer version."

"She usually does."

They continued down the corridor.

Hermione carried a list of every known detail about the attacks. She had divided the parchment into categories: victims, locations, timing, physical evidence and unknowns. Voice in walls had been underlined three times. Beside water and camera, she had drawn a line connecting the two.

"What are you thinking?" Harry asked.

"That the Chamber's monster should have killed them."

Draconia glanced at her. "A cheerful observation."

"Professor Binns said the creature was intended to purge the school. Mrs Norris and Colin were not killed. Either the story is wrong, the attacks are failing or petrification is a secondary effect."

"Secondary to what?" Harry asked.

"That is what I'm trying to discover."

They entered Charms with the rest of the escorted group.

Professor Flitwick had arranged cushions around the room for their Cushioning Charm lesson. Under normal circumstances, the sight of Dean Thomas rebounding from the ceiling would have entertained Harry for the remainder of the afternoon.

Instead, he watched the walls.

He had begun listening whenever the castle grew quiet. He listened beneath the scrape of chairs, the crackle of fires and the whisper of robes. Pipes ran through the stone in every direction. He had never noticed them before. Now each knock of old metal drew his attention.

Hermione noticed that too.

After class, she stopped beside a brass heating pipe.

"You said the voice moved through the walls."

"Yes."

"Could it have been inside them?"

Harry placed his palm against the pipe. It vibrated faintly with running water.

"The walls aren't hollow."

"They contain plumbing."

Draconia went still.

Harry turned to her. "What?"

"Nothing."

"You keep saying that."

"Sometimes it's even true."

Hermione stepped closer. "What sort of creature could travel through pipes?"

"Something small," Harry said.

The voice returned to him: low, ancient and hungry. He thought of the way it had stretched through the stone.

"It didn't sound small."

Hermione looked towards the nearest window. Outside, snow covered the grounds in an unbroken sheet.

"A ghost?"

"Ghosts don't need pipes."

"A spirit?"

"Would a spirit Petrify a cat?"

Draconia adjusted the satchel on her shoulder. "You need to stop."

Hermione blinked. "What?"

"This investigation. Stop it."

The corridor was nearly empty. Professor Flitwick had left with the students, and the next escorted group had not yet arrived.

Harry studied Draconia's face. "Why?"

"Because you are attracting attention."

"We attracted attention when Mrs Norris was found above our heads," Hermione said.

"You are asking questions about a creature that has already attacked twice."

"That is generally when questions become useful."

"It is when they become dangerous."

Hermione folded the parchment. "Do you know what the creature is?"

"No."

"Do you know how it's moving through the school?"

"No."

"Do you know who opened the Chamber?"

Draconia's breathing changed.

Only slightly, but Harry heard it.

Hermione heard it too.

"Draco," she said, much more softly.

Draconia looked past them towards the staircase.

A line of students appeared on the landing below, led by Professor Sprout. Their conversation rose through the corridor.

Draconia took one step backwards.

"If you continue," she said, "one of you will be next."

Harry felt cold move across his skin.

"How do you know?"

"I don't."

"You just said—"

"I said there have been two attacks. Continuing to search for whatever caused them increases the possibility of a third."

"That isn't what you meant," Hermione said.

Draconia's fingers closed around the satchel.

"Leave it alone."

She joined the approaching group before either of them could stop her.

Harry watched her disappear down the stairs.

Hermione stood beside him, the parchment crushed between her fingers.

"She's frightened," Harry said.

"She's hiding something."

"Both can be true."

Hermione smoothed the list against her robes. "We cannot stop."

"I know."

"If she knows enough to warn us—"

"I know."

Hermione looked at him. "You're worried about her."

"Aren't you?"

"Of course I am."

The admission made her angry, though not at him. Hermione folded the parchment into careful quarters and slipped it into her bag.

"That's why we need the answer."

The answer did not arrive all at once.

It accumulated across three days of stolen conversations, hurried notes and books read beneath the supervision of increasingly suspicious professors. Curfew prevented Harry and Hermione from searching the castle freely, while the rule forbidding students from walking alone turned every journey into an escorted procession. They learnt to exchange theories on staircases, compare observations during lessons and leave questions written inside library books for the other to find later.

Hermione began with the victims.

She devoted two pages of her notebook to Mrs Norris and Colin Creevey, recording where each had been discovered, what they had carried and which direction they had apparently been facing. Her handwriting became smaller whenever space ran short, spreading into the margins until arrows and annotations surrounded the original list.

Mrs Norris had been found in a flooded corridor with her eyes directed towards the water beneath her. Colin had carried a camera whose lens cracked and whose film melted. Neither victim had necessarily seen the attacker directly.

Or, Hermione insisted, neither had needed to.

She moved next to the voice.

Harry described it so many times that the words began to feel inadequate. It was rough without being human, ancient without sounding weak and close even when it travelled somewhere beyond the walls. It spoke of hunger, tearing and killing. The sound moved without footsteps and vanished whenever Harry reached the place where it should have been.

He could understand it.

Nobody else could.

"Parseltongue," Hermione said on the third evening.

The library had emptied around them. Curfew had reduced the usual crowd to a handful of students permitted to study beneath Madam Pince's direct supervision. Green-shaded lamps cast small pools of light across the tables, leaving the spaces between the shelves dark. Snow brushed the windows in soft bursts, vanishing against the glass before beginning again.

Harry and Hermione occupied a corner behind the Magical Creatures shelves. Books surrounded them in precarious stacks, some borrowed from the ordinary collection and others obtained after Hermione spent twenty minutes persuading Professor McGonagall that researching defensive magic did not constitute an attempt to hunt the Chamber's monster.

Madam Pince had not believed this explanation.

She had allowed them to remain anyway, provided every book returned to its proper shelf and no pages were damaged in the pursuit of what she called their "inevitable detention."

Harry leant over a hand-drawn map of the castle. Hermione had copied every pipe marked in Hogwarts: A Plumbing History, a book so relentlessly detailed that Harry suspected it had never before been opened voluntarily.

"What about Parseltongue?" he asked.

"The voice appeared before Mrs Norris was attacked. You heard it moving through the corridor, and I heard nothing."

"I remember."

"You are a Parselmouth."

Harry's hand stopped above the map. Even after several days, the word carried the echo of students retreating from him in the Duelling Club.

"You think it was a snake."

"I think we ought to check."

"A snake large enough to attack a cat and destroy Colin's camera?"

"Magic alters size. Age alters size. Different species grow to different sizes."

"Pipes don't," Harry said. "It would need to fit inside them."

"Not every pipe is narrow."

Hermione pulled the plumbing book towards her and opened one of its diagrams. Thick iron lines spread beneath the castle foundations, connecting bathrooms, kitchens, classrooms and parts of the dungeons which had apparently been modernised several centuries earlier.

"Some of the oldest channels are wide enough for a person," she said. "The castle's original drainage system runs beneath almost every floor."

Harry studied the diagram. "Why would anyone build pipes that large?"

"Hogwarts used to draw water directly from the lake."

"That doesn't make the lake sound more appealing."

"Plumbing was also less efficient in the tenth century."

"So was personal hygiene, according to Nearly Headless Nick."

Hermione slid the diagram aside and opened Uncommon Creatures of the Medieval Isles. Dust rose from its pages, catching in the green light.

"There are stories about snakes beneath castles," she said. "Most of them aren't about real snakes. People used them as symbols, the way Slytherin did."

"Slytherin used a snake."

"Yes."

Harry read the paragraph upside down. "That feels less symbolic now."

Hermione turned several pages. "A serpent hidden by Salazar Slytherin. A voice only a Parselmouth can understand. A creature capable of moving around the school without being seen."

"Through the pipes."

"Possibly."

"And it doesn't kill anyone."

Hermione's finger stopped beneath a line of text.

"Perhaps it can't."

"It spoke about killing."

"Wanting to do something and being able to do it are different."

"Not usually with monsters."

"We don't know what sort of monster it is."

Harry looked at the shelves surrounding them. Their search had already included venomous snakes, enchanted serpents, creatures capable of paralysing prey and several species which Hermione refused to believe could live inside the British climate.

None explained everything.

"Then why did Draconia tell us to stop?" he asked.

Hermione's hand remained on the open book, but she no longer appeared to be reading it.

The question had accompanied them since the corridor outside Charms. So had the memory of Draconia's warning, delivered with more fear than anger.

If you continue, one of you will be next.

The green satchel had become impossible to ignore. Draconia carried it to every lesson, kept one hand near it whenever they asked about the attacks and refused to show them the notebook concealed inside. Her unexplained absences had grown more frequent. Some days she appeared exhausted without knowing why. On others, she arrived with the wrong books, missing hours she explained as oversleeping or losing track of time.

Harry did not want those details to fit together.

"She may be protecting her family," Hermione said carefully.

"From us?"

"From whatever came through the Manor."

"She said the notebook was from Narcissa."

"She said she assumed it was from Narcissa."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No."

Harry closed the plumbing book. The cover struck the table more loudly than he intended, and Madam Pince raised her head from the front desk.

He waited until she resumed writing.

"Dobby said the danger was close to people I love."

Hermione's eyes lifted to his.

Harry had not intended to say the final word aloud. It sounded larger in the quiet library, too honest to be hidden beneath a joke once spoken.

Hermione's face softened. "That could mean any of us."

"That isn't better."

"No."

"If Dobby knows where the danger came from—"

"He may be unable to tell us."

"He nearly injured himself when I asked."

Hermione glanced towards the entrance. "Draco may know enough to be frightened without understanding what is happening."

"That isn't better either."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

A shadow crossed the open page.

Someone had stopped beside their table.

Draconia stood between the shelves.

Snow had darkened the shoulders of her robes, and several damp strands of hair clung to her cheek. She carried the green satchel but no books. Her breathing was slightly uneven, suggesting she had climbed the stairs quickly or had only recently remembered where she intended to be.

Hermione glanced towards the clock above Madam Pince's desk.

"You didn't come to dinner."

"I wasn't hungry."

"You missed lunch too."

"I ate earlier."

"What did you eat?"

Draconia looked at her. "Food."

"That answer becomes less convincing each time you use it."

Harry pulled the basket Narcissa had sent from beneath the table. The remaining pastry rested inside, wrapped in the blue cloth to keep it warm.

He pushed it towards Draconia.

"Eat."

She stared at the basket. "Have you both become my parents?"

"Narcissa is much more frightening," Harry said.

"Not when she is worried."

"That doesn't help your argument."

A trace of Draconia's familiar irritation returned. "You have both become unbearable."

"You started it."

"With what?"

"Me."

Harry uncovered the pastry.

Draconia sat.

She took one bite because he continued watching. Hermione returned her attention to the book, although she did not turn another page until half the pastry had disappeared.

For several quiet minutes, they were only the three of them beneath the green library lamps.

The wind brushed snow against the windows. Pages turned elsewhere in the room, and Madam Pince's quill scratched steadily across parchment. Harry passed Hermione a reference book without being asked. Draconia corrected the way he had stacked the plumbing volumes and ate the remainder of the pastry while pretending not to notice their satisfaction.

No Chamber. No voice in the walls. No strange notebook concealed beneath Draconia's hand.

Harry could almost imagine the year had become ordinary again.

Then Hermione drew Deadliest Beasts and Their Defeats from beneath the pile.

The heavy book landed on the table.

Draconia stopped chewing.

"We asked you to stop."

"You asked," Hermione said. "We disagreed."

"I was not requesting a vote."

"You don't control us."

"I'm aware. It is one of your worst qualities."

Harry reached for the book before the argument could gather force. Its leather cover had cracked with age, and several warnings had been stamped across the title page.

"What are we searching for?"

"A serpent connected to petrification," Hermione said.

Draconia's head turned sharply towards her.

The reaction was immediate. Her shoulders stiffened, and the hand resting beside the satchel closed against the table.

Harry saw it.

Hermione did too.

"You recognise that," she said.

"No."

"You reacted."

"I reacted to the absurdity."

"What is absurd about it?"

"All of it."

"That is not an explanation."

"It is the only one you are receiving."

Draconia shoved back her chair. The wooden legs scraped across the flagstones, slicing through the library's quiet. Madam Pince's head rose, and several nearby students glanced between the shelves.

Harry caught Draconia's hand before she could stand.

Her skin was cold.

"Stay."

Draconia looked down at their joined hands. Surprise briefly broke through her anger.

Harry loosened his grip, conscious of the tenderness which remained in his newly regrown arm, but he did not release her.

"You don't have to tell us everything tonight," he said.

Hermione inhaled.

Harry kicked her lightly beneath the table.

Her mouth closed.

Draconia's attention moved from Harry to Hermione and back again.

"But stay," Harry repeated.

The anger in her face faltered. Beneath it lay the same exhaustion he had seen in the hospital wing, deeper now and impossible to blame upon missed sleep alone.

She sat.

Harry released her hand.

None of them opened the creature book.

Hermione pulled a blank sheet of parchment from her bag and announced that their Astronomy chart required revision. Draconia identified two incorrect calculations before Hermione finished spreading it across the table. Harry contributed by passing quills, holding down a curling corner and claiming moral responsibility whenever either girl arrived at the correct answer.

The pretence was transparent.

They kept it anyway.

By the time the library bell announced closing, snow had covered the windows and the lamps beyond their table had begun extinguishing themselves. Madam Pince moved between the shelves, directing students towards the doors with increasingly pointed looks.

They packed together.

Hermione returned the creature book to the bottom of her bag. Harry gathered their notes and maps. Draconia stood between them, checking the clasp of the green satchel twice before lifting it over her shoulder.

At the library doors, she reached inside.

Harry stopped.

For one hopeful second, he thought she might produce the notebook.

Instead, Draconia withdrew a small mirror with a silver handle. Pale light from the corridor passed across its polished surface.

Hermione frowned. "Why are you carrying that?"

"The corridors are dangerous."

Harry understood first. "You can check around corners without looking directly."

"Yes."

Hermione took the mirror and examined the glass. "That is actually sensible."

Draconia's eyebrows rose. "I shall treasure the occasion."

"Where did you get it?"

"Pansy."

"Does she know?"

"No."

"That makes the method less sensible."

"She has three more."

"That doesn't make taking it acceptable."

"I will return it when the castle stops containing an unidentified monster."

Draconia reclaimed the mirror and offered it to Hermione.

"Carry one."

Hermione looked beyond the open library doors.

The corridor lay empty beneath widely spaced torches. Shadows gathered between them, and the old pipes behind the walls knocked softly as heated water moved through the castle.

"I will," she said.

By the following afternoon, Hermione believed she had found the answer.

She did not announce it at once.

That alone should have warned Harry.

Instead, she disappeared after lunch with nothing more than a promise to check a final reference. She had taken her notes, the silver mirror Draconia lent her and three books she refused to identify because Harry would "attempt to help by doing something dangerous."

Harry knew she had not gone alone. Professor Vector saw her enter the library, and several Ravenclaws later reported seeing her leave with Penelope Clearwater, a sixth-year prefect. Penelope knew the older sections of the castle, had access to the prefects' bathroom and possessed enough sense to prevent a second-year from investigating a monster without protection.

All of those facts should have been reassuring.

By dinner, they were not.

"She said she was checking one reference," Harry told Draconia.

They stood outside the Great Hall while students passed between them in noisy groups. The castle had darkened early. Snow pressed against the tall entrance-hall windows, turning the remaining daylight a deep winter blue, while candles floating beyond the open doors cast warm gold across the floor.

The smell of roast potatoes and gravy drifted from inside.

Harry had no appetite.

Draconia's gaze moved through the students entering the Hall. She had arrived late from the dungeons, one side of her robes creased and her face paler than the cold could explain.

"Did she tell you where?"

"No."

"Did you let her go alone?"

"She took Penelope."

"That is not an answer."

"I didn't let her do anything. Hermione generally objects when people speak about her that way."

"She objects to most things."

"Only the incorrect ones."

A small bird made from folded parchment emerged from the staircase above them. It fluttered unsteadily across the entrance hall, narrowly avoided a suit of armour and landed upon Harry's shoulder.

He unfolded it.

Hermione's writing covered the inside.

I found it. Meet outside the library before dinner. Bring Draconia. Do not come alone.

The message had been sent twenty minutes earlier.

Draconia read it twice.

"Why would she tell you not to come alone?"

"Because she has more sense than me."

"That is not difficult."

"I've survived so far."

"Through the intervention of exhausted friends, medical professionals and unusually loyal birds."

"You make it sound less impressive."

"She should already be waiting," Draconia said.

The light humour left Harry.

He looked towards the marble staircase.

"Library?"

"We'll use the west passage. It is busier."

They began climbing.

Students flowed down around them, eager for dinner and loud with the relief of reaching the Great Hall safely. Their footsteps echoed beneath the vaulted ceiling. Scarves brushed the banisters, and fragments of conversation followed them between the floors.

Harry and Draconia reached the first landing.

A voice slid through the noise.

Hungry.

Harry stopped with one hand against the railing.

Draconia continued two steps before turning. "What?"

He raised his hand.

The descending students divided around him. Their voices echoed from the stone, ordinary and human. Beneath them, nearly lost inside the groan of the castle pipes, the other voice moved.

Closer than Harry had ever heard it.

So hungry.

His mouth went dry.

"It's here."

Draconia's face emptied. "The voice?"

Harry nodded.

"Where?"

He turned his head, trying to separate direction from distance. The words entered through the wall beside the staircase, travelled above them and faded into the ceiling.

The pipes continued east.

Towards the library.

"Hermione."

Harry ran.

Draconia followed.

They abandoned the crowded staircase and took the narrow passage behind the tapestry of Wendelin the Weird. The painted witch shouted when Harry pulled the fabric aside, protesting that nobody respected historical suffering, but her complaints disappeared behind them.

The hidden corridor smelt of dust and cold stone. Harry's footsteps struck hard against the floor, joined by the quicker rhythm of Draconia's shoes. They forced open the door at the far end and emerged near the Charms corridor.

The voice remained ahead.

Let me tear.

Harry turned left without slowing.

"Where is it?" Draconia called.

"Above us."

They crossed a connecting bridge between towers. Wind forced its way through the narrow windows, carrying loose snow across the floor. Harry's shoes slipped against the wet stone. Draconia caught the back of his robes, kept him upright and released him immediately.

The voice travelled through the wall on their right.

Let me kill.

"It's moving," Harry said.

"Towards Hermione?"

"I don't know."

The answer frightened him more than certainty might have.

They ran down the next staircase, taking the final four steps in a single jump. Portraits leant from their frames as they passed. One called after them to slow down. Another began shouting for a professor.

Harry barely heard either.

They reached the second floor.

The voice stopped.

Silence met them at the end of the staircase.

The corridor stretched ahead beneath rows of guttering torches. Snow-light entered through the narrow windows, mingling with the flames and leaving long blue shadows across the floor. Most classroom doors had been closed for dinner.

No students moved between them.

Harry listened.

The pipes knocked once inside the wall.

"Hermione?" he called.

No answer returned.

A leather schoolbag lay near the far corner.

Its clasp had opened. Books had spilled across the passage, their pages folded beneath their weight. A bottle of ink had shattered against the stones, spreading black liquid through the cracks.

Harry recognised the bag.

His body went cold.

Draconia drew her wand.

Harry stepped forwards.

The silver corner of a hand mirror extended beyond the turn in the corridor. Its glass faced upwards, reflecting torchlight across the ceiling.

"Don't look around the corner," Harry said.

Draconia stopped beside him. "What?"

"Don't look at anything directly."

"Harry—"

"If Hermione was right about reflections, looking directly is what kills."

Draconia lowered her gaze.

A suit of armour stood several feet behind them, one gauntlet resting upon the handle of a broad ceremonial sword. Harry seized the polished shield from its other arm.

The armour clattered indignantly.

"I beg your pardon!"

"Sorry."

"Young people have no regard for military presentation!"

Harry tore the final strap free.

The shield was heavier than he expected. His regrown arm shook beneath its weight, forcing him to support it with both hands. He angled the polished metal towards the corridor ahead.

The reflection trembled across its surface.

He saw grey stone, torchlight and the dark spread of ink. A quill lay near the wall. The silver mirror rested several feet beyond the corner, its handle still caught beneath someone's fingers.

Then a blue sleeve appeared at the edge of the shield.

Harry's breath caught.

"Hermione."

He lowered it.

Draconia caught his shoulder. "You said not to look."

"The voice has gone."

"You don't know that."

"She's there."

"That does not mean the creature has left."

Harry pulled free.

He rounded the corner with his gaze fixed upon the floor.

Penelope Clearwater lay nearest the wall. Her body had fallen sideways against the stones, one arm extended towards the mirror. Her dark hair remained pinned neatly behind her head, but one of the silver clips had broken loose and lay beside her wand.

Hermione was several feet beyond her.

Her hair spread across the floor, hiding half her face. One arm had folded beneath her body. The other remained curled tightly against her chest, her fingers clenched around a torn piece of parchment.

Harry dropped beside her.

"Hermione?"

His knees struck stone.

He placed one hand against her shoulder.

Her body was cold and rigid beneath her robes.

"Hermione."

No response came.

Harry knew what Petrification looked like. He had seen Mrs Norris hanging beneath the writing and Colin motionless upon a stretcher. He knew Hermione was alive. He knew Mandrakes would eventually restore her and that Madam Pomfrey would place her in a bed until the potion could be brewed.

None of that knowledge reached the part of him waiting for her to answer.

He pushed the hair away from her face.

Hermione's eyes were open. They stared past him towards the ceiling, fixed upon the reflection she had seen inside the mirror.

Behind Harry, Draconia stopped breathing.

She sank to her knees on Hermione's other side. Her wand hovered above Hermione's chest, the tip trembling badly enough to scatter light across her robes.

"What spell?" Harry asked.

Draconia did not answer.

"You know healing spells."

"Not for this."

"Try one."

"I don't know what to cast."

"Anything."

"I don't know!"

Her voice broke against the corridor walls.

Harry looked up.

The fear Draconia had concealed for weeks had finally torn through whatever remained of her control. Her face had gone white, and tears shone in her eyes without falling. One hand remained suspended above Hermione's arm, unable to touch her.

"She's alive," Harry said.

It was the only fact he could hold.

Draconia swallowed. "Yes."

"We need a professor."

"Yes."

Neither of them moved.

Harry kept his hand against Hermione's shoulder. Draconia stared at her face, breathing too quickly. Dinner continued somewhere below them, distant enough that the castle might have contained nobody else.

The parchment crackled inside Hermione's fist.

Harry noticed it only because her fingers had crushed one corner through the paper. He carefully worked the sheet free, easing it from a grip which did not loosen.

The page had been torn from an old library book. A rough illustration of a serpent occupied the upper half, its body coiled beneath a crown. Most of the surrounding text concerned creatures recorded in Greece and North Africa, but Hermione had underlined one paragraph twice.

Harry read it.

Of the many fearsome beasts that roam our world, none is more deadly than the Basilisk. Born from a chicken's egg hatched beneath a toad, the King of Serpents may reach gigantic size and live for hundreds of years. Its venom is fatal, its armour proof against most spells and its gaze brings instant death to any who meet it directly.

Beneath the paragraph, Hermione had written two words.

Indirect sight.

Harry turned the page over.

Notes covered the back in hurried lines. Some had been written so quickly that the ink spread where Hermione's hand dragged across it.

Mrs Norris—reflection in water.

Colin—camera lens.

Penelope and mirror for protection.

Travels through pipes.

Roosters fatal to it. Spiders flee.

Voice = Parseltongue.

At the bottom, pressed so deeply that the quill had almost torn through the parchment, Hermione had written the answer.

The monster is a Basilisk.

Draconia read over his shoulder.

"No," she whispered.

The word barely reached him.

Harry looked at her.

All colour had vanished from her face. Her gaze remained fixed upon the serpent illustration, but the horror in it went beyond learning the name of the creature which had attacked Hermione.

"You knew."

Draconia's eyes lifted. "No."

"You recognise it."

"I said no."

The denial sounded desperate rather than convincing.

Harry closed his fingers around the page. "The notebook told you."

"No."

"Then how—"

A faint noise travelled behind the wall.

Both of them froze.

It was not a voice.

A heavy body moved through the pipes, scales dragging across old metal with a low, dry scrape. The sound passed above their heads and continued through the stone.

Harry lowered his gaze.

"Don't look up."

Draconia reached blindly across Hermione and found his wrist. Her fingers locked around it.

The scraping continued.

Harry raised his wand towards the ceiling without lifting his head.

"Periculum!"

Red sparks burst from its tip.

They filled the corridor with scarlet light, struck the vaulted roof and exploded through the stone in a shower bright enough to be seen from every staircase nearby. The suits of armour shouted. Portraits woke inside their frames and began calling for help.

Footsteps answered almost immediately.

Harry remained beside Hermione.

The sound inside the wall receded.

Draconia continued gripping his wrist. She trembled with the same helpless terror she had shown on Halloween night, when she emerged from the crowd and found Mrs Norris beneath the dark writing. Harry wanted to ask what she remembered from that day.

He could not ask while Hermione lay between them.

Professor McGonagall reached the corridor first.

Her robes flew behind her, and one side of her tartan dressing gown showed beneath them, suggesting she had been summoned from her rooms rather than dinner. Professor Snape followed close enough that their shoulders nearly collided as they rounded the corner.

Both stopped.

McGonagall's hand rose towards her mouth.

The movement lasted only an instant before discipline returned. She crossed the distance in three quick strides and knelt beside Hermione.

Snape's attention went first to Penelope.

Then to Draconia.

"Move away from them," he ordered.

Draconia did not release Harry.

"They're Petrified," Harry said.

McGonagall pressed two fingers beneath Hermione's jaw. Her other hand moved above the girl's chest, tracing a diagnostic charm which glowed pale blue before dissolving into her robes.

"Alive," she said.

Harry's lungs unlocked.

He drew a breath which hurt.

Snape examined Penelope with the same spell. "The prefect is in an identical condition."

"They had a mirror," Harry said. "Hermione worked out what the monster is."

He held out the torn page.

McGonagall accepted it and read Hermione's notes. Her eyes moved swiftly down the parchment, slowing when she reached the underlined description.

Her face became utterly still.

Snape rose and read over her shoulder.

"A Basilisk," he said.

Hearing the name spoken aloud changed the corridor. It was no longer a possibility scratched onto paper by a second-year. It became a creature somewhere inside the castle, centuries old and large enough for its body to scrape against the pipes.

McGonagall folded the page carefully. "You will both return to your common rooms."

"No."

The word left Harry before he considered the consequence.

Her head lifted. "Potter."

"I'm not leaving her."

"Miss Granger requires immediate attention in the hospital wing. You cannot help her by remaining here."

"I can go with her."

"The castle is not secure."

"Which is why I'm not walking somewhere else alone."

Snape glanced at him.

McGonagall's mouth tightened. "A prefect will escort you."

"I'm going with Hermione."

"Potter—"

"I'm going."

The determination in his own voice surprised him. Beneath it sat a colder fear: if he allowed Hermione to disappear behind another closed door, something else might happen before he reached her again.

Snape rose from beside Penelope.

"Allow him, Minerva."

McGonagall looked towards him.

Whatever she saw in Snape's face altered her answer.

"Stay beside me," she told Harry. "You will touch nothing else and follow every instruction immediately."

Harry nodded.

She summoned two stretchers. Blue light gathered beneath Hermione and Penelope, lifting them carefully from the floor. Their robes hung motionless below them. Hermione's hand dropped over the edge, her fingers open now that the page had been removed.

Draconia finally released Harry's wrist.

Red marks remained where her nails had pressed through his sleeve.

Snape stepped towards her. "Miss Malfoy."

"I'm going too."

"That was not an invitation."

"She's my friend."

Her voice cracked across the final word.

Snape's anger disappeared.

He lowered one hand towards Draconia's shoulder.

She flinched before he touched her.

The reaction was violent and instinctive. Draconia recoiled far enough that her back struck the wall, one hand rising protectively towards her chest. The green satchel swung against her side.

Harry saw Snape notice.

The professor's gaze moved from Draconia's face to the satchel, then to the leather guard covering her wrist. His mouth hardened, but he did not ask the question Harry expected.

Draconia seemed to realise what she had done. She straightened and forced her hand down. Control returned to her features one careful piece at a time, though it no longer concealed what lay beneath.

Snape withdrew his hand.

"Then stay where I can see you," he said.

They followed the stretchers through the castle.

Portraits watched them pass. Some pressed handkerchiefs to their mouths. Others hurried into neighbouring frames, carrying the news ahead until faces appeared in every corridor before the procession reached it.

Dinner was ending when they descended towards the entrance hall.

The doors of the Great Hall opened. Students began spilling into the corridor, still speaking and laughing until they noticed Professor McGonagall leading two motionless bodies down the staircase.

The noise vanished.

Teachers moved quickly to hold the crowd back. Professor Sprout drew the nearest Hufflepuffs away from the foot of the stairs, while Flitwick raised both arms and ordered the Ravenclaws to remain inside the Hall.

It did not stop them from watching.

Hermione passed beneath the floating candles upon the stretcher, rigid and silent while hundreds of students stared.

Harry walked beside her.

The same people who had avoided him now moved back for a different reason. Fear spread through them without the protection of whispers. Hermione Granger had spent weeks defending Harry from accusations that he had opened the Chamber.

Now the Chamber had taken her too.

Harry kept expecting her to sit upright.

He expected her to complain that McGonagall had folded the page before the ink dried or point out that everyone trampling through the entrance hall might be destroying useful evidence. He expected her to remind him that the book from which she had torn the page would need repairing and that Madam Pince would consider Petrification an insufficient excuse.

Hermione did none of those things.

Her hand remained open over the edge of the stretcher.

Harry reached for it before remembering she could not feel him.

He held it anyway.

Draconia walked on the other side. She stared straight ahead, one hand wrapped around the strap of her satchel. Her face had regained its composure, but she had become too controlled, every step measured and every breath deliberate.

Harry knew that kind of stillness.

It was what remained when feeling anything became too dangerous.

The doors to the hospital wing opened before them.

Madam Pomfrey took one look at the stretchers and began clearing beds. Curtains flew along their rails. Equipment crossed the ward under its own power, filling the air with the rattle of metal trays and glass bottles.

Colin remained behind the screens near the window.

His trainers were still visible beneath the curtain.

Mrs Norris remained in the staff quarters, waiting for Mandrakes mature enough to restore her.

Now Hermione joined them.

Madam Pomfrey guided Hermione onto the empty bed nearest Colin. The blankets drew themselves over her, covering everything except her face and the hand Harry had been holding.

He did not release it until the healer ordered him back.

Draconia stopped near the foot of the bed. She wrapped both arms around herself, trapping the green satchel between her body and her robes. The silver clasp pressed into her fingers, but she appeared not to notice.

Harry stood on the opposite side.

Madam Pomfrey spoke in a low voice to McGonagall. Snape unfolded Hermione's notes and read them again beneath the ward's white light. Somewhere beyond the high windows, wind crossed the castle towers and pressed snow against the glass.

Their words reached Harry without meaning.

Hogwarts had once seemed incapable of resembling Privet Drive.

It had moving staircases and talking portraits, fires which never went out and feasts beneath an enchanted sky. Its corridors had been crowded with people who knew his name. It had given him rooms in which his arrival was expected, friends who noticed when his letters stopped and more than one person to wave to from a railway platform.

At Privet Drive, Harry had survived by wanting very little. He had learnt not to expect anyone to come when he called, not to leave possessions where they could be taken and not to reveal when something hurt. Hogwarts had taught him different habits. Hermione and Draconia had taught him to expect footsteps beside his own.

Now Hermione lay silent before him.

Draconia stood close enough to touch, yet the secrets between them had widened into a distance Harry did not know how to cross.

A familiar numbness stirred beneath his fear. It did not arrive dramatically. It came with the quiet efficiency of an old defence, reminding him how simple life became when he expected nothing and needed no one.
