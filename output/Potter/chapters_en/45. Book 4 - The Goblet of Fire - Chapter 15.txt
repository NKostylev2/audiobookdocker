15

By breakfast the following morning, Harry's family had become an unfair advantage, or so the Daily Prophet informed him when an owl dropped its special edition into his porridge at eight minutes past seven. The front page opened across the bowl, absorbed milk along one corner and displayed a moving photograph of Harry coming out of the lake with Sirius beside him. His godfather's arm was around him. Again and again, the image returned to the moment Harry's knees failed and Sirius caught him, letting anybody who watched decide which of them had performed the rescue. The paper offered no glimpse of the water before that moment. Gabrielle had been cropped out.

Harry lifted the wet page by its corner, milk dripping back into the bowl. Gabrielle had been there. He remembered Draconia taking her from Sirius and Fleur running across the bank; he remembered the child's hands clutching her sister's neck, alive and frightened and very much part of the photograph. The photographer had needed to stand almost directly in front of them to produce this picture. Removing the child had required more effort than printing the truth.

BLACK'S BOY: A RESCUE ARRANGED?

Hermione set down her tea. Draconia had barely sat beside Harry before the conversations around them began to falter. Several students found urgent reasons to reach for the butter or reread a timetable, turning just enough to listen without appearing to do so.

"She's cut out an eight-year-old," Harry said.

"She gets in the way of the argument," Hermione replied, her eyes moving down the column.

"What argument?"

Draconia took the paper before milk could erase it. "That your guardian shouldn't have been allowed to take part. Apparently his experience gave you an advantage."

"He was asleep."

"She spends quite a lot of time on his experience. Very little on whether he was conscious."

The article referred to Sirius as a former Azkaban inmate four times before admitting that he had been cleared. Harry found the qualification beneath a paragraph about Dumbledore's alleged preference for families whose names could command attention. It occupied half a sentence. His godfather's years of imprisonment filled the rest of the column. Rita had made them sound like evidence against him, although the decision to take part had been discussed with teachers and recorded before the task. Sirius had written about paint decisions at Hawthorn, as though they were having an ordinary argument across the kitchen table. Harry had found him sleeping under the merpeople's protection. Now, reading the paper, he could scarcely recognise the morning he had lived through. Rita had found a scheme in the letter, the safeguards and the simple fact that Sirius wanted him home.

"According to this," Draconia said, "he helped you complete the underwater portion."

"He woke at the surface."

"That rather spoils the headline."

Hermione reached across the table and turned the page. Another photograph showed her and Draconia entering the shallows. Hermione's mouth was open around Harry's name; Draconia held out both hands for Gabrielle, whose body had disappeared beyond the edge of the image.

POTTER'S OTHER COMPETING INTERESTS.

Harry looked from the caption to the smaller column beneath it. Rita had joined the Ball to the task with the confidence of somebody who believed standing beside Harry amounted to a confession. Hermione and Draconia remained his childhood companions and abandoned admirers. Luna, because she had danced with him, remained a potential romantic secret. The article managed to accuse him of depending too heavily upon his guardian while implying that every girl who helped him did so in the hope of becoming indispensable herself.

"I am going to feed her quill to a Blast-Ended Skrewt," Hermione said.

"Cruel," Harry replied.

"To Rita?"

"To the Skrewt."

Neville choked upon toast. Ginny covered a smile with the sports section. Harry almost joined them, then saw the photograph repeat beneath Draconia's hand. Sirius had caught him. Remus had cut the fastening. Cedric had gone back into the water. The paper had turned every hand that reached for him into evidence of a plan. He was tired of seeing people punished for helping him.

"It quotes him," Hermione said.

Harry leaned closer. Halfway down, Rita quoted Sirius admitting he had accepted the role partly to prevent another child being asked. She called it an acknowledgement that the selection had been influenced by his personal interests.

"He said that in the tent."

"While Madam Pomfrey was outside," Draconia said. "While I was with Remus."

Harry remembered the flap closing, his own voice asking why Sirius had agreed. He had not been thinking about reporters. He had been trying to tell his godfather why finding him had hurt, even with all the safeguards in place. He had thought that conversation belonged to the two of them. The paper buckled beneath his fingers, creasing Sirius's face.

"He is my guardian," Harry said.

Hermione looked up. Draconia's hand remained upon the edge of the page. Harry had not intended to speak to anyone beyond their table. But people nearby had already stopped pretending not to hear. He could feel their attention, and for once the thought of saying nothing was worse than the thought of being watched.

"He agreed to be there after checking the protections. He didn't coach me underwater. He didn't wake until we reached the surface. I carried him because he was the person they asked me to bring back."

Seamus turned upon the bench. Further down, somebody lowered another copy of the paper.

"And Gabrielle was there too," Harry continued. "She's eight. Fleur couldn't reach her. Look at the photograph—you can see where Rita cut her out."

He held up the photograph. Its empty edge ran beside Draconia's outstretched hands.

"Having somebody who wants me home isn't cheating."

The sentence came more quietly than the others. Only after he said it did Harry feel how much it had cost. Other people could mention home without exposing anything; his voice nearly caught on the word. Hermione watched him without moving. Draconia's hand left the paper and settled upon his knee beneath the table.

"And if anyone wants to know who I'd go into a lake for," he added, "it's an inconvenient number of people. That isn't a scandal. It's poor survival instinct."

Cedric raised his cup from Hufflepuff. "I went back in from the shore," he said, loudly enough to carry. "Mr Black was awake by then. Harry had already brought both of them up."

"You could make that sound less unpleasant," Harry called.

"I could. It would make it less accurate."

Fleur stood. The attention moved towards her so quickly that Harry could hear the benches shift.

"Harry Potter brought my sister back," she said, each word clear in the sudden quiet. "The judges heard what happened from the merpeople. If this paper finds their account inconvenient, perhaps it should explain why it cut a child out of the photograph before accusing the people who rescued her."

She sat. Applause began near Beauxbatons, joined by Hufflepuff and then the Gryffindors around Harry. Other students argued across their papers. Harry lowered the photograph, painfully aware of how many people had heard him mention home. The Hall had begun talking again, but his face still felt warm. He looked down at his ruined breakfast, grateful when Hermione gave him something else to attend to. Hermione pushed his bowl aside and gave him a clean one.

"You made a speech."

"I clarified a photograph."

"With a speech."

Draconia reread the smaller column. "You didn't deny the competing admirers."

Harry stared at her. Hermione's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

"That's the bit you noticed?"

"Precision improves conversation."

"Would you like another announcement?"

Draconia lifted her eyes from the page. He saw the caution beneath her teasing. She was waiting for his answer more carefully than she wanted him to notice, her fingers still on the page as though they were discussing its grammar.

"No," she said.

Harry folded the newspaper until neither photograph remained visible. "Good."

Luna arrived with her shoes hanging from one hand. Silver moons moved around her purple socks. She noticed the strip of yellow wool visible above Harry's shoe and smiled at the blue fish turning around his ankle.

"You wore them."

"You checked."

"They looked pleased yesterday."

Harry did not ask how socks expressed an opinion. Luna examined the folded paper, opened it at the smaller column and considered the paragraph which assigned her a secret affection for him.

"Are we in love?"

"Apparently."

"That seems sudden."

"I thought so."

She glanced at Hermione and Draconia. "Will it cause difficulties?"

"Already has," Hermione said.

Harry took the paper back. "Luna was my friend before the Ball. She's still my friend. We haven't been hiding some enormous secret."

"It has discovered a dress," Draconia said.

"And appears very impressed by it." Luna sat beside Neville and took a piece of toast from Harry's plate. "Rita Skeeter was near the judges' platform as a beetle yesterday."

Hermione went still. "As a what?"

"A green beetle. The markings around its eyes looked like spectacles. It was on the railing while we were waiting, and later on the medical tent."

Draconia lowered the newspaper. Harry looked towards the front page again, towards a sentence spoken inside a closed tent and reproduced by somebody who ought never to have heard it.

"You saw an Animagus," Draconia said.

"I saw a beetle listening to private conversations. It might also have been unusually interested in journalism."

Hermione reached across Neville for the paper. "We need to check every private conversation she's printed. Who was there, who could have heard it—all of it."

For the first time that morning, Hermione looked delighted. Harry knew the expression well enough to move his porridge clear of her notebook before she reached for it. Rita had made a mistake considerably more dangerous than lying about the girls: she had given Hermione a problem with an answer to find.

The castle changed its opinion of Harry for eleven days. Students who had accused him of entering the Tournament now held doors open. Younger children followed him through corridors in hopeful groups. A third-year Hufflepuff asked him to sign a napkin because she had nothing else available. Two Slytherins who had ignored him since first year nodded when he passed. Harry found their approval no easier to bear than their hostility; either way, they were watching someone they had made up. Harry had been liar, orphan, champion, romantic tragedy and hero within four months. None of the titles had required the person underneath to change. He took the quieter corridors when he could, and this time Hermione and Draconia went with him, talking about essays while the noise receded behind them.

They no longer had to guess where he had disappeared to. Harry told them before changing routes, sometimes catching himself halfway towards a door and coming back to explain. When the Great Staircase became crowded after lunch, he asked whether they minded using the passage behind the tapestry. When he needed quiet, he said so and named where he would be. It felt ridiculous to make such a fuss over taking a different staircase or needing half an hour alone. Yet the more often he did it, the less often Hermione looked up as though she had been counting the minutes. Hermione stopped checking the Marauder's Map before breakfast. Draconia stopped sending first-years to confirm Harry reached Gryffindor Tower. Harry pretended not to know she had been doing that, although one first-year had made the mistake of delivering his report while Harry was still within earshot. The Ball still lay between them, as did the embrace inside the medical tent. Some days they could talk around both with surprising ease; on others, a touch or an unfinished sentence made Harry feel the distance again.

During study, Harry sometimes looked up and found Hermione watching the page rather than reading it. Draconia sat closer than she had before Christmas and farther away than she wanted. Accidental touches lasted a second too long or ended too quickly. Harry was beginning to notice the hesitation behind a hand withdrawn too quickly, or the hope in a glance that lingered. He did not always know what to do with either. Rita's article had made the entire school determined to decide for them.

"Lovegood rejected him," Pansy announced in Potions on the fourth day. "That's why she's still barefoot."

"That's an impressive leap," Harry said.

"No one asked you."

"You were discussing me from six feet away."

"Perhaps we thought you'd have enough dignity not to listen."

"That was optimistic."

Snape removed five points from Gryffindor for conversational trespass and five from Slytherin for conducting gossip without originality. Behind her cauldron, Draconia pressed her lips together. Harry caught the almost-smile before she looked away and spent the rest of the lesson finding it easier to concentrate.

Cedric invited Harry flying three days later. The invitation arrived during History of Magic upon a folded scrap of parchment carried by a paper bird. It struck Harry behind one ear, circled Hermione twice when she attempted to catch it and landed inside his open textbook. Two lines appeared in Cedric's writing.

Pitch after lessons. Bring your broom.

Below them, in smaller letters, he had added:

And my mug, if the attachment has become manageable.

Harry looked towards the mint-coloured cup protruding from his schoolbag. Hermione read over his shoulder.

"You have had that for nearly two months."

"Cedric said there was no rush."

"That generally means several days."

"It has emotional significance now."

Draconia, seated across the aisle, caught Harry's eye. He turned the note so she could see it.

"Go," she mouthed.

It still startled him when either girl encouraged him to spend time elsewhere. He had grown used to measuring every invitation against the possibility that someone would feel left behind. The Quidditch pitch stood empty beneath a sky scrubbed pale by wind. Winter had loosened its hold upon the grounds, leaving the grass flattened and wet beneath Harry's boots. Puddles occupied the goalmouths. The spectator towers creaked whenever a gust moved through them, and long strips of forgotten House ribbon snapped against the railings from a season the Tournament had cancelled. Cedric waited beside the broom shed with a Quaffle beneath one arm.

"That's not Seeker equipment," Harry said, eyeing the Quaffle.

"Neither is the mug you've kidnapped."

Harry handed it over.

Cedric examined the handle and a small chip near the rim. "You used it."

"Twice."

"There's tea staining halfway down."

"Repeatedly, then."

Cedric placed it on the bench. "Keep it."

"I carried it across the castle."

"Think of it as a character-building exercise."

They mounted their brooms. Harry's Firebolt rose at the smallest shift of weight. Cold air pressed tears from his eyes as the ground dropped away, taking the corridors, articles and careful conversations with it. Cedric climbed beside him upon a Cleansweep, slower in a straight line but steadier when the wind struck across the pitch. For several minutes, they did nothing but fly. Harry let himself follow the tug of the broom rather than a route somebody had worked out on a map, turning whenever the open air invited him. Harry circled the eastern hoops, rolled beneath Cedric when the older boy attempted to cut him off and drove towards the grass until wet green filled his vision. He pulled up close enough for the broom's tail to strike water from a puddle. Cedric swore as the spray caught his face.

"You did that on purpose."

"The wind changed."

"At ground level?"

"Unusual weather."

Cedric threw the Quaffle at him. Harry caught it against his chest, nearly lost his balance and accelerated before Cedric could recover. They invented rules while breaking them: one point for passing through the lowest hoop, two if the other person failed to steal the ball and five for striking the abandoned commentator's podium without breaking a window. Harry won that one by accident. By the time they landed, mud streaked Cedric's robes and Harry's hair had abandoned every direction except upward. His lungs hurt from cold and laughter rather than fear. They sat halfway up the lowest stand with their brooms across the bench before them.

The lake shone beyond the grounds. From this distance, it looked harmless.

"You came back for me," Harry said.

Cedric leaned his elbows upon the rail. "You were carrying two people."

"You had already finished."

"We made an agreement."

All back.

Harry looked at the dark line of water. "You could have lost points."

"I didn't."

"That isn't the point."

"Exactly." Cedric glanced sideways. "Annoying when someone else does it, isn't it?"

Harry considered pushing him from the stand.

"I understand Hermione better now," Cedric added.

"I definitely understand the urge to shove someone off a stand."

Wind moved across the empty seats.

Cedric's smile faded into something quieter. "I meant what I said before the task. We're friends, Harry. You don't have to keep a list of who warned who about a dragon or went back into a lake."

Harry ran one thumb along the Firebolt's handle. "I know."

"Good kind?"

Harry looked at him sharply.

Cedric laughed. "Hermione said it in the medical tent. Half the shore heard her."

"I may transfer schools."

"The Goblet seems to think you already have."

Harry groaned and leaned back against the bench. Cedric watched the clouds move above the castle.

"Cho says Rita's article will fade once somebody more interesting makes a mistake."

"That's a comforting view of journalism."

"She also says Luna is enjoying the rumours."

"Luna enjoys observing confusion."

"And Hermione?"

Harry turned his head.

Cedric raised both hands. "Not my business."

"Correct."

"You looked as if you wanted advice."

"I looked at you."

"Easy mistake to make."

Harry laughed despite himself.

"Cho and I ask each other questions," Cedric said. "It saves a fair bit of misery."

"There it is."

"What?"

"Advice."

"You looked as if you could use it."

Harry turned the Firebolt across his knees. "What if the question doesn't have a sensible answer?"

"Then you ask whether the other person wants the honest one."

"That sounds suspiciously simple."

"Simple and easy are not the same."

Cedric stood and collected his broom. Harry stayed on the bench a moment longer, looking across the empty pitch towards the castle windows. Hermione and Draconia would be waiting for dinner. For once, the thought of an unanswered question did not make him want to find another reason to stay outside. Then he picked up Cedric's mug.

"You said keep it," Harry reminded him.

Cedric began walking towards the changing rooms. "You seem emotionally attached."

Harry followed, carrying his broom in one hand and the mug in the other. The castle's opinion would change again. The Tournament would invent another way to test whether any of them deserved to return. For one cold afternoon, none of it had followed them into the air. That was enough.

Sirius collected Harry from Hogsmeade on the first Saturday in March. He arrived twelve minutes late riding a black motorbike which announced his presence throughout the village. Harry heard it before the machine turned into the High Street. Shop windows trembled. Three owls abandoned the post-office roof. Madam Rosmerta emerged from the Three Broomsticks holding a polishing cloth and watched Sirius draw to a stop beside the designated Apparition point. His hair had escaped its tie. Snowmelt streaked one boot. The motorbike continued growling after he removed both hands from it. Professor McGonagall closed her eyes.

She had escorted Harry to the village herself. His guardian's permission to take him off school grounds already contained six conditions; the sight of the motorbike appeared likely to produce several more than the seventh she had added that morning.

"You possess a perfectly serviceable Floo connection," she said.

"Harry spent the journey here in July in the sidecar. He has never ridden behind me."

"And I can see several reasons to preserve that distinction."

Sirius looked at Harry. "She dislikes joy."

"I dislike avoidable funerals."

"Entirely different subjects, then."

McGonagall handed Sirius a signed parchment. "Mr Potter returns before eight. He does not operate the vehicle. He wears a helmet, a warming charm and whatever protection is required to keep him attached during flight."

Harry looked at the motorbike. "Are we flying again? I thought we were taking the road."

Sirius's grin widened. McGonagall pointed one finger at him.

"On the road."

The grin diminished. "Tyranny."

"Eight o'clock."

She left before Sirius could renegotiate. Harry accepted the spare helmet. It had once belonged to James and still bore a faded gold stag beneath one ear. He traced the chipped paint with his thumb, careful around the worn edges of the stag. The padding inside had softened with use. Sirius waited while he looked, his grin easing into something quieter.

"He thought it made him look dangerous," he said.

"Did it?"

"It made his head look enormous."

Harry smiled and put it on. The helmet fitted perfectly. Sirius secured Harry to the seat with three charms and checked each twice, tugging at the fastenings until McGonagall would have had little to criticise. He then spent the journey claiming he was not worried. The motorbike followed the road through hills washed clean by melting snow. Harry held on as Sirius leaned into the bends. Wind caught his laughter and carried it away before he could decide whether the speed ought to frighten him. Sirius heard and accelerated until the world became stone walls, bare trees and cold sunlight breaking across fields. Hawthorn Cottage appeared beyond a bend. Smoke rose from the chimney. Hedwig waited upon the garden wall and turned her back when the bike stopped.

"She's still angry you left her here," Sirius said.

"You said she liked it."

"She likes being angry here."

Harry removed the helmet. Hedwig flew to his shoulder, bit his ear and pressed her head beneath his chin.

"Very convincing."

Inside, the cottage smelled of bread. Harry stopped in the kitchen. A round loaf cooled on the table beneath a clean tea towel, its evenly risen crust brown rather than black. Harry leaned closer. Nothing leaked from the centre.

"Who made that?"

Sirius removed his coat with offended dignity. "I did."

"Did Remus supervise?"

"He left written instructions."

"Did you follow them?"

"Mostly."

Harry looked beneath the table.

"What are you doing?"

"Looking for the first attempts."

Sirius kicked a covered basket farther under his chair. "There were no first attempts."

Something inside the basket gave a brittle crunch. Harry sat down before Sirius could elaborate, watching him cut the successful loaf. The knife through the crust, the butter waiting on the table, Hedwig stealing crumbs while Sirius pretended not to notice—all of it made Harry's chest tighten in a way he had not expected. No emergency required them to be in the same room. No investigation had begun yet. Harry ate three slices. Sirius counted none of them aloud. When the plates were empty, Sirius collected crumbs with his thumb and rearranged the butter dish. Then he opened a cupboard, stared inside without taking anything and closed it again. Harry waited at the table, beginning to suspect the cupboard was buying Sirius time.

"What did you break?"

Sirius looked offended. "Why must something be broken?"

"You're behaving responsibly."

"I can behave responsibly."

"Remus says you require supervision."

"Remus has built an entire personality around slander." Sirius wiped both hands upon a tea towel. "Come upstairs."

"That didn't make it sound any less suspicious."

The upper corridor remained narrower than the one below, its ceiling following the slope of the roof. Sirius passed his own door and the smaller room Remus used, then stopped before Harry's. The dark blue paint and little brass nameplate had survived the winter. Harry touched the key beneath his jumper. He had never been asked to return it; there was no sensible reason for the sight of his door to make him hesitate now.

Sirius's hand settled upon the handle.

"I found something," he said. "I thought you might want it upstairs."

"Is it alive?"

"Your faith wounds me."

He opened the door. Harry's room looked like Harry's room. The blue-grey walls held the afternoon light, the dark green quilt lay across the bed and both windows remained clear, one facing the orchard and the other the garden. The desk still faced into the room, where Harry could see the door without turning. His Quidditch poster hung above it. A book he had left during the December visit waited with a scrap of parchment marking his place.

The ugly brass lamp had not returned.

Harry looked at the bedside table, then at Sirius.

"I expected you to sneak it back."

"Remus has been forced to appreciate it downstairs. I consider the matter settled."

There were additions, but Sirius had placed them upon the desk rather than rearranged anything. A stack of Quidditch magazines waited beside a small wooden box. The uppermost issue was three years old; beneath it, the paper grew yellower. Harry lifted the newer copies carefully.

"Those are mine," Sirius said. "The older ones were James's. I found them with the helmet."

Harry opened one of the older issues. An argument occupied the margin beside an article about defensive formations, written in two different hands. James had underlined a sentence so firmly that the next page bore its shape. Sirius had drawn an arrow towards it and written something uncomplimentary about the author's eyesight.

"You argued with magazines?"

"They refused to admit defeat."

Inside the box lay three photographs and a broken Snitch which fluttered one wing when Harry disturbed it. James laughed from the first picture, holding the same helmet Harry had worn that morning. Harry set the magazine down before his fingers could crease it.

"Are these for me?"

"If you want them."

He looked towards the shelves. They were no longer entirely empty: books, photographs and the things he had chosen to leave here occupied them. There was space beside those things. Sirius had kept it, although Harry spent most of the year sleeping in another room hundreds of steps above the Hogwarts grounds.

"You could keep some," Harry said.

"I have some." Sirius's voice roughened. "I wanted you to have these."

Harry carried the box to the shelf, then moved one of his books to make room. He placed the photograph where he could see it from the desk. Sirius watched without suggesting a better arrangement.

"I keep forgetting," Harry admitted.

"Where things go? That appears hereditary."

"That I can leave them."

The joke disappeared gently from Sirius's face.

"You've had this room since July, Harry. Being at school doesn't give it back to me. Nor does being entered in something neither of us wanted."

Harry's hand closed around the key through his jumper.

"For Easter?"

"For Easter. For summer, apart from whatever short stay the blood wards still require. For any time you come home." Sirius glanced at the photographs. "I would prefer you to stop growing long enough for the clothes in the wardrobe to remain useful, but I'm prepared to negotiate."

Harry laughed once, then crossed the room and held him. The embrace knocked Sirius backwards against the wardrobe, setting the hangers rattling. This time, Harry knew which of his clothes hung among them. He held tighter, his face pressed into his godfather's shoulder, and did not apologise for the noise.

"The walls can stay blue," he said.

"You've developed taste. Remus will be unbearable."

"It's my room."

Sirius drew back enough to look at him, his eyes bright.

"It is," he said. "That part was settled before you ever went back to school."

After lunch, Remus arrived carrying a leather folder. Lucius followed through the Floo, stepping onto the hearthrug and examining the soot on his sleeve as though the fireplace had broken an agreement between them.

"You could have Apparated," Sirius said.

"Your boundary wards redirected me into a pond."

"Only the first time."

"Once was sufficient."

Lucius removed soot with a charm and looked at Harry. "You appear thin."

"Hello to you too."

"Did you receive my letter?"

"Both words."

"Yet you remain thin."

Sirius put another slice of bread upon Harry's plate.

"Don't encourage him," Harry said.

"I'm terrified of your future father-in-law."

The kitchen fell silent. Lucius's wand stopped halfway into his cane, and Harry stared at the bread in his hand as though he had forgotten what came next. Sirius looked from one to the other and visibly reconsidered his sentence.

"Potential," he added. "Theoretical. One of several possible—"

"Stop," Harry said.

"Excellent suggestion," Lucius replied.

Remus placed the folder upon the table with enough force to rescue everyone.

"The gillyweed."

Harry opened it immediately. The first page contained a map of Britain. The three licensed growers were marked in red. One, on the Cornish coast, had been circled in a heavier hand.

"The salt in the bladderwrack matches a cultivated pool near Falmouth," Remus said. "The owner reported a theft at two in the morning on the day of the task. One cutting. No money, equipment or other plants."

"Two?" Harry looked up. "Sprout said six hours."

"Out of salt water. Whoever took it could have kept it in brine until they packed it for you." Remus tapped the report. "We know when the owner noticed it was gone. We don't know when it was wrapped."

"Wards?" Harry asked.

"Never activated."

Lucius leaned against the counter. "The greenhouse was protected against Apparition, Portkeys, human trespass and magical pests. None of its wards would have excluded a house-elf."

Harry looked up. "Winky."

"Possibly," Remus said. "Or any elf ordered to retrieve the plant."

"Pipkin?"

"Dumbledore asked the Hogwarts elves. He didn't say who had received the plant, and none of them admitted leaving the grounds."

"Would they be able to admit it if a wizard ordered silence?"

Remus glanced at Sirius, and Lucius looked down at the map. Harry remembered Winky's hands closing around her own throat when she spoke of young master. He could not mistake their silence for an answer that would help her.

"Winky left Hawthorn Cottage before the task."

"Three nights before," Sirius said. "We believe she returned to the castle. Food disappears from the kitchens at hours when no student is present. The elves refuse to say for whom."

"They're protecting her."

"Yes."

Harry returned to the map. The line from Cornwall to Hogwarts crossed nearly five hundred miles. An elf could travel it in seconds, pass wards designed for people and leave the package outside a room no one should have known they occupied.

"We need to ask her," Lucius said.

Hermione's voice returned from the kitchens months earlier.

If Winky needs anything, she may ask any of us. Without answering questions first.

"No," Harry said.

Lucius's eyes lifted. "She may know who is attempting to kill you."

"And she nearly strangled herself the last time young master escaped. We told her she didn't have to answer our questions."

"Circumstances have changed."

"Promises don't only apply while they're convenient."

Lucius became very still. Sirius watched Harry with quiet approval. Remus closed the folder.

"Then we give her a way to come to us safely," he said. "A message through the Hogwarts elves. We won't insist on answers, and she gets our protection whether she tells us anything or not."

Lucius did not look convinced. He also did not argue.

"And Crouch?" Harry asked.

Sirius's expression hardened. "His instructions stopped for thirty-six hours surrounding the task."

"Exactly when someone needed to collect the gillyweed."

"They resumed the morning after the task," Remus said. "Same handwriting. Same wand seal. The first ordered Percy to authorise additional hedge imports for the Third Task."

"Could an elf write them?"

"No."

"Could an elf carry instructions from someone forcing Crouch?"

"Yes."

Harry looked towards the sitting room, where firelight moved across the repaired walls.

"If his son survived Azkaban—"

Lucius's face changed. "He did not."

"Winky called someone young master. Crouch refuses to explain how his wand was stolen. Then she disappeared from his wards during the World Cup, and he vanished after my name came out of a Goblet he transported."

"Those things may be connected. That does not make them proof."

"You taught Draconia to build cases from association."

"I taught Draconia never to mistake a compelling theory for a concluded one."

Harry thought of Moody's scarred face, the magical eye, advice arriving whenever he needed it and gillyweed appearing outside a door.

"What about Moody before the school year?"

Remus and Sirius exchanged a look.

"His last confirmed sighting was the twenty-ninth of August," Sirius said. "Ministry officers answered a disturbance at his house on the thirtieth. Moody claimed rubbish bins had been tampered with."

"Claimed in person?"

"Through the door."

Harry looked at him. "Then no one saw him."

"No one we have found."

Lucius straightened. "Alastor Moody has known Dumbledore for decades. Polyjuice would not give an impostor his memories, his habits or the magical signature Dumbledore has checked repeatedly."

"The Goblet accepted a fourth school," Harry said. "Ancient magic can be persuaded."

Sirius placed both hands upon the table. "We keep looking."

"Right."

Harry sighed. "Apparently I announced it at breakfast."

The man wore Ministry robes torn open at one shoulder. Mud darkened them from hem to waist. One shoe was missing. Grey hair lay plastered across a face made skeletal by exhaustion. Blood marked his mouth and both hands. He took two steps into the sunlight before his knees gave way. It took a moment for Harry to recognise the man beneath the blood and exhaustion.

"Mr Crouch?"

The man's head lifted. His eyes searched Harry's face for several seconds before they seemed to find something familiar.

"Potter."

His voice scraped across the name. Harry moved closer without lowering his wand. Krum circled to Crouch's other side. The Forest remained dark behind him. Nothing followed between the trees.

Crouch stared at the grass. "Must see Dumbledore."

"We can take you."

"Alone." His bloodied fingers closed around Harry's sleeve. "Dumbledore. Must warn—mistake. My mistake."

He attempted to stand. His legs failed. Harry caught him beneath one arm. Krum took the other. Together, they lowered him onto the grass. Crouch's skin felt cold through his torn robes. Up close, Harry could see that the Forest had not done all of this to him. Yellow bruises shadowed both wrists in complete rings. Ink stained the sides of his fingers and had collected beneath broken nails, as though he had been forced to write long after holding a quill became difficult. Beneath the mud, his Ministry robes smelled faintly of sweat, old potion and the trapped air of a room whose windows had not been opened.

"Weatherby."

"Percy?"

Crouch's eyes moved beyond him.

"Weatherby, the reports must be filed before Thursday. International Cooperation cannot become an excuse for delay. Where is Winky?"

The clipped, familiar rhythm belonged to the man Harry had seen at the World Cup, but Crouch was looking past him now, speaking to somebody who was not there. Harry crouched to bring his face level with his.

"Winky is safe."

For an instant, every tree possessed a black shadow and Crouch's ruined face became stark beneath it. Several distant castle windows reflected the signal. "In a minute." Harry did not know whether the absence meant no one had seen or someone had ensured nobody would.

Harry and Krum exchanged a look.

"Whose eye?" Harry asked.

Crouch struck one hand against the ground. "Must see Dumbledore."

Crouch looked from Harry to the trees, his lips moving around words that would not come. Whatever he was trying to warn them about seemed to have slipped beyond his reach again.

"Mr Crouch, who did Winky watch?"

The man's mouth moved.

"My son."

Harry became very still. Krum looked at him.

His voice collapsed. Krum looked towards the castle. No one had answered the sparks.

Crouch recoiled at the name.

Crouch looked at him. For one moment, complete awareness returned.

"My son."

Harry's blood went cold. "Where?"

Harry remembered the disturbance before Moody's arrival, the advice that had carried him through the First Task, and the gillyweed outside a room nobody should have found. Each detail had seemed possible to explain on its own. He no longer knew how to hold them apart.

Crouch's mouth opened. His eyes lost focus.

"I have no son."

"Yes."

Krum drew his wand and positioned himself between Crouch and the Forest.

Harry ran.

"Crouch."

Moody's face revealed nothing. "You've spent months asking whether his dead son lived."

Harry could hear how reasonable that sounded. There always seemed to be a reasonable explanation, and he was beginning to hate how easily he could find one.

Hermione dropped beside him. She found a pulse. "Alive."

The trio sat inside the unused Charms classroom with the door locked but not bolted. Harry had repeated Crouch's words to Dumbledore, McGonagall, Snape, Sirius and Remus. With each retelling, the words became easier to repeat and harder to connect to the blood on Crouch's hands. MY SON.

POLYJUICE.

WINKY WATCHED.

NOTT.

BERTHA KNEW.

A FACE DUMBLEDORE TRUSTS.

Which one?

BARTY CROUCH.
