13

Harry sat on the floor with his back against a desk, still wearing the white shirt and dark trousers from the Ball. Without the green robes, polished shoes or properly tied cravat, they had lost any suggestion of elegance. His collar hung open, the sleeves were creased to his elbows, and a faint gold smear marked the cuff against which he had rested the egg. He had chosen the room because no one used it and because the door possessed a bolt on the inside. The bolt had seemed practical when he arrived; now, in the pale morning light, it looked too much like something Uncle Vernon would have installed.

Beyond the classroom windows, snow had erased the grounds beneath a clean white sheet, softening the roofs, the battlements and the distant line of the Forbidden Forest until Hogwarts appeared to stand at the edge of an unfinished world. Inside, the golden egg had fallen silent, although its final note seemed to linger in the stone rather than the air: too thin to hear now, and impossible to forget.

Harry looked away and drew the egg closer between his knees. Every scale and curved feather in its chased metal caught what little light entered the room, bright enough to suggest importance, or at least an answer worth finding. Open, it produced only pain. Closed, it became beautiful again. There was probably a lesson in that, though Hermione would have found it too obvious and Draconia would have called it sentimental before remembering it anyway.

He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until colour burst against the dark. He had not slept, and exhaustion made every thought arrive without its protective skin.

We need time.

Not forever.

He believed Hermione had meant it when she called the words a promise. That was almost worse. Forever could be hated properly, could be recognised as something solid enough to push against. Time had no shape. It might mean an hour, a week, or the moment in which both girls discovered that life became simpler when Harry was not standing between them, wanting more than he knew how to name.

The argument returned whenever he closed his eyes, though exhaustion no longer preserved it in order. Hermione stood at the foot of the staircase with one hand crushing blue fabric; the green wardstone in Draconia's hair caught the light before she descended towards the dungeons. Then came Luna's uncomplicated kindness, Krum listening, Blaise offering his hand. Bits of the evening slipped past one another, each acquiring a significance Harry had failed to understand while there was still time to do something with it.

I wanted you to ask.

I did want to ask you.

That should have comforted him. Instead, it revealed the precise shape of what they had missed. No rival had stolen the evening from them. No rule, article or Tournament official had forced the three of them apart; they had stood within reach of what they wanted and waited for someone else to become brave first.

Harry was ashamed of the hurt he had caused. He was also angry that Hermione and Draconia had demanded a courage neither had managed to summon, and the feelings refused to cancel one another. Guilt did not make him less wounded; being wounded did not make him innocent. Both truths sat beneath his ribs, making breathing feel like an argument he could neither win nor leave.

Footsteps entered the corridor, and Harry went still. They stopped outside the classroom, close enough for him to hear the quiet movement of fabric and the faint clink of crockery being lowered onto stone. No one tried the handle. No spell touched the lock.

"We are not coming in," Hermione said through the door.

Harry watched the bolt.

Draconia's voice followed, nearer to the floor. "There is tea. It will be dreadful by the time you decide accepting it does not constitute surrender."

"It is already dreadful," Hermione said. "The kitchens were busy."

"That was an observation, not an accusation."

"You looked at the cup accusingly."

"Tea should possess standards."

For one dangerous moment, Harry nearly smiled. The expression failed before it formed, leaving him with the ache of having recognised something familiar and been unable to answer it. Silence gathered on both sides of the door.

"You don't have to speak to us," Hermione said. "But please eat something."

Harry lowered his hands. He could picture her kneeling in the corridor, hair still half pinned from the Ball because removing the last of it would have required admitting the night was over. Draconia would be beside her, perhaps with a silver leaf still caught near the back of her head, sitting very straight whenever she wanted to appear less frightened than she was. They would both be tired. Hermione would be worrying a loose corner of the Marauder's Map between her fingers, looking at the small inked name on its parchment instead of the door before her.

He knew them too well to open it, and not well enough to understand why.

After a while, fabric shifted.

"Sirius knows where you are," Hermione said.

Harry's gaze moved to the pocket containing the mirror.

"We told him you were safe," Draconia added. "Do not make liars of us."

Their footsteps retreated. Harry listened until the corridor had forgotten them, then waited another ten minutes before sliding back the bolt. The small scrape sounded unnaturally loud in the empty classroom.

A tray stood outside, bearing covered bowls of porridge and stewed apples. Toast had been wrapped in a napkin to preserve its warmth, although the cloth was already cooling, and beside the teapot lay a folded square of parchment. Harry stared at it long enough for the moving staircase at the end of the corridor to complete an entire journey and return. Eventually, he picked up the note.

The first line was Hermione's.

This is breakfast, not an attempt to force a conversation.

Beneath it, Draconia had added:

It is also not optional.

Hermione had written again.

Technically, it is.

You are undermining the message.

I am preserving accuracy.

At the bottom, in smaller writing, were four words with neither correction nor reply.

We are still here.

Harry sat in the doorway with the tray across his legs. He ate half the porridge after it went cold, working through each mouthful without much awareness of the taste. The toast required less effort, so he finished one slice and tucked the rest inside his pocket. A thin skin had formed across the tea. He drank it anyway, then folded the note twice and placed it between the pages of his clue notebook.

He told himself it was because throwing parchment onto a classroom floor would create litter. The lie was familiar enough to keep.

Harry returned towards Gryffindor Tower shortly before ten, when most of the castle had woken and enough students filled the corridors for him to become one face among many. The remains of the Ball were everywhere: pine needles patterned the Entrance Hall, enchanted snow continued falling beneath the Great Hall ceiling despite sunlight brightening the real sky, and two suits of armour slept against the same pillar, their helmets tilted together as they snored through a badly harmonised waltz.

Students moved more slowly than usual. Several wore yesterday's clothes beneath winter cloaks; others had abandoned dignity entirely and arrived at breakfast in dressing gowns. Every table hummed with unfinished stories about who had danced with whom, who had disappeared into the rose garden, and which Durmstrang student had attempted to climb the marble staircase after mistaking a decorative banister for a dragon. Harry kept his eyes on the flagstones, allowing the voices to blur into noise.

He had nearly reached the central staircase when Cedric descended from the Hufflepuff corridor carrying two mugs. The bruise beneath his jaw had faded since the First Task, though a faint red mark remained near his temple where one of the Short-Snout's spines had caught him. It was visible now because his hair refused to lie flat. He looked offensively rested until Harry noticed the shadows beneath his eyes.

"Morning," Cedric said.

"Is it?"

Cedric glanced towards the windows. "The evidence is persuasive."

Harry stepped aside to let him pass, but Cedric merely held out one of the mugs.

"Tea."

"I've had some."

"This one's warm."

Harry looked at it without reaching.

"You look like you fought the Horntail again," Cedric said. "And lost on points."

"That would require judges to agree on a scoring system."

"There he is."

Harry accepted the mug because refusal would turn the exchange into concern, and concern was harder to escape than tea. It smelled faintly of mint. Cedric leaned against the banister while students passed around them, leaving Harry room to drink without making the pause feel like an examination. Across the Entrance Hall, Cho Chang appeared with two Ravenclaw friends and met Cedric's gaze. She smiled, small and private, and his entire face answered before he seemed aware of it.

Harry looked into the tea.

"Last night go all right?" Cedric asked.

"Parts of it."

"That sounds ominous."

"How was yours?"

"Cho only stepped on me twice."

"You stepped on her four times."

Cedric's eyebrows rose.

"I was near the dance floor," Harry said.

"You were dancing."

"That explains why the floor kept moving."

Cedric smiled, although the expression did not quite distract him from Harry's face.

"You want to talk about it?"

"Desperately. Just not with another person."

"Fair."

The answer held no offence. Cedric drank from his own mug and looked out across the Hall while Harry waited for the question to return in a more careful shape. It did not.

"How's the egg?" Cedric asked instead.

Harry's fingers tightened around the warm ceramic. "Loud."

"They're good at that."

"You've solved yours."

Cedric's pause was brief, but it existed. "I've made progress."

"That means yes."

"It means I'm not discussing it in an Entrance Hall full of people who would sell either of us to the Prophet for three Sickles."

Harry glanced towards a group of second-years pretending not to listen. One walked directly into a pillar.

"Good point."

Cedric straightened. "Don't leave it too late."

"The task is in February."

"That is what I told myself about the dragon until I learned dragons were involved."

Harry thought of the unused classroom, the closed egg and the notebook containing a single instruction he had yet to follow properly.

Full immersion.

"I'm working on it."

Cedric studied him. Harry held his gaze until the older boy looked away first, perhaps unconvinced but willing to leave him the dignity of the lie.

"Keep the mug," Cedric said.

"Why?"

"You seem emotionally attached."

Harry realised he was gripping it with both hands. "I'll return it."

"No rush."

Cedric crossed the Entrance Hall to join Cho near the doors. Harry remained at the staircase, pressing the warm mug between his palms, and finally looked towards the Great Hall. Hermione sat at the Gryffindor table with an untouched plate before her. She had removed the pins from her hair, which fell in tired waves around her shoulders while she read a book without turning the page.

Draconia occupied her usual place at the Slytherin table. The distance between them measured most of the Hall, yet when Hermione looked up, Draconia was already watching her. Neither looked towards Harry. He climbed the stairs before either could.

Respecting distance proved easier than surviving it.

During the remaining days of the holiday, Hermione did not wait beside Harry's usual chair in the library, and Draconia stopped appearing near the dungeon staircase when he returned from the lower corridors. Neither cornered him after meals, demanded progress on the egg, or asked why the shadows beneath his eyes continued to deepen. They gave him the space he thought they wanted, although they did not abandon him. That would have been simpler to understand.

Books appeared outside the unused Charms classroom with relevant chapters marked by strips of parchment. A tray arrived at midday, then stopped appearing after Harry returned too much of it untouched; from then on, smaller things found him instead. There was an apple beside his Transfiguration notes, half a sandwich tucked into the pocket of a robe he had left near the Potions classroom, and a wrapped biscuit balanced on the golden egg. Hermione's accompanying note explained that crumbs were preferable to collapse.

A fresh phial of burn salve rested beside his bed on the morning he forgot to apply the old one. No message identified its source, but its ingredients were expensive and precisely balanced, and it smelled faintly of Draconia's satchel. Concern reached him everywhere. The girls themselves did not, leaving him to decide what that distinction meant whenever he found something new.

On the second afternoon, Harry entered the library early enough to see Hermione place three inkpots on their usual table. Her hand paused above the third. She looked at the empty chair, removed the inkpot and carried it to a table near the windows instead. Harry left before she could notice him, with the image of that small, deliberate movement following him into the corridor.

Coming back from the dungeons the following day, he found Draconia beside the staircase, one hand resting on the strap of her satchel. His body recognised the arrangement before his mind did. For years, he had finished packing after Potions while she pretended not to be waiting, then climbed with her until their routes divided towards Gryffindor and Slytherin. This time, she saw him approach and straightened.

Harry slowed. For one breath, neither moved; then Pansy called Draconia's name from the passage behind her, and Draconia glanced back. Harry used the moment to take the opposite staircase. He told himself she had been waiting for Pansy. Whatever Draconia decided the encounter meant, he could not know, which allowed him to choose the explanation that hurt most.

He never acknowledged the care behind any of it. He returned what little crockery appeared, stacked the books neatly outside the classroom once he had finished them, and used the salve. Gratitude remained easier when no one witnessed it.

The mirror brightened every evening. Harry turned it face down the first night; on the second, Sirius called until the silver glass grew hot enough against the bedside table to wake Neville.

"Is that meant to be doing that?" Neville asked from behind his curtains.

"Probably."

"Should we evacuate?"

"Not unless it starts swearing."

The mirror produced a muffled, unmistakable obscenity. Neville drew his curtains closed again.

"Good luck."

Harry carried the mirror to an empty classroom and answered it. Sirius appeared at once, close enough to the glass that Harry had the uncomfortable impression he had been waiting without looking away.

"You are alive."

"Hermione told you I was."

"Hermione has also told me that a person can be alive and profoundly unwell. She included references."

The cottage sitting room lay behind him, its firelight warming shelves crowded with objects Sirius had not yet decided how to arrange. The edge of Remus's shoulder was visible in an armchair, a book open across his knee. Harry sat on a desk and tried not to look too closely at the room he wanted to be in.

"I needed a day."

"You've had two."

"Very efficient counting."

"I learned in prison."

Harry flinched, and Sirius's expression changed immediately.

"That was not a comparison," he said. "It was a poor joke."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Harry looked towards the empty blackboard. Someone had drawn three linked circles in the chalk dust, probably the remains of a Transfiguration diagram. One had been rubbed away more thoroughly than the others, although its outline persisted if he looked at it from the right angle.

"Are you angry?" he asked.

Sirius did not pretend to misunderstand. "At you?"

Harry nodded.

"No."

"Disappointed?"

"That you hurt two people you love?" Sirius considered. "I wish you hadn't. That is not the same as being disappointed in you."

The word love felt no smaller the second time. It seemed to enter the classroom and touch every object Harry had arranged to resemble solitude: the single chair pulled from its desk, the bolted door, the egg tucked beneath a blanket. He tightened his grip on the mirror.

"Don't call it that."

Sirius's face remained steady. "Which part?"

"You know which part."

"I know which word frightened you."

"It can mean too many things."

"Yes." Sirius did not rescue him from the admission. "That is why most people find it inconvenient."

Harry returned his gaze to the chalk circles. "I don't know what kind it is."

"Then don't decide today."

"You just decided for me."

"No. I said they are people you love. You are the one who heard a particular meaning."

Heat reached Harry's face. Sirius possessed the decency not to smile.

"Call it caring, if you prefer," he said.

"That sounds smaller."

"Interesting."

"Shut up."

"There you are."

Harry stared at the frame. "I told them."

"Hermione mentioned that part."

"Everything?"

"Enough to explain why she sounded as though she wanted to cry and organise a tribunal at the same time."

Despite himself, Harry's mouth moved, and Sirius caught that almost-smile too. It vanished as quickly as the first.

"Cedric said that."

"Diggory is stealing my material."

Remus turned a page behind him. "You have material?"

Sirius shifted the mirror so Remus disappeared, then returned his attention to Harry.

"Did you mean it?"

"What?"

"That you wanted to ask both of them."

Harry wished he had remained silent. Then he remembered that silence was what had created the problem, and looked down at his hands instead of searching for another way around the question.

"Yes," he said. "Anything beyond that, I don't know."

"Then the truth was not the mistake."

"The timing was."

"Spectacularly."

"Thank you."

"You seem surrounded by people who soften things until you can mistake comfort for uncertainty. I am providing balance."

Harry watched the fire behind him, following a spark until it disappeared up the chimney.

"They asked for time."

"So I heard."

"I'm giving it to them."

"Are you?"

Harry looked back. Sirius rested his forearms on the table beneath the mirror. His hair was tied away from his face, revealing a line at one temple Harry did not remember from summer. Freedom had returned weight to him without erasing Azkaban. Some nights the mirror opened on a room blazing with every lamp because Sirius could not bear the dark; on others, he sat with only the fire and listened more than he spoke. Tonight, he was watching Harry with a patience that left nowhere to hide.

"You are leaving plates outside doors," Sirius said. "You are avoiding meals. You have moved your egg research into an empty classroom. That is not giving them time. That is turning yourself into a punishment."

"They don't have to see me."

"Exactly."

Harry set the bottom edge of the mirror against his knee.

"I don't know what they want."

"They may not know yet."

"That's helpful."

"It is true." Sirius's voice gentled. "Harry, time is not a test you pass by suffering convincingly."

The room became very quiet. Harry looked at the linked chalk circles, at the place where someone had tried to rub one out and failed.

"I hurt them."

"Yes."

"If I go back to normal, it's like I don't care."

"There is a significant distance between pretending nothing happened and deciding you no longer deserve breakfast."

"I eat."

"Toast is not a moral achievement."

Harry almost told him about the porridge, then realised that would make the defence worse. From beyond the mirror, Remus spoke.

"May I?"

Sirius glanced aside. Harry nodded before he could decide against it, and Remus entered the glass to sit beside Sirius. Tiredness made his face appear narrower; a healing scratch crossed one cheek from the last full moon. He settled himself without attempting the reassuring smile Harry had already prepared to resist.

"When someone asks for room," he said, "you do not have to fill the room with your absence."

Harry examined the frame. "Did Hermione give both of you a speech?"

"No," Sirius said. "She gave us evidence."

"Much worse," Remus agreed.

Warmth entered the ache, making it harder to hold. Harry changed the subject before it could loosen anything he was not ready to explain.

"What was in the message Crouch sent Percy?"

Neither man missed the movement. They allowed it anyway, and Sirius leaned back.

"An explanation for his continued absence. He claims his illness worsened over Christmas and formally appointed Percy to represent him for the remaining preparations."

"Was it real?"

"The seal was."

"And the writing," Remus added. "The phrasing was unusually brief, but not impossible."

Harry remembered Percy reading the parchment beneath the floating mistletoe, pride and confusion competing across his face.

"Could Crouch be forced to write it?"

"Yes," Sirius said. "Or ill. Or hiding. Or attempting to place an ambitious junior clerk exactly where someone else requires him."

"That isn't reassuring."

"It was not designed to be."

"Winky?"

"Still at Hawthorn Cottage," Remus said. "Still unable to speak clearly about her former household. We have stopped asking direct questions unless she invites them."

Sirius's gaze moved briefly beyond the mirror, towards the staircase Harry could not see.

"Dobby brings messages from the Hogwarts elves," he added. "Mostly assurances that dismissal ended Crouch's legal authority over her. She listens. I'm not certain she believes them."

"The note you and Hermione sent is beside her bed," Remus said. "She has not answered it, but she has not put it away."

Harry thought of the elf twisting the tea towel between her hands, the effort it had cost her to let even a few words escape.

Young master.

"And Moody?"

Sirius's mouth hardened. "Remains Mad-Eye Moody according to every charm Dumbledore is willing to cast upon an old friend."

"That isn't the same as innocent."

"No."

The answer came too quickly for comfort.

"Be careful around him," Sirius said. "Do not accuse him. Do not investigate alone. If he gives you information, ask why he possesses it."

Harry looked towards the golden egg beneath its blanket at the foot of the desk.

"All right."

Sirius watched him. "That agreement was suspiciously easy."

"I can argue if it helps."

"Tomorrow. After breakfast."

"You're obsessed."

"I recently acquired a godson. The instructions were incomplete."

Harry looked down before the expression on his face became visible. Sirius saw it anyway, but this time he let Harry have the moment without comment.

"Answer tomorrow," he said. "Even if you don't want to talk."

"What would be the point?"

"I will see you refuse."

It was ridiculous. It was also different from a closed bedroom door, where silence had once allowed the Dursleys to imagine that anything occurring on the other side did not concern them.

"All right."

Sirius remained until Harry promised to return to the dormitory. When the mirror darkened, Harry sat alone for another minute, listening to the small sounds of the castle around him. Then he picked up the golden egg and left the classroom before isolation could become an accident he called a decision.

The Christmas holiday ended beneath a storm. Rain struck the castle sideways, turning the snow on the grounds into grey, collapsing ridges, while carriages laboured up from Hogsmeade through mud deep enough to swallow their wheels. By evening, the quiet corridors filled with trunks, wet cloaks and students attempting to tell three weeks of stories before the first bell. Hogwarts returned to itself. The trio did not.

Hermione saw Harry the moment he entered the Great Hall on the first morning of term and forced herself to continue buttering toast. He paused inside the doors as though the noise had struck him physically, then chose a place beside Neville, halfway down the Gryffindor table and four seats from where she sat. He was neither opposite her nor far enough away to be accused of avoidance. Somehow, the care with which he had placed himself hurt more than an empty chair would have done.

He looked exhausted. The swelling around his eyes had faded, but the skin beneath them remained bruised with sleeplessness, and his jumper hung more loosely than it had before Christmas. When Neville offered him eggs, Harry took one and moved it around his plate until the yolk became a yellow smear. Hermione lowered her gaze to her book and read the same sentence four times.

"You are holding the book upside down," Ginny said.

Hermione lowered it. "No, I'm not."

Ginny leaned across the table and rotated it beneath Hermione's hands.

"The chapter heading was at the bottom."

"An unusual printing choice."

Ginny followed her gaze towards Harry. "Are you three fighting?"

Hermione's first instinct was denial. The trio did not conduct their worst moments for public examination; they had defended Draconia from her House, Harry from the Prophet, and one another from questions asked with more curiosity than care. Ginny's concern, however, contained no appetite, and Hermione could not quite bring herself to dismiss it.

"Not exactly."

"That means yes."

"It means the situation is private."

Ginny considered Harry's untouched breakfast. "He looks awful."

"I know."

Across the Hall, Draconia sat between Daphne and an empty chair while Pansy spoke near her ear without receiving an answer. Draconia watched Harry lift a cup, discover it was empty and set it down without refilling it. Then her gaze found Hermione's. They had agreed not to speak for him; they had not agreed to stop speaking to each other.

Hermione closed the book.

"If Professor McGonagall asks, I went to the library."

"Before lessons?" Ginny asked.

"Especially then."

She left through the side door, feeling Ginny's gaze remain on her until she passed out of sight. Draconia joined her four minutes later beneath the covered passage outside the library. Rain lashed the courtyard beyond the arches, and wind drove cold spray across the flagstones, carrying the smell of wet earth and smoke from the greenhouses.

"He ate nothing," Hermione said.

Draconia's expression sharpened. "He ate toast yesterday."

"One piece."

"And half the soup at dinner."

Hermione looked at her.

Draconia folded her arms. "I was not watching him."

"Of course not."

"You counted bites."

"Only because there were so few."

Rain drummed on the stone roof. Across the courtyard, students hurried beneath the opposite arches with bags held above their heads. A first-year lost three rolls of parchment to the wind and chased them between the pillars while his friend laughed hard enough to become useless. Hermione watched the ordinary disaster until it passed, grateful for something that required no decision from her.

"Are we doing the right thing?" she asked.

Draconia did not answer at once. During the first two days after the Ball, she had defended the distance more fiercely than Hermione, and Hermione understood why. Hurt required room. Harry could not confess a feeling large enough to change all three of them and then expect an immediate answer simply because uncertainty frightened him. On the third day, however, Hermione had caught a Slytherin first-year carrying burn salve towards Gryffindor Tower and extracted the truth in under a minute. Draconia had paid him in Sugar Quills.

"We asked for time," Draconia said.

"He hears abandonment."

"He hears abandonment when a door closes too loudly."

"Which we knew."

Draconia looked towards the courtyard. "Knowing why someone is hurt does not remove their capacity to hurt you."

Hermione's throat tightened. "I know."

"Do you?"

The question was not cruel. That made it worse. Hermione leaned against the cold pillar, letting the stone press through her robes while she searched for an answer that would not simply make her sound kinder than she had been.

"Part of me wanted him to suffer," she admitted. "Not like this. I wanted him to miss us enough to understand what he did."

Draconia's face did not change. "So did I."

Hermione looked at her, and Draconia met her eyes without flinching.

"I wanted him to notice every place we were not standing," she said. "I wanted him to understand that fear is not an excuse reserved for Harry Potter. We were afraid as well. He took all three silences and made them entirely his fault, as though even our mistakes must belong to him."

"That sounds like him."

"It sounds like all of us."

Hermione exhaled. Rain struck the courtyard hard enough to blur its opposite wall, turning the students beyond it into shifting shapes beneath the arches.

"When you danced with him," she said, "I hated you."

Draconia went very still. The words sounded appalling once released, but Hermione left them where they were. Taking them back would not make them less true.

"Only for a minute," she continued. "Perhaps two. You knew how he moved. He looked comfortable with you in a way he had not looked with Luna or me. I thought that if he had asked you first, the entire evening would have belonged to you and I would have been expected to smile because you were both happy."

Draconia looked beyond the arch, where a rain-darkened tree bowed beneath the wind, its bare branches reaching towards the windows.

"When you danced with him," she said at last, "he forgot the room."

Hermione's breath caught.

"I had spent weeks teaching him," Draconia continued. "He counted every step with me. Watched his feet. Apologised whenever he stood too close. Then you placed your hand upon his shoulder and he forgot there were steps to remember."

"He looked for you."

"Not until the dance was almost over."

The admission carried no accusation Hermione could answer. Pleasure rose before guilt smothered it, and that, more than anything, made her understand how easily affection could become cruel when treated as a contest. She had wanted to be seen that way. Hearing that she had been seen did not stop it hurting Draconia.

"Did you want him to ask me?" Hermione asked.

Draconia's eyes returned to hers. "No."

The honesty hurt. Hermione preferred it to kindness.

"Did you want him not to?"

Draconia took longer. "No."

Hermione nodded slowly.

Draconia's chin lifted. "Did you want him to ask me?"

"No."

"Did you want him not to?"

Hermione watched water spill from the gutter in a silver sheet. "No."

There it was: the impossible centre none of them had been brave enough to name before Christmas. Hermione did not want Harry to choose Draconia. She also could not bear the thought of Draconia discovering that four years of loyalty, fear and tenderness had been building towards a door that would close in her face. Perhaps Harry had stood before the same door and decided that refusing to touch the handle counted as mercy.

It did not.

"He meant it," Draconia said.

Hermione did not ask what she meant. "Both."

"Yes."

"Do you know what to do with that?"

"No." Draconia's voice remained level, but one hand tightened against her sleeve. "Do you?"

"No."

"Would you prefer he had lied?"

Hermione thought of Harry on the landing, frightened by his own answer and still forcing himself to finish it.

"No."

Draconia's mouth twisted faintly. "We have achieved an impressive number of conclusions beginning with that word."

"It is the only answer I trust at present."

The confession settled between them, neither clean enough to become forgiveness nor anywhere near simple enough to become a plan. It did, however, remove the pretence that their concern for one another erased the rivalry beneath it. Hermione cared about Draconia. She also wanted things Draconia wanted. Both truths could occupy the same body without behaving politely.

"Whatever happens," Hermione said, "we cannot punish each other for what Harry has not decided."

Draconia regarded her. "And when he does decide?"

Hermione had no noble answer prepared. "Ask me then."

Something like approval moved through Draconia's expression.

"Acceptable."

"He answered Sirius," Hermione said.

"I know."

"Did Sirius call you too?"

"Mother did. Sirius called Mother. Father objected to being excluded from what he termed a developing emotional emergency. Mother reminded him that he believed affection could be managed through contracts until June."

Hermione tried not to smile. "How did he take that?"

"He went to his study and drafted a letter to Harry."

"Oh no."

"Mother burned it."

The laugh escaped before Hermione could stop it, and Draconia's mouth softened in response. It was the first time either had laughed since the Ball. The sound ended quickly, but not painfully; for a moment, the courtyard felt less cold.

"We need rules," Hermione said.

"Your answer to distress remains alarmingly consistent."

"A boundary, then. We give him room, but not enough to harm himself inside it."

Draconia considered. "Two missed meals."

"One full day without sleep."

"Difficult to verify."

"His eyes turn red at the edges."

"You have criteria for this?"

Hermione ignored her. "If he disappears from the Map for more than an hour—"

"The Map cannot follow him outside the grounds."

"Then leaving the grounds counts automatically."

"Agreed."

"If his schoolwork deteriorates."

Draconia lifted an eyebrow. "You intend to diagnose emotional collapse through essay marks?"

"It is reliable."

"For you."

They argued through the first bell and arrived late to Arithmancy and Ancient Runes respectively. Hermione carried the uncomfortable knowledge that they had agreed on the point at which to intervene without quite deciding how either of them would manage it.

By the second week of January, Harry had become excellent at appearing functional. He attended lessons, completed assignments and answered when professors called on him. He slept often enough that the nightmares did not enter daylight, and if he kept his eyes on the board, there was rarely anything in his behaviour a teacher could question.

He also learned the least crowded times for every meal, which staircases offered views of approaching students, and how long a person could remain beneath the covered bridge before the cold in his fingers became pain. The answer to the last question was thirty-seven minutes. Harry knew because he had timed it, watching the hands of his watch until the numbers gave him something definite to think about.

Solitude possessed an efficiency friendship did not. No one asked whether he was eating; no one recognised when a joke concealed fear. The golden egg could scream for an hour without caring that his hands shook when he closed it. None of this made him happier, but happiness was not what he had arranged it for.

In Potions, Professor Snape assigned them a Restorative Draught delicate enough to curdle if spoken over too loudly. Harry worked beside Neville after Hermione chose a place with Lavender. The arrangement appeared accidental. Nothing about it was. Across the aisle, Draconia partnered Daphne, and Harry placed his ingredients where he would not have to look beyond them.

For the first twenty minutes, the dungeon contained only the scrape of knives and the soft bubbling of cauldrons. Rain moved green shadows across the high windows; the lake above them had darkened with winter, reducing daylight to a submerged glow. Harry was slicing valerian root when his knife slipped and opened the pad of his thumb.

Neville hissed in sympathy. "You all right?"

"Fine."

Harry wrapped the cut in his handkerchief and continued chopping. Across the aisle, Draconia's pestle stopped. Hermione turned before the sound did, but Harry looked at neither of them.

"Mr Potter."

Snape stood behind him. Harry set down the knife.

"Professor."

"Perhaps you would care to explain why your valerian pieces resemble evidence recovered from a violent scene."

The root lay unevenly across the board, some pieces too thick to dissolve and others reduced to pulp. Normally, an answer would have arrived before caution. Harry looked at the board and found that he had nothing to say beyond the obvious.

"I cut them badly."

Snape waited. When nothing followed, unease moved through the nearest tables: Pansy glanced up, Seamus stopped stirring, and even Neville looked at Harry as though a familiar staircase had carried him somewhere unexpected.

Snape's eyes narrowed. "A rare moment of accurate self-assessment. Begin again."

Harry swept the root into the waste bowl, and the lesson continued. His second batch was precise. The draught remained pearly grey through the addition of hellebore and brightened when Neville stirred clockwise. With three minutes remaining, it possessed the correct consistency and none of the restorative properties.

"We forgot the salamander blood," Neville whispered.

Harry looked at the measured vial beside his elbow. "Right."

There was no time to incorporate it safely. He extinguished the flame while Neville stared at the potion, trying to find a remedy in something they both knew could no longer be saved. Hermione made a tiny movement towards them, and Draconia caught her wrist beneath the level of the desk.

Space.

Snape inspected their failed draught last. He dipped a silver rod into the cauldron, drawing it out with a thread of liquid that clung like paste.

"Impressive," he said softly. "Two students have transformed a Restorative Draught into expensive wall coating."

Neville went pale.

"It was my fault," Harry said.

"I did not ask whose failure deserved commemoration."

"I forgot the blood."

Snape's gaze moved from the untouched vial to Harry's bandaged thumb.

"Ten points from Gryffindor."

No one reacted. Ten points was mercy by Snape's standards.

"Mr Longbottom, you may leave. Potter, remain."

Hermione packed her bag too slowly. Draconia finished before anyone else and did not move, prompting Snape to look towards them both.

"Unless Miss Granger and Miss Malfoy have developed an academic interest in watching me assign detention, I suggest they discover the corridor."

Harry kept his eyes on the failed potion as two bags closed and two sets of footsteps crossed the dungeon. When the door shut, Snape vanished into his storeroom without speaking, leaving Harry beside the table. Steam lifted from abandoned cauldrons and disappeared against the vaulted ceiling. Without the students, the dungeon felt larger, every drip of condensation distinct.

Snape returned carrying a small brown bottle and placed it beside Harry's hand.

"Drink."

Harry examined the liquid. "What is it?"

"An ambitious poison. I thought the mystery might restore your interest."

"Professor—"

"Nutrient solution. Drink it before I discover a disciplinary interpretation of deliberate stupidity."

Harry removed the stopper. The potion tasted of iron, overripe fruit and every vegetable Aunt Petunia had ever boiled past recognition. He swallowed once and nearly stopped, then caught Snape's gaze and finished the bottle.

"Revolting," he said.

"Your gift for analysis survives."

Snape took the empty bottle.

"I ate breakfast."

"Moving food around a plate is not consumption, however convincing the performance appears from the staff table."

Harry's embarrassment turned sharp. "Have Hermione and Draconia started reporting to you?"

Snape's face became very still.

"Miss Granger would rather swallow her timetable than request my assistance. Miss Malfoy understands that I am capable of observing a classroom without a written report."

Harry folded the stained handkerchief over his thumb, hiding the place where blood had begun to show through.

"I'm handling it."

"Poorly."

"I'm still doing the work."

"You produced paste."

"Once."

"You sliced your hand and continued as though bleeding into potion ingredients were an innovative technique. You accepted the loss of points without argument. Last week, you allowed Mr Zabini to describe your flying as adequate and did not hex him."

"You would have taken points if I did."

"I would have taken fewer than this behaviour warrants."

Snape set the empty bottle on his desk, carefully enough that its base made no sound against the wood.

"You have not acquired restraint, Potter. You have lost the spark required to care what follows."

Harry looked at him. The anger he expected was absent. Snape's contempt possessed its usual architecture, but something uneasy lived behind it, something Harry could not fit into the conversation he had thought they were having.

"What do you want me to say?" he asked.

"Nothing. I want you to remember that indifference is not discipline."

Harry laughed without humour. "I'm still here. That's enough."

Snape's expression altered. For one second, Harry saw not a professor but the man who had stepped between students and a transformed werewolf; who loved Draconia through insults because kindness had once become evidence against him; who had spent years surviving choices that never stopped demanding payment.

"No," Snape said quietly. "It is the minimum."

Harry's throat closed. Before he could find anything to do with that, Snape moved to the classroom door and opened it. Hermione and Draconia stood in the corridor. Neither had attempted discretion this time.

Snape looked at them, then at Harry.

"Professor Sprout requires the fourth-year restorative stores inventoried before Friday," he said. "You will report here tomorrow at seven and complete the work."

Harry understood the detention for what it was: an hour in a room where Snape could observe whether he remained alive without admitting concern.

"Yes, Professor."

He stepped into the corridor. Hermione's gaze dropped to his bandaged hand; Draconia noticed the empty brown bottle Snape still carried. Neither asked, and Harry walked between them towards the stairs.

For nineteen steps, they moved together as they once had. Their sleeves nearly touched, Hermione's bag bumped Harry's knee, and Draconia's shoes struck the stone in their familiar measured rhythm. Harry knew each small sound before it came. At the landing, however, the corridor divided: Hermione needed Charms, Draconia the opposite staircase for Ancient Runes, and Harry's next lesson was Divination, three floors above.

They stopped.

"Your hand," Hermione said.

"Knife slipped."

"Did Madam Pomfrey—"

"It's a cut."

Draconia studied his face. Harry could feel the potion warming his empty stomach, making the cold in the corridor suddenly more noticeable.

"I'm fine," he said.

Both girls flinched as though he had chosen a worse word. Harry left before either could answer.

Cedric found him beside the Black Lake three days later. The storm had passed, leaving the grounds iron-hard beneath a colourless sky. Reeds stood frozen along the bank, and the water moved in slow, dark folds, too restless to freeze and too cold to reflect much beyond the broken silhouette of the castle. Harry sat on a flat stone with his clue notebook balanced on one knee and his fingers stiff around the quill.

He had listed every language identified by the translation charm, every environment tested, and every substance through which the egg's sound travelled differently. Water remained the only useful result. The words FULL IMMERSION occupied the centre of the final page, underlined firmly enough to score the parchment.

Beneath them, Harry had written:

Find a larger container.

Hogwarts contained hundreds of baths. Unfortunately, most belonged to dormitories where opening a screaming golden egg underwater would create questions. The prefects possessed a private bathroom somewhere on the fifth floor, but Percy's old descriptions had been too reverent to include directions. Harry had spent more time considering how to find it without asking anyone than he cared to admit.

"You're going to freeze."

He looked up. Cedric stood several feet away wearing his Hufflepuff Quidditch cloak, a broom resting across one shoulder. Mud streaked his boots and one side of his trousers, and his face was pink from the cold.

"You look warm," Harry said.

"I've spent two hours chasing first-years who think a formation is something that happens to other people."

"Leadership suits you."

"Cruelty doesn't." Cedric lowered the broom. "May I?"

Harry shifted enough to create space on the stone. Cedric sat beside him, settling the broom across his knees; heat rose faintly from the place where his gloved hand had gripped it. Across the lake, something disturbed the surface and disappeared before Harry could identify it, leaving a widening ring among the darker folds of water.

"Cho says you haven't been in the library," Cedric said.

"Cho monitors my reading?"

"Cho notices Hermione occupies one table, Malfoy another, and you've developed an intimate relationship with abandoned classrooms."

Harry closed the notebook. "People are very interested in where I sit."

"It's a school. Geography is ninety per cent of scandal."

Cedric removed one glove and flexed his reddened fingers.

"I'm not asking what happened."

"You're doing an excellent impression."

"I'm asking whether you want help with the egg."

Harry stared at the water. The cold had entered his shoes some time ago, though he felt it distantly, as something occurring to a part of him he did not currently need.

"Isn't that cheating?"

"You told me about the dragons."

"I told all the champions."

"After telling me first."

"That was fairness."

"So is this."

Harry turned the notebook beneath his hand, tracing its softened corner with his thumb.

"I know water changes it."

"Good."

"I need full immersion."

Cedric smiled. "Better."

"I need somewhere private."

"Perfect."

Harry looked at him. Cedric leaned closer as though sharing a conspiracy.

"Take a bath."

Harry waited. "That is either a clue or criticism."

"Both can be useful."

"I bathe."

"I'm relieved." Cedric stood and offered a hand. "Prefects' bathroom. Fifth floor, fourth door left of the statue of Boris the Bewildered. Password is Pine Fresh."

Harry accepted the hand and allowed Cedric to pull him upright. His knees protested when he straightened; he had not realised how long he had been sitting there.

"Pine Fresh?"

"Someone let the Head Boy choose."

"That feels like an abuse of power."

"Wait until you see the taps."

Cedric replaced his glove and collected the broom.

"Go late," he said. "And put your head under before you open it."

Harry looked towards the black water. "What did you hear?"

Cedric's expression sobered. "Enough to wish I hadn't."

"That's reassuring."

"It's meant to help us prepare."

"So did the dragon."

Cedric grimaced. "Fair."

He began walking towards the castle, then stopped a few paces away.

"Harry."

"What?"

"Solving it alone doesn't make the solution more yours."

The remark landed too accurately to be casual. Harry tightened his grip on the notebook.

"Did Cho tell you to say that?"

"No. She told me to be subtle."

"You failed."

"I'm a Seeker. We prefer direct routes."

Cedric walked away across the frozen grass, his broom rocking against his shoulder. Harry remained beside the lake until the cold became impossible to mistake for anything else, then followed at a distance, carrying a set of directions that felt unreasonably like an invitation.

The statue of Boris the Bewildered stood in an alcove on the fifth floor, one stone glove raised to a face carved with permanent concern. Harry found it shortly after midnight. The corridor had emptied half an hour earlier, but he waited beneath his invisibility cloak while two Ravenclaw prefects passed, arguing about the acceptable use of a telescope after curfew. When their voices faded, he counted four doors to the left.

The last appeared no different from the others: dark oak, a brass handle, no sign. Harry removed the cloak and stood before it with the egg tucked beneath one arm.

"Pine Fresh."

Nothing happened. He looked back at Boris.

"Cedric has killed me through embarrassment."

The lock clicked. Harry pushed the door open and discovered a bathroom larger than the Dursleys' ground floor.

White marble covered the walls and floor, veined with silver that moved faintly beneath the surface like moonlight trapped in ice. A sunken pool occupied the centre of the room, deep enough to swim in and wide enough for every Gryffindor prefect Harry had ever known to develop an unreasonable opinion about taps. There were hundreds of them, arranged in three shining rows around its edge, each bearing a coloured jewel in place of a handle.

Mirrors framed in gold stretched from the basins to the ceiling. Above the pool, a chandelier floated without chains, its candles enclosed inside glass spheres; beneath it, the empty water basin caught and multiplied their light. Towels rested in neat white stacks on a heated bench. Harry closed the door and set the golden egg beside the nearest step.

"This is absurd."

His voice returned from the marble in gentler echoes. He turned a sapphire tap, and hot water rushed into the pool carrying blue foam that smelled of rain. A ruby tap produced bubbles the size of apples. Another, fitted with a green stone, released something thick and sparkling that attempted to arrange itself into the shape of a swan. Harry shut that one immediately.

Within minutes, steam softened the mirrors and foam spread across the surface in bright islands. Harry added enough cold water to make the pool survivable, then sat on the edge to watch it fill. His clue notebook waited beside the egg. He had brought his wand, the invisibility cloak, three towels, a recording charm and a knife, because Sirius had taught him that mysteries became less dangerous when one prepared for the possibility that they possessed teeth.

He had not brought Hermione or Draconia. Their absence altered the room more than its size.

Hermione would have tested the taps before allowing him into anything. Draconia would have examined the drain for hostile enchantments and insulted the swan. One of them would hold the egg while the other wrote; Harry would complain about being used as an experimental listener, then put his head underwater first because the idea had been his. He could almost hear the conversation, right down to the point at which both girls would ignore his objection in entirely different ways.

He removed his glasses and set them on the folded cloak. A carved screen stood beside the heated bench, and he changed behind it into plain black swimming shorts, folding his clothes with less care than the towels had received. When he returned to the pool carrying his wand, the room had blurred into light and colour.

Warm water closed around his ankles, knees and waist. Heat found the cold that had remained inside him since the lake and pulled it slowly from his muscles. He sank to his shoulders, rested both arms on the marble edge, and hated how good it felt. For several minutes, he did nothing. No voices carried from the corridor; the taps whispered into the pool, each stream making its own pattern across the surface, while blurred candlelight bent through the steam.

Then Harry took the egg in both hands, drew a breath and submerged. He opened the shell beneath the water.

Music unfolded around him.

Harry nearly released the egg. The shriek was gone, replaced by many voices rising through the pool in a harmony too strange to belong to human throats. Some notes sank low enough to tremble inside his ribs; others threaded through them like silver wire. The melody seemed to arrive from a depth far below the marble floor, patient and cold and beautiful in the way winter stars were beautiful when viewed from somewhere warm.

Words emerged gradually.

Beneath the shore where daylight dies,

we wait beyond the watching skies.

What magic names your heart's great cost

will sleep with us, concealed and lost.

Harry's lungs began to ache, but he remained beneath the surface, gripping the shell more tightly as the voices continued.

When morning marks the second hour,

come through the cold without your power.

One hour we grant to find the way;

the lake keeps those who choose to stay.

The last note stretched through the water. Harry broke the surface gasping, and the open egg screamed. He slammed it shut. Sound struck the marble once, twice, then vanished, leaving him clinging to the pool's edge while water ran into his eyes.

What magic names your heart's great cost.

Not an object. Someone.

The Tournament intended to take a person into the lake.

Harry saw Hermione beneath black water, hair drifting across a face gone still. Draconia was beside her, silver-blonde strands tangled in lakeweed, green light moving across the skin that Tom Riddle had once emptied from within. Then came Sirius behind iron bars and Remus alone beneath a moon he could not escape. The images arrived faster than reason, each one carrying the certainty of something Harry had already failed to prevent.

He pushed away from the edge, but water resisted his legs. For one wild second, the marble pool became the Chamber: deep green light, a body on stone, blood leaving him faster than Fawkes could heal it. His scar burned without Voldemort, and the room narrowed around him. He reached for his wand, struck it with wet fingers and knocked it into the pool, where it disappeared among the foam.

"Brilliant."

His voice shook. Harry forced both feet against the floor and held them there, feeling the solidity beneath his toes. Warm water. Marble. Jewel-handled taps. A chandelier above him, its light broken by steam. January. Hogwarts. He named each thing silently until the room began to return to itself.

Not the Chamber. Not yet the lake.

He crouched, found his wand beside the lowest step, and came up breathing too quickly. For a long time afterwards, he sat on the edge wrapped in two towels, with the closed egg resting within reach and drops slipping from his hair onto the marble.

He wanted the mirror. He wanted Hermione's ruthless certainty that a clue was only information until they decided what to do with it. He wanted Draconia's ability to become angry at danger as though it had committed a social error by approaching them. Wanting them did not shorten the time they had asked for, and he could not decide whether calling now would mean he had respected it only until something frightened him.

Harry dried his hands and opened the notebook. He wrote the song from memory, but the first attempt misplaced two lines and the second preserved the rhyme without preserving the exact words. The recording charm had captured little beyond the rush of water and his own gasping when he surfaced. He submerged three more times, opening the egg only long enough to verify one verse before returning to the air. It was easier when he knew what was coming, though he kept his wand on the step where he could see it.

When he finished, water had wrinkled his fingers and the candles above the pool had burned an inch lower. He underlined four phrases.

BEYOND THE SHORE.

WITHOUT YOUR POWER.

ONE HOUR.

YOUR HEART'S GREAT COST.

Harry stared at the final words. The Cup had accepted four champions because someone had persuaded ancient magic to misunderstand the truth. He did not trust what Tournament magic might decide he valued most. That frightened him more than the lake.

Cedric was waiting outside.

Harry emerged beneath the invisibility cloak, noticed a pair of shoes beside Boris and nearly drew his wand before Cedric spoke.

"If you hex me, I'll deduct points."

Harry pulled off the cloak. Cedric sat against the wall in tartan pyjama trousers, a jumper and a prefect's cloak thrown over both. His hair stood up on one side, and he held a book he had not been reading.

"Have you been here the entire time?" Harry asked.

"No."

"How long?"

"Enough to regret not bringing socks."

Harry looked at Cedric's bare ankles above his shoes. "Why?"

Cedric closed the book. "Because you looked like the clue might make you do something stupid."

"Opening it was the point."

"Not the egg."

Harry's grip tightened on the damp notebook. Cedric rose, his eyes moving over Harry's wet hair, the egg beneath one arm, and whatever he had failed to remove from his face.

"You heard it," he said.

"The lake."

"Yes."

"They're taking someone."

Cedric's expression confirmed what the song had not explained. Harry stepped closer.

"Do you know who?"

"No."

"How do they decide?"

"I don't know."

"Do they volunteer?"

"Harry."

"Do they know?"

Cedric tucked the book beneath one arm.

"I asked Professor Sprout. She said the hostages will be protected by the merpeople and the headmaster's magic."

"That wasn't what I asked."

"It's all she would tell me."

Harry turned away. Boris watched them with stone concern, one hand still pressed to the face of someone who had evidently found the fifth floor troubling long before either champion arrived.

"They used dragons without telling us," Harry said. "They put you in front of one that burned half your cloak. They sent Fleur against a Welsh Green after she drew last. Why should we believe 'protected' means safe?"

"We shouldn't."

The agreement stopped him. Cedric drew his cloak more tightly around himself, but his gaze did not leave Harry's face.

"That's why we solve it," he said. "That's why we prepare until the hour doesn't matter. Then we go down and bring them back."

There was fear in Cedric's face: fear held firmly enough to be useful, without the pretence that a professor's reassurance had made it disappear. Harry recognised it more easily than he would have recognised confidence.

"Who did you think of?" he asked.

Cedric glanced towards the dark windows at the end of the corridor. "My parents first."

"Not Cho?"

"Her too." A faint smile touched his mouth. "Which seems unfair. We've had one date and now a magical artefact may consider her kidnap-worthy."

Harry almost laughed. Cedric's smile faded as he watched him.

"More than one person came to mind for you."

It was not a question. Harry looked down at the notebook, at the water darkening its cover beneath his thumb.

"Yes."

Cedric waited.

"I don't know what that means," Harry said.

"It means you have more than one person you couldn't bear to lose."

"The magic asks for one."

"Then the magic has a limited imagination."

Harry looked at him again. Cedric shrugged beneath his cloak.

"Ancient objects are allowed to be wrong. The Goblet has already proved it."

Harry studied him through the dim corridor, searching for something that would make the kindness easier to understand.

"Did you wait out here because I warned you about the dragons?"

Cedric's brow furrowed. "What?"

"You don't owe me."

"I know." Cedric shifted the unread book beneath his arm. "That is why I'm not calling this repayment."

The answer left Harry with nowhere comfortable to place his suspicion. Cedric glanced towards Boris, whose stone expression suggested bewilderment remained the only sensible response to either of them.

"I waited because you went into a locked room after midnight carrying a clue designed by people who consider dragons an appropriate opening exercise," he said. "And because we're friends, unless you have privately decided otherwise."

Harry looked down at the wet notebook. "I haven't."

"Good. I would hate to have frozen my ankles for an acquaintance."

Footsteps sounded below, and both boys moved at once. Cedric became a prefect conducting rounds; Harry vanished beneath the cloak. Filch climbed into view carrying a lantern and muttering about steam in unauthorised corridors, which prompted Cedric to offer an elaborate explanation concerning a leaking pipe. Harry stayed still while the lantern passed, then followed behind Filch when he left.

At the staircase, Harry slipped the cloak from his head.

"Cedric."

The older boy looked over.

"Thank you."

Cedric nodded, accepting the words without turning them into something Harry would have to repay.

"Start with the Bubble-Head Charm," he said. "And don't practise alone."

Harry went downstairs alone.

Breathing underwater was a problem magic had solved in dozens of ways, all apparently designed for someone more advanced than a fourteen-year-old. The Bubble-Head Charm demanded simultaneous control of pressure, airflow and membrane strength. Human Transfiguration offered gills at the acceptable price of possibly losing the ability to breathe anywhere, while conjured air dissolved too quickly to be useful. Potions required ingredients that matured for six months beneath coastal water, or included warnings such as permanent webbing has occurred in rare instances.

By the last days of January, Harry had filled three pages. He worked before breakfast, between lessons and after curfew, carrying books from one empty room to another as though a change of desk might produce a change of answer. The Second Task waited on the twenty-fourth of February. Fewer than four weeks remained, and the number was small enough now that he counted it whenever he opened the notebook.

His first Bubble-Head Charm formed around his left ear. The second sealed his nose and left his mouth exposed. The third expanded until it resembled an enormous glass pumpkin, lifted three inches above his shoulders and burst loudly enough to bring Professor Flitwick through the classroom door.

"Ambitious," Flitwick said, surveying the wet feathers Harry used to test airflow. "Misguided, but ambitious."

Harry lowered his wand. "How do I stabilise it?"

Flitwick's expression became regretful. "Tournament assistance restrictions prevent me from providing direct instruction."

"Can you provide indirect instruction?"

"I am afraid not."

"Vaguely relevant criticism?"

"Your pressure differential is attempting to inflate your head."

"That is vaguely relevant."

Flitwick's mouth twitched. "Practise with soap bubbles before enclosing your airway, Mr Potter."

He left the door open when he departed. Harry stared at it for a moment. He had taken to closing every door behind him; Hermione left them open whenever she knew he was inside. The corridor's light lay across the floor, interrupting the little space he had made for himself.

Harry continued practising. This time, the membrane lasted forty-two seconds before collapsing.

"That won't be enough."

He spun. Professor Moody stood in the open doorway, his magical eye regarding the room before settling on the bucket, the wet feathers and the reference books stacked across two desks. His ordinary eye examined Harry.

"Planning a swim?"

Harry heard Sirius as clearly as if the mirror had opened beside him.

If he gives you information, ask why he possesses it.

"Why would you think that?" Harry asked.

Moody's scarred mouth shifted. "Because champions don't spend January studying underwater spellwork for recreation."

"You know what the task is."

"I know every Triwizard Tournament held in the past two centuries involved one test of magical adaptability. This castle has a lake. Four champions received eggs whose hinges retain mineral residue after being opened in water."

Harry glanced at the egg. White deposits marked its inner seam, fine enough to escape notice unless someone knew where to look. He had not noticed them himself.

"You've been inspecting the eggs?"

"I inspect everything." Moody entered with his wooden leg striking hard against the floor. "Should try it."

"Sirius says constant vigilance is not a substitute for trust."

"Black spent twelve years trusting the wrong rat."

Anger came fast enough to feel like relief.

"And you spent years turning suspicion into a personality. We all have hobbies."

Moody barked a laugh. "There's the champion."

The phrase felt wrong. Moody sounded pleased to discover Harry angry, as though the response had confirmed something he had come to find. Harry set down his wand, deliberately placing it where his hand could reach it again.

"Did you need something, Professor?"

"Saw light beneath the door."

"You were passing an unused Charms room after curfew?"

"I was passing every room after curfew."

The magical eye rotated towards the corridor, looking through stone. Harry resisted the urge to turn and see what it had found.

"Your charm is unstable," Moody said. "You might master it in twenty-six days. You might not."

"Excellent teaching."

"Teaching's restricted."

"Apparently criticism is not."

Moody came to the table and looked at the open book describing partial transfiguration. For a moment, the only sound was the slow drip of water from a feather into Harry's bucket.

"Wizards are arrogant," Moody said. "Always trying to invent a spell for a problem the natural world solved first."

Harry said nothing.

"Animals breathe beneath water. Plants survive where animals cannot. Herbology library is three corridors east."

There it was: information delivered close enough to an observation that it could deny being advice. Harry left the book where it was rather than reaching for another one.

"Why are you helping me?"

Moody's ordinary eye fixed on him. "Because someone entered a fourteen-year-old into a deadly competition. Whoever they are expects you to reach the end."

"So you're helping them?"

"I'm making certain you survive long enough for them to make a mistake."

"You could tell me who you suspect."

"Could."

"Will you?"

"No."

Moody took one step back.

"Stop letting the people hunting you decide when you deserve allies, Potter."

Harry stared. For one impossible moment, the advice sounded exactly like something Sirius might say. Then Moody left, his uneven footsteps travelling along the corridor, pausing once and continuing. Harry listened until he could no longer hear them before opening his clue notebook to a new page.

He wrote every word Moody had said. Beneath the account, he added:

Why did he know there are twenty-six days?

The answer might have been a calendar. It might also have been something worse, and Harry no longer wanted to be the only person deciding which.

He gathered his books and went to find Hermione and Draconia.

They were in the library alcove. Harry stopped outside it, looking at the table that, for weeks, had belonged to all three of them. Hermione always chose the chair nearest the shelves, where she could reach reference books without standing. Draconia sat facing the entrance because she disliked being approached from behind. Harry occupied whichever space remained and gradually covered most of it with his things, to the increasingly pointed displeasure of both girls.

Now two chairs stood close together on one side. Hermione had an Arithmancy text open beneath one elbow, and Draconia was translating a runic inscription onto parchment divided into columns. Neither was working. Their voices remained low, private and easy in a way Harry had once feared could only exist when he was absent. He could not hear the words, only the familiar rhythm of their conversation.

He nearly left. Then Draconia looked up, and every trace of conversation disappeared from her face. Hermione followed her gaze to where Harry stood beneath the arch, carrying six books, the golden egg and a bucket with three wet feathers protruding from the top.

"I solved the clue," he said.

Madam Pince hissed at him from behind the circulation desk. Harry lowered his voice.

"Mostly."

Hermione's eyes dropped to the bucket. "Why are the feathers wet?"

"Evidence of failure."

"Which failure?" Draconia asked.

"The third one."

He waited, his arms beginning to ache beneath the awkward collection. Neither told him to leave. Harry approached the table and placed the clue notebook between them, carefully settling the egg beside it before putting down the books. The note from Boxing Day remained folded inside the notebook, its edge showing between the pages. Hermione noticed, but said nothing.

Draconia opened to the underwater song. Harry watched both girls read, standing beside the empty chair as Hermione's concentration sharpened line by line and Draconia became more still. When they reached the underlined final phrase, their eyes lifted at the same moment.

"They are taking a hostage," Hermione said.

"That's what Cedric thinks."

"Cedric gave you the clue?"

"He suggested I bathe. It was a difficult conversation."

No one smiled. Draconia closed the notebook, keeping one finger between the pages.

"When?"

"Three nights ago."

Hermione stared at him. "You knew for three nights?"

"I've been researching. Moody found me working tonight and pointed me towards plants. There's something about what he said—"

"You've been researching alone."

"That was the arrangement."

The words emerged harder than Harry intended. Draconia's eyes narrowed.

"No."

"You asked for time."

"We asked for time to understand what you said. We did not request your withdrawal from human civilisation."

"I've been in lessons."

"Your body has attended lessons," Hermione said. "The distinction has become noticeable."

Harry looked from one to the other. Anger rose because anger offered more structure than shame; he could stand behind it without feeling quite so exposed.

"I'm trying to respect what you wanted."

"By nearly failing Potions?" Hermione asked.

"One potion."

"By missing meals?" Draconia said.

"I eat."

"Toast is not a moral achievement."

Harry stared at her. "Sirius said that."

"Then Sirius is occasionally correct."

"You have all been discussing me."

"Constantly," Draconia said. "It is exhausting."

Hermione placed one hand over the clue notebook, preventing Draconia from opening it again.

"Harry, you have turned the space we asked for into evidence that you are sorry."

"I am sorry."

"Then apologise. Listen. Change something. Do not make yourself miserable and expect us to accept misery as restitution."

Harry's jaw tightened. "I didn't expect you to accept anything."

"That is the problem," Draconia said.

The library continued around them. Pages turned, quills scratched, and a ladder rolled along the far shelves under its own power, carrying a student who remained absorbed in the book balanced against its top rung. Their alcove felt separated from all of it by something thinner than privacy and more dangerous. Harry became aware of how tightly he was gripping the empty chair's back.

"I don't know how to be near you while you decide," he said, lowering his voice.

The anger left Hermione first. Draconia's gaze changed more slowly, although she no longer looked as though she were preparing to answer before he had finished.

"Why?" Hermione asked.

"Because I want an answer."

"Of course you do."

"Wanting it feels like pressure."

"Wanting is not taking," she said.

Harry looked at her. Hermione had folded her hands together on the table, her knuckles white beneath the lamplight.

"We are allowed to need time," she continued. "You are allowed to hate waiting. Both can be true without one of us becoming cruel."

"It already feels cruel."

The honesty hurt all three of them. Draconia looked away, and Harry wished he could call the words back before they became another thing he would have to apologise for. Then she spoke.

"Yes," she said. "It does."

Hermione closed her eyes for one breath.

"We should have told you what time meant."

Harry's anger lost some of its shape. "Did you know?"

"No."

"Then what were you meant to tell me?"

"That we didn't know," Hermione replied. "That we were still angry. That seeing you hurt did not make the anger disappear, but neither did it mean we wanted you gone."

Draconia's attention remained on the shelves. "We wanted you to come back without being invited."

The words were so close to everything Harry had wanted before the Ball that none of them could miss it. Hermione gave a pained exhale, looking down at her hands.

"We did it again."

"Apparently waiting in silence remains our shared talent," Draconia said.

Harry looked at the clue notebook, where the edge of their Boxing Day message still protruded between the pages. He had read it often enough to know which line came beneath which, but somehow the final four words had remained easier to keep than to believe.

"I would have come," he said. "If I'd known you wanted me to."

"We wanted you to," Hermione said.

"We were also not ready for you," Draconia added.

"Those are difficult instructions to follow."

"Yes." Her grey eyes found his. "They were unfair ones."

Hermione's fingers tightened around one another.

"We wanted you to miss us. We didn't understand how quickly you would turn missing us into a reason to disappear."

"I wasn't trying to punish you."

"We know," Hermione said. "That does not mean we handled it well."

The apology existed without anyone making it simple by naming it. Harry felt some locked part of him loosen anyway, although his hands remained on the chair and he did not yet know whether sitting would be too much to ask.

"I am still angry," Draconia told him.

"You've been very subtle."

Her mouth almost moved.

"I am also frightened," she said. "By what you want. By what I want. By the possibility that one of us will be chosen and the other will be expected to behave as though she has not lost something."

Harry's anger failed. Hermione looked at Draconia, the hurt in her face changing without disappearing.

"And," Draconia continued, each word more deliberate than the last, "I am furious that even now, after everything we have survived, your first response to hurting us was to decide we would be better without the inconvenience of caring whether you eat."

"I didn't decide that."

"You did not ask."

Harry went still. The words mirrored Sirius so precisely that they found the weakness beneath every defence. Not choosing remained a choice; not asking remained an answer, however carefully he had persuaded himself that he was leaving the decision to someone else.

Hermione's expression softened without losing its hurt.

"You keep trying to protect us by making decisions in rooms we are not permitted to enter," she said. "The Ball. The egg. Now Moody. Probably several other things we haven't discovered yet."

Harry glanced at the bucket. "The feathers weren't dangerous."

"That is not reassuring."

"They were already dead."

Hermione made a strangled sound. Draconia looked at the ceiling, and for the first time in weeks, the shape of them appeared: Hermione horrified by inadequate safety procedure, Draconia asking patience from architecture, and Harry improving a situation by choosing the least helpful available fact.

A laugh caught in his chest. It came out wrong, half breath and half pain, but Hermione heard both parts. She reached across the corner of the table and pulled out the empty chair.

"Sit down."

Harry did. The three of them formed an uneven triangle, with more space between their chairs than before Christmas. It was still little enough for Hermione to reach for his bandaged thumb.

"This happened a week ago," she said.

"It keeps reopening."

"Because the bandage is terrible."

"I did it one-handed."

"You possess another hand."

"The cut one was occupied."

Draconia removed a narrow silver case from her satchel. "Give it to me."

Harry surrendered his hand. She unwound the stained cloth with careful fingers, revealing a shallow cut irritated along the edges. After cleaning it with a charm, she spread a line of dittany across the skin and began wrapping fresh gauze around his thumb. Her touch remained precise. Only the slight pressure of her fingertips against his pulse betrayed anything else.

Hermione had not released his wrist. Harry became aware of the arrangement by degrees: her thumb resting against the inside of his arm, Draconia's fingers supporting his palm, and his own hand suspended between them as though neither trusted the other to keep it steady alone. The familiarity of being cared for sat strangely beside the unfamiliarity of how carefully they were doing it.

Draconia noticed next. Her eyes lifted to Hermione's, and Hermione looked back. Neither withdrew immediately. The silence changed into something uneasy and measuring, too new for Harry to examine without making it disappear. Heat travelled from his wrist to his face.

Hermione released him first and turned to her satchel with unnecessary attention. Harry watched her bent head, then looked back at the fresh gauze.

"I used the salve," he said.

Draconia did not look up. "What salve?"

"The unidentified expensive one which appeared beside my bed."

"A mystery."

"Smelled like your bag."

"That sounds more concerning than you intend."

Hermione produced a wrapped sandwich from her satchel. Harry stared at it.

"Do you carry emergency food now?"

"Only since you became an emergency."

Draconia secured the bandage. "Eat."

"You two are alarmingly similar when united by tyranny."

"Eat," Hermione repeated.

Harry took the sandwich. It contained roast beef, mustard and enough watercress to qualify as a small hedge, and he ate half while Hermione examined the books he had brought. Draconia turned to his account of Moody's visit. Neither commented on every bite, which made finishing easier; he could look at the pages before them rather than feel himself being watched.

"He led you towards plants," Hermione said.

"Without naming one."

"He knew the task involved water," Draconia added. "His explanation is plausible."

"Which doesn't make it true."

Her eyes lifted to his. Something quiet passed between them: approval, concern, and the memory of every time Harry had accepted help because danger wore a familiar face.

"You wrote his exact words," she said.

"Sirius told me to."

"Sirius is increasingly correct. I dislike the trend."

Hermione gathered the Herbology volumes into a new stack, examining their titles before moving one to the top.

"Moody may be manipulating you towards a solution."

"I know."

"That does not mean the solution is unusable."

"I know."

She looked at him sharply. Harry raised both hands, sandwich included.

"Not the bad kind of 'I know.' I genuinely know."

"There are categories now?" Draconia asked.

"Apparently."

"We need a list."

Hermione reached for parchment, and Harry laughed. This time, the sound worked, startling all three of them. Madam Pince glared over the top of her desk, and Hermione lowered her voice, although she did not quite manage to remove the warmth from it.

"We help with the task. That does not mean the conversation after the Ball is resolved."

Harry's laugh faded, but the room did not darken with it.

"I understand."

"You are not to interpret research assistance as an answer," Draconia said.

"That sentence is terrifying."

"It was designed by committee."

Hermione ignored them. "And if you need space from us, you say so. You don't disappear."

"All right."

"You attend meals."

"That feels unrelated."

"It is not."

"I had half a sandwich."

"You will have the other half."

Harry looked at Draconia for support. She pushed the sandwich closer.

"Betrayal," he said.

"Nutrition."

He finished it. Then they began.

Hermione built an index of aquatic plants organised by magical effect, her quill moving quickly across a fresh sheet of parchment. Draconia rejected anything requiring tropical water, a lunar harvest or preparation longer than twenty-six days, while Harry searched field guides for species obtainable in Britain without explaining his plans to a suspicious apothecary. Gradually, their books spread across the table, closing the gaps between the separate stacks until Harry could no longer tell which belonged to whom.

The first candidate was sea-spleen, which increased lung capacity but turned skin blue for several weeks.

"Could be worse," Harry said.

"You would clash with your eyes," Draconia replied.

Hermione crossed it out. Marsh bell caused temporary gills in amphibians and permanent sterility in mammals; they crossed that out faster. Black reed allowed a wizard to extract air from water for seven minutes, provided the reed remained unbroken and the water contained no grindylows.

"The lake contains grindylows," Hermione said.

"Of course it does."

The list grew longer without becoming more useful. At half past one, Madam Pince confiscated two empty ink bottles and informed them that champions were not exempt from closing hours. They relocated to the corridor with their books, the bucket and the egg, leaving the table empty beneath her disapproving gaze.

Books formed a wall around their feet. Harry's shoulder rested against one side of the alcove arch, Hermione sat cross-legged opposite him, and Draconia occupied the centre with her back to the stone. It was colder out here, and they had to keep their voices low in the sleeping castle. The arrangement was not what it had been. It was something.

Hermione opened a cracked volume entitled One Hundred Unpleasant Plants and Their Occasional Uses. Several pages clung together before she eased them apart, then stopped with her finger halfway down the text.

"Here."

Harry and Draconia leaned closer. A moving illustration showed a bundle of grey-green stems writhing in the hand of a wizard who appeared to regret touching them. Beneath it, cramped print described a rare Mediterranean plant whose magic produced functional gills and webbing for approximately one hour. The duration varied with water temperature and the freshness of the sample.

"Gillyweed," Harry read.

"Difficult to cultivate," Hermione said. "Almost impossible to preserve once harvested."

Draconia turned the page. "Controlled in Britain because of its use in illegal cross-species transfiguration."

"Can we obtain it?"

"Father could."

Hermione shook her head. "Any Ministry-monitored purchase linked to the Malfoys would be noticed. If Moody wants Harry to use it, we need to know whether he expects someone to supply it."

Harry read the entry again. One hour: exactly the time named in the song. After days of warnings and conditions that made every possibility useless, the simplicity of that number felt almost suspicious.

"We test it first," he said.

Draconia's gaze moved to him. "Not on you."

"I'm the one who has to eat it."

"Testing unknown magical vegetation by consuming it is not research," Hermione said. "It is what happens before a cautionary footnote."

"How else—"

"We find a sample, confirm it with Professor Sprout without revealing the task and perform non-ingestive identification charms," Draconia said. "Then you eat it."

Harry looked between them. "Comforting conclusion."

"You wanted help."

He had. The realisation remained new enough to feel like stepping onto a staircase before knowing where it led. He looked at the book balanced between them, at Hermione's finger holding their place and Draconia's hand keeping the cracked cover from closing. The answer was still dangerous, but it was no longer something he had to hold alone.

Hermione closed the book.

"Breakfast tomorrow."

Harry braced for instruction. When it did not come, he realised she had left room for him to answer.

"Will you sit with me?"

Hermione's face changed. Draconia looked towards the dungeon staircase, then back at him.

"Gryffindor table," she said. "Half past seven."

"You don't have to—"

"Do not ruin this through politeness."

"I was going to say you don't have to arrive that early."

"You were not."

Harry picked up the egg. They divided the books and walked back through the sleeping castle together, their footsteps overlapping in corridors he had grown accustomed to crossing alone. No one apologised again, and no answer was offered. At the dungeon stairs, Draconia hesitated before leaving, as though some older instinct expected Harry to vanish the moment she turned away.

"Half past seven," she repeated.

"I'll be there."

Hermione accompanied him to Gryffindor Tower. They spoke about the Bubble-Head Charm, the water temperature of the lake, and whether an hour of gillyweed effect on land included the time required to reach the shore. The questions were ordinary enough to let them walk without examining every silence. They did not speak about the Ball.

At the portrait hole, Hermione stopped.

"Harry."

"What?"

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Not forever," she said.

The promise had not changed. He had. This time, he heard uncertainty inside it rather than an ending, and could bear the difference long enough to meet her eyes.

"I know," Harry said.

It was the good kind.

The following morning, Hermione saved two places beside her. Harry arrived at twenty-eight minutes past seven, carrying no books and leaving the egg in his dormitory. He paused only long enough to see where she sat before making his way towards her.

Draconia crossed from the Slytherin table at precisely half past, ignoring Pansy's stare and the conversation that weakened behind her as she passed. She took the seat on Harry's other side, placed a book concerning British water plants beside his plate, and reached for the toast. For several minutes, none of them knew how to begin.

The Great Hall carried on without assistance. Owls descended through the rafters, Seamus spilled pumpkin juice, and the usual mixture of conversation and clattering crockery rose towards the enchanted ceiling. At the staff table, Snape looked towards Harry's full plate, then returned to his coffee without visible reaction.

Hermione unfolded the morning post. Draconia spread marmalade across a slice of toast and placed it in front of Harry.

"This feels oppressive," he said.

"It is breakfast."

"The two are not mutually exclusive."

Hermione smiled behind the newspaper. She poured tea into Harry's cup and reached for the honey.

"He doesn't take honey," Draconia said.

Hermione paused. "He did at Hawthorn Cottage."

"He had a cough."

"I still have opinions," Harry said.

Both girls looked at him.

"We are aware," they said together.

They looked at one another. Something passed between them that was not quite challenge and no longer alliance alone. Harry recognised it only because he had seen the beginning of the same thing in the library, when they held his hand between them. Hermione set down the honey, and Draconia pushed the sugar bowl towards him instead.

"Choose," she said.

Harry stared at the bowl. Hermione made a small sound behind the newspaper, while Draconia's eyes widened by a fraction as she heard what she had said.

Harry looked between them. "We may need to retire that word for several weeks."

Hermione laughed first. Draconia attempted to preserve her dignity for three seconds longer, then surrendered with a quiet breath that softened her entire face. The laughter was not large. It did not erase the landing, the separate partners, or the weeks spent learning how efficiently they could hurt one another by trying not to. It was simply the first thing they shared that did not require an apology afterwards.

Harry put sugar in his own tea and ate the toast.

Their knees touched beneath the table, Hermione's on one side and Draconia's on the other. Harry noticed at once. So did they. Hermione's eyes remained on the newspaper, though she had stopped reading, and Draconia turned a page in the book without looking down. Neither moved away. After a moment, Hermione glanced across Harry towards Draconia, who met the look over the rim of her cup.

Neither yielded the small point of contact. Heat travelled slowly into Harry's face, and he took another bite of toast because it provided something to do with his mouth. He discovered, too late, that both girls noticed that as well.

Nothing had been solved except an egg, and even that answer had opened into darker water. Hermione remained frightened of being overlooked; Draconia remained frightened of becoming the choice Harry regretted after seeing Hermione hurt. Harry remained incapable of separating what he felt into anything orderly enough to offer them. But none of them had left the table.

For weeks, Harry had believed distance was the only respectful shape love could take. He was beginning to understand that staying required space too: enough to be hurt, enough to be honest, and enough to return without pretending nothing had changed.

The three of them sat inside that uncertain space while rain began softly against the windows. Between them lay the morning post, a book of water plants, and more than one possible future.

For now, it held.
