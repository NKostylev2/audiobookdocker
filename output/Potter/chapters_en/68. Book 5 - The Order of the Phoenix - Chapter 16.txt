16

Professor Flitwick had borrowed a doll from the Hospital Wing. It lay upon his desk in a knitted bonnet, between a copper measuring frame and a stack of fifth-year essays. One painted eye had been rubbed almost white. Madam Pomfrey used it to demonstrate bandages to younger students; Harry had seen her wrap its wooden arm with considerably more patience than she extended to his actual one.

"It has no magical core," Flitwick explained. "No pulse, and no injury for the charms to detect. We can give the attachment a test signal without hurting anyone."

"I expect it appreciates that," Harry said.

Draconia gave him a look. She stood beside the desk with her grey stone in a shallow glass dish. Harry's green one occupied another, its silver cage catching the early light. Without the cord against his neck, he kept reaching beneath his collar and finding skin. Flitwick had noticed the first time. He had not mentioned the subsequent four. Instead he moved the dishes away from the pile of essays and told Harry where they would remain. Harry appreciated knowing which dish held the green one and where Flitwick would put it afterwards. He could see it from his chair; that did not quite stop his hand returning to his collar.

On Friday, the eighth of March, they met before lessons in the professor's small workroom, where drawers climbed almost to the ceiling and each handle bore a label in handwriting too fine to read from the door. Rain tapped upon the window. Beneath it, a pot of blue flowers turned slowly towards an illuminated globe which supplied the sunlight the weather had withheld. Hermione sat on a stool with the test record. She had drawn the apparatus without decorating it, leaving plenty of space for results which might be disappointing. Flitwick's own notes lay beside hers. Harry could see where he had written observed above one column and not established above the next.

"The doll represents the wearer," the professor continued. "This attachment sends the warning that would ordinarily come from your side of the pair. The doll is only something to fasten it to." He adjusted its bonnet. "Madam Pomfrey would like it back with both arms attached."

He lifted Harry's green stone from its dish and fastened it beside a silver testing clip beneath the doll's bonnet. A thin lead joined the clip to the measuring frame. When he turned the dial, the attachment fed a harmless signal into the green stone's charm. Draconia's grey stone brightened across the desk. She touched the edge of its dish, watching until Flitwick returned the dial to its first mark.

"That's what mine did in January. The light, I mean."

"Yes. The grey stone is receiving the warning from the green stone's side of the pair. Without the intercepting field, it arrived promptly." Flitwick removed the clip and returned Harry's stone to its dish. "What we learnt yesterday afternoon concerns the other arrangement."

He showed them the second record. The grey stone had been placed beyond the small sample Peake had left, separate from the corridor apparatus but charged enough to reproduce its interference. The test signal had stayed in the attachment until Flitwick removed the obstruction. He had checked the same arrangement with the colours reversed and recorded the result beneath it. Harry followed the two lines with one finger, making certain which stone had been on which side. He read the delay twice.

"So the charm on my side could react, and Draco still wouldn't know?"

"In this test, yes. The attachment sent the signal. Miss Malfoy's stone did not receive it until the obstruction was gone. That is what we measured; it doesn't tell us what happened during any earlier incident."

Draconia's gaze stayed upon the numbers. Her hands were still, palms resting on either side of the dish. Harry had seen them shake during the corridor test; seeing the effort she put into keeping them quiet now made him want to take them.

"Would mine look different while it waited?" she asked.

"Not in either of these tests. It looked exactly as it did before the signal."

Hermione's quill stopped. Flitwick let them absorb the answer before returning to his desk.

"A quiet stone can mean nothing has triggered it," Flitwick said. "It can also mean the warning hasn't reached it. I wish I could offer you a way to tell those apart. At present, I cannot."

"Can you make it tell us?" Harry asked.

"I could try making the pair check in regularly. You might then notice a missed signal. But that signal would still have to cross the obstruction, and I would need to test it before promising you anything."

Harry looked at the doll. Its smile had survived years of practice bandages; one arm hung over the edge of the desk, entirely unconcerned by what the numbers meant. He would have liked Flitwick to bring out a different attachment and demonstrate that the problem was gone. The professor was still holding the same clip.

"You could make it shout every ten seconds," he suggested.

"I could. You would then ask me to remove it before lunch."

Draconia almost smiled.

"Before breakfast," she said. "I would like to sleep without my jewellery arguing with me."

The professor put the clip away. He was not offering them a replacement which did everything the previous objects could not. Harry found the restraint both reassuring and deeply annoying. He had hoped that somebody sufficiently clever would repair uncertainty as neatly as Hermione repaired his headphones.

"The stones have helped you before," Flitwick said. "They can still help. Now we know one circumstance in which their warning may not reach you."

Harry glanced at Draconia. She was looking at the grey dish rather than the record.

"I don't want to stop wearing it," she said. "It's warned me before."

"I haven't asked you to. I want you to know what it can do, so you can decide when you need something else as well."

She nodded, slowly. Harry moved his chair enough that his knee touched hers. Neither of them had to explain why they still wanted the familiar weight beneath their collars. Hermione moved her notebook towards Harry. Beneath the diagram she had written three short lines.

A missed answer does not tell us whether someone is safe.

Ask somebody who can check separately; another object inside the same field may fail too.

If the object fails, use another way to get help.

Harry remembered Ron drawing the second position beyond the chalk line. He had been thinking about a corridor with two ends. Outside Hogwarts, a person might not know where the affected area ended. He thought of somebody waiting for a mirror to answer without knowing why it would not. The drawing had made the problem look smaller than that.

"Only three lines?" he asked. "Is the rest on another page?"

Hermione looked towards the flowers.

"Not until we've practised the ones we already have."

He could hear how hard that answer had been to reach. Draconia reached over and nudged the notebook back towards her.

"Three lines," she said. "I may frame the page."

"Then leave room beneath it. I haven't promised there won't be an appendix."

Flitwick returned both stones and went through the results once more. He had cleared the test warnings and checked the fastenings; he had not added the regular signal they had discussed. That would need more work. Draconia asked him to show her the reverse test again, and he did, without hurrying either of them. Harry fastened the green one beneath his shirt. It settled against his skin with the familiar coolness. Draconia watched until the cord lay flat, then slipped her own beneath her collar.

"Will they put the anchors back?" Harry asked.

"Not here without a further test."

"Somewhere else?"

The professor's hands came to rest upon the doll.

"That is a question I have asked in writing."

Harry appreciated the honesty. He would have appreciated a different answer more. At the door, Flitwick called him back.

"Potter. Keep using the mirror. If it doesn't answer, find somebody nearby who can help check. Don't assume that a failed call tells you what has happened at the other end."

"I'll still have Hermione asking whether I've read the instructions."

"I heard that," she said from the corridor.

Flitwick smiled. "That one seems to work through most obstructions."

Harry went after his friends. There were damp footprints outside the classroom, three students arguing over a missing essay and a suit of armour complaining that somebody had put a flower in its visor. The green stone remained quiet against his chest. He knew a little more about what the quiet meant.

That afternoon, Angelina allowed Ron four minutes to explain his idea. Ron looked at the watch, decided she meant it and rubbed one arrow off his board before anybody could ask what it represented. She stood in the changing room with a broom under one arm and a watch in her hand. The walls were lined with hooks, the floor with muddy boots and the benches with Gryffindors attempting to make the afternoon's rain seem less likely by ignoring it. Harry sat beside Ron, who had borrowed a board from the tactics cupboard and drawn six arrows upon it. Three pointed towards the same hoop.

"You aren't listening," Ron told Fred.

"I'm listening," Fred said. "I just don't know which of those arrows is me, or why two of them have gone through George."

"That's why you move."

"Glad we settled that."

George reached over and rotated the board.

"Better."

Ron snatched it back. "That's the ground."

"Good. We were about to defend that," Fred said.

Angelina held out her hand for the board. Neither twin gave it to her; Ron did, with the aggrieved expression of somebody surrendering his only useful possession. She turned it the right way up and passed him the chalk.

"Start with where you want us before the pass. One arrow at a time, Ron. Leave their Chasers out until we know where our own players are."

Harry hid his face in his scarf. Angelina saw him doing it and pointed the watch in his direction.

"You helped him prepare this."

"I told him which bits worried me."

"Then you can provide silence while he finishes."

The idea was less ridiculous than it looked. Ravenclaw's Chasers preferred to force a Keeper sideways, taking advantage of the moment when his body committed to one hoop and his attention abandoned another. Ron wanted the Beaters to close the outside route before the Quaffle reached it, giving him time to cover the middle rather than chase the pass. Harry had watched him rehearse the movement against an empty pitch. Without an audience, Ron trusted his hands. With one, he seemed to remember every occasion on which they had dropped something. Harry had seen him make the same save five times after the others left, then look towards the empty stands as if they might still contain somebody ready to laugh at the sixth.

Angelina asked two questions. Ron answered the first quickly and the second after looking at Harry, who kept his mouth shut. He began again, rubbing out the part he had explained badly. Harry watched the chalk leave a pale smear upon Ron's thumb and made himself stay quiet. Ron knew this. He had worked it out while they were sitting on the changing-room floor after practice, too tired to bother pretending it was simple.

"We'll try it," she said at last. "If it leaves Alicia facing a Bludger alone, we stop."

Ron rubbed out one arrow. "It shouldn't. Fred stays with her until George has the outside covered."

"Then show us. Slowly first."

They took the board outside, propped it against a bench and ran the movement slowly first. Alicia shouted when the outside gap closed too late. George shouted back that he could see it, and Angelina made them try again before the argument acquired a history. On the third attempt, Ron stopped watching the Beaters and waited for the pass itself. The Quaffle struck both palms. He held it.

They practised until the grass became too slippery for safe landings. Harry could feel the Firebolt respond beneath him with the little movements his body had stopped making properly when fear made every action urgent. Above the hoops there was no paper to read and no expression to interpret. Wind pushed from the right; he leaned. A Bludger rose beneath him; he climbed. The ground stayed where it ought to be. He made one low turn through the stands and heard Angelina shout his name.

"I know," he called before she could finish.

"Then stop doing it!"

He rose, grinning, and found Ron waiting near the right hoop.

"She'll remove your legs."

"You'll miss me getting the Snitch."

Ron caught the practice Quaffle against his chest.

"You don't have to prove you can fly every time you go up."

Harry looked at him. The sentence sounded too much like something Remus would say. Ron seemed to realise that and added, hastily, "Everyone knows. You're unbearable about it."

That made answering easier.

"You could try looking pleased."

"With you? It would encourage things."

They came down together. Ron's proposed defence worked twice, failed once and produced an argument between the twins about which of them had understood it first. Angelina accepted the results with a grunt and told everyone to be at breakfast by half past seven. By dinner, Harry's hair had dried badly and his feet had warmed. Hermione looked up from a letter as he sat beside her. There were two envelopes on the table: one addressed to her parents, the other to Neville, although Neville sat ten yards away and could be reached by walking.

"An invitation?" Harry asked.

She put her hand over the second envelope.

"A book list. For the exhibition."

"He's going to study before looking at plants?"

"I said I might enjoy knowing what we were looking at."

Harry glanced towards Neville. He had soil on one cuff and was laughing at something Ginny said. Harry realised he had been treating their table in the library as somewhere Hermione would always be waiting, however many afternoons he arranged elsewhere. He had not meant to expect that. He had still expected it. For a moment he missed the old certainty that he knew where both girls would be. Then Neville laughed, wiping soil off the table before Ginny could object, and Harry was glad Hermione would have an afternoon she wanted too. Hermione followed his glance.

"What?"

"Nothing useful."

"That has rarely prevented you."

He took a roll.

"I'm glad you asked him."

"I haven't. He asked me."

"I'm glad you answered."

Her hand loosened over the envelope. The warmth returned to her face. Harry smiled back, relieved that he could be pleased for her without making a speech about it. She slid the envelope into her bag.

"Thank you," she said.

Ron arrived, sat opposite and asked whether anyone had seen his gloves. Harry lifted them from the bench. They had been beneath him since he came in.

"Keeping them warm," he explained.

Ron took them, checked the damp cuffs and made Harry move along the bench before sitting down. "Next time warm them somewhere I can see them." Hermione returned to her letter. Beneath the table, her foot touched Harry's for a second before she moved it away. He let the contact be what it was, and reached for the potatoes.

By Saturday morning, the ninth of March, the wind had stripped most of the remaining snow from the pitch. Harry could hear it under the changing-room roof while Angelina checked that every player had both gloves. Ron's missing pair had been returned so often during breakfast that Fred now seemed to regard them as team property. Through the narrow changing-room window, Harry could see the remaining snow lying in grey heaps beneath the stands, stained with earth where boots had cut through it. The grass shone wet and dark. Above the mountains, clouds moved in long, broken layers, exposing blue sky just often enough to convince everybody that the next shower belonged to someone else's afternoon. Harry tightened the strap on his left glove and checked that his goggles sat securely over his glasses. The edge of the leather had folded beneath his wrist; he pulled it flat before reaching for the Firebolt. His fingers had remembered the work from yesterday. His stomach had decided to remember every match he had ever played. Ron stood beside him, staring at the doors.

"If you keep doing that, they'll feel threatened," Harry said.

"What?"

"The doors. You've been glaring at them since we came in."

"I'm thinking about the first pass."

"Good. For a moment I thought they'd insulted your gloves."

Ron elbowed him. It hurt enough to improve his concentration. Angelina gave her final instructions without raising her voice. She waited until the twins stopped talking, then looked at Ron long enough to make him look back. Harry listened while tightening the wet end of his glove strap. He knew the instructions. Hearing them here, with the team close around him, helped him stop imagining the match before it began. Fred and George held their bats against their shoulders. Alicia tied her hair again. Katie rolled the Quaffle between her palms until Madam Hooch asked for it back. Then the doors opened, and the noise came in.

Harry walked into a broad wind carrying wet grass, wood smoke and the roar of students who had been waiting for something they were allowed to shout about. Red banners moved above the Gryffindor stand. Blue filled the next. A group of Hufflepuffs had come in scarves of both colours and seemed determined to support anyone who performed something interesting. He found his friends before he meant to look.

Hermione wore the thick coat her mother had sent after Christmas. Draconia stood beside her in Slytherin green, refusing to conceal which House she belonged to merely because she wanted a Gryffindor to win. Luna had tied a red ribbon around the brim of a blue hat. When she saw him, she lifted it; the hat produced a small shower of paper stars. Sirius leaned over the railing behind them. Harry almost stopped walking, then hurried to catch the team before Angelina noticed the gap. His godfather had one foot braced against the lower bar and was waving as though the entire stand belonged to him.

He had known his godfather might come. Knowing was different from finding him there in a scarf which had been knitted badly enough to require affection as an explanation. Sirius waved both hands. Remus stood beside him, lifted one and caught Sirius's elbow before it struck Hermione's hat. Sirius apologised to her without looking away from Harry. She moved a little to one side, clearly prepared for him to do it again. Harry smiled so openly that Fred looked past him to see why.

"There he is," Fred said. "I wondered who you were grinning at. Think he'll shout louder than Lee?"

"I'll tell Angelina you're distracting the Seeker."

"She'll tell me to keep it up. You fly better when you're cross."

They mounted. Madam Hooch released the balls, and the pitch fell away. For the first few minutes, Harry did nothing spectacular. He circled above the game, keeping the sun behind his shoulder when it appeared and the Ravenclaw stand within view when it vanished. Cho flew twenty feet lower, her dark hair gathered beneath a blue ribbon. She glanced up at him, gave a brief smile and turned towards a flicker near the commentators' box. Harry followed without committing to a dive. The flicker belonged to someone's watch. Below, Ravenclaw scored first. The Quaffle passed beyond Ron's left hand and struck the net before he could follow it. Harry saw the red shoulders bunch beneath his robes. He wanted to fly down and say something, but the next attack was already forming. Ron needed to watch that more than he needed Harry beside him.

The goal drew a roar from blue scarves and a furious gesture from Angelina. Ron had moved half a second too early. Harry saw him strike the shaft of his broom with one hand, then force himself back between the hoops. At the next approach, Fred closed the outside lane. The Ravenclaw Chaser checked her pass, and Ron remained central until the Quaffle left her fingers. He caught it cleanly against his chest. Harry heard Lee Jordan's voice carry across the pitch.

"And Weasley holds it! A clean save—perhaps the first was just to keep Ravenclaw interested!"

McGonagall said something which the microphone did not quite catch. Harry grinned and climbed. The next few minutes belonged to the Chasers. Harry followed the Quaffle long enough to see Alicia bring it out of a tight corner, then turned back towards Cho. She had risen while he was looking elsewhere. He matched her height, leaving a little more distance than usual between them and the hoops.

The higher air was colder. It caught the edges of his goggles and drew tears from the corners of his eyes. Beyond the stands, the lake had become a flat sheet of grey, broken where wind pushed against its surface. Hogwarts stood upon its ridge with smoke lifting from three chimneys. He could see the kitchen garden, the greenhouse where Draconia had laughed into her tea, the boathouse where Remus had put a chair in his path and made him look at it. He could even make out a pale line of smoke drifting beyond Hagrid's chimney. Then a Bludger climbed towards his left shoulder.

He rolled, felt it pass close enough to rattle the broom and recovered without looking down for permission to have been frightened. Cho had seen something. She turned sharply beneath him, accelerating towards the Ravenclaw goalposts. Harry dropped after her. Their brooms converged beside the left hoop; its iron rim flashed silver, then disappeared behind her shoulder. The Snitch ran along the underside like a bead shaken free from a necklace. He leaned forward. Cho reached first. Her fingers missed by an inch. The Snitch shot between them, descended through the hoop and vanished against the wet grass. Harry pulled up before following it into the crowded scoring area.

"Sensibly done," Cho called.

"Don't sound surprised."

"I enjoy novelty."

She wheeled away, laughing. Harry rose after her, wondering whether Cedric had been telling her about his previous matches. He would ask later, when accusing somebody could be done without allowing the opposing Seeker to get beneath him.

The rain arrived eighteen minutes into the match. It blew sideways, fine at first, then hard enough to turn robes heavy and make the Quaffle slick. Ravenclaw widened their formation. Gryffindor had to chase the ball further across the pitch, and Ron began losing the fraction of time the revised defence had bought him. With Ravenclaw leading seventy–thirty, Angelina signalled to Alicia beneath the far stand. Katie kept possession, circling away from the nearest Ravenclaw while the other two flew close enough to exchange a few shouted words. The game continued around them; there was time for one adjustment, not a discussion. Harry watched Angelina point towards the outside lane Ron's plan had been intended to close. Her next gesture sent Alicia wider than before. Harry circled while she spoke. The Snitch had not appeared again; Cho watched his movements while pretending to inspect a stretch of empty sky.

He could feel his shoulder tightening. Cold pulled through the muscles around the shoulder that had been injured on the train. Pomfrey had cleared him to play, and the ache was familiar after practice. The difficulty came when he stopped feeling the wind and began remembering stone beneath his back, his arm held where he had not chosen to put it. He changed his grip, flexed his fingers and kept the Firebolt level. Then a gust caught the torn edge of a red banner. It snapped outward below him, the fabric cracking against a post. For half a second he saw a bridge railing, flames along the roof of a carriage and a girl's hand slipping where he had not been able to reach her. His broom dipped.

"Harry!"

Ron's voice came from the hoops. The pitch returned in fragments: polished wood beneath his hands, rain upon the lenses, a Bludger coming from the right. Harry moved too late to avoid it cleanly. The Bludger struck the end of the Firebolt with a crack he felt through the handle, kicking the tail sideways. His right knee slipped. For an instant his weight pulled away from the broom and the wet shaft slid beneath his left palm. He tightened both legs, caught the handle higher up and felt the nose rise sharply as the Firebolt answered the uneven grip. Fred's bat met the ball before it could come back. Harry saw him arrive through the rain, red robes streaming behind the broom, and realised the next blow had already been kept away from him. The sound cut through the rain with satisfying force.

"Up, Harry! I can't keep doing that down here!"

Harry climbed. Nobody stopped the match. Nothing had happened which the stands could recognise beyond a Seeker making an ugly recovery. He reached the clear air above the posts, checked that the Firebolt was responding normally and looked at Ron, who had one hand raised briefly before the next attack required both. Harry lifted his own, then looked at the goalpost behind Ron. Ron was back in place. The red cloth was still tied to the railing; a student reached to hold it down and received a faceful of rain for the effort. Harry let the Firebolt carry a slow circle above them. He could feel the handle again, wet but solid, and the pull of air against both cheeks. Cho came alongside.

"All right?"

He could have said yes quickly enough to end the conversation.

"Need a moment. The broom's answering me."

She nodded and moved away, continuing her search. He watched her go without resenting the advantage. The rules did not promise him a private pause in the middle of a game. He kept above the crowded scoring area and let the next breath come without forcing it. His fingers ached from gripping too hard. He loosened one hand at a time, checking that the Firebolt stayed level while he did. Below, Katie stole the Quaffle. Alicia took the pass and scored. The Gryffindor stand erupted. Harry turned towards it. Hermione had both hands pressed against the railing. Draconia stood very still beside her. Sirius had stopped waving. Remus stood close beside him, one hand upon the rail, and pointed towards Harry's clear stretch of sky as he spoke. Harry could not hear what he said. Sirius looked up and followed the next circle with his eyes.

Harry made his next circle wider, letting them see that he could hold it. He kept the turn gentle, then tried a small climb and felt the Firebolt answer without pulling to either side. If that changed, he would land. For the moment he could see the pitch, feel both hands and choose where he was going. Ravenclaw reached a hundred and ten; Gryffindor still had forty. Lee began explaining that numbers were only one way of understanding a game, which McGonagall appeared to consider a poor theory for someone appointed to announce them. Then Cho went low. Harry saw the Snitch a moment after she did. It flew towards the western stand, skimming above the muddy strip where spectators had entered. Golden wings beat against the rain with absurd indifference to everything humans found difficult about weather. He dropped.

The Firebolt accelerated beneath him. Wind drove cold water against his mouth. Cho held the inside line, forcing him wider around the post. He could cut beneath her broom, take the narrow space above the ground and gain two yards before the Snitch changed course. He saw the route. He saw the support beam. Harry stayed wide. Cho gained a length, her broom skimming low enough to spray water from the grass. For a second he thought he had given the catch away. Then the Snitch turned towards the upper edge of the stand, where the wind came hard between two gaps in the timber. Cho had to correct sharply against the wind.

Harry had room to follow. He angled the Firebolt upward, keeping his left knee pressed to the shaft while his right hand left it. Cho's fingers crossed the air above his wrist. The Snitch flicked once towards the post; Harry followed with the Firebolt instead of reaching farther than his balance would allow. Its wings were suddenly bright against a strip of blue sky. He reached again. Gold struck his palm. He closed his fingers.

For a second he continued climbing because his body had not yet understood that the pursuit was over. Then the whistle sounded beneath him, thin and distant, and the stands began breaking into sound. Harry raised his fist. Rain ran down his wrist. The Snitch struggled between his fingers. Ron flew towards him from the hoops with the expression of someone who had personally invented winning.

"You got it!"

"It's trying to get out again." Harry tightened his fingers around the wings.

"We won." Ron laughed, sounding surprised by his own certainty.

"Yes, Ron. That usually follows."

Ron struck his shoulder, remembered which shoulder it was and looked horrified. The hit stung through the wet robes, but the sting stayed exactly where Ron's hand had landed. Harry caught his sleeve before the apology could become the first thing they said after winning. "You're allowed to be pleased," Harry said.

Ron's grin returned. Fred arrived on Harry's other side and informed them that Angelina would prefer celebration to occur below the level at which a fall could interrupt it. They descended together. Harry's feet met the grass. His knees accepted the landing. Around him, his teammates shouted over one another while Madam Hooch recorded a score of a hundred and ninety to a hundred and ten. Angelina put an arm round Ron long enough to pull him into the cluster of players, then reached for Harry's sleeve too. The twins were talking over Lee's commentary as though he could hear either of them. Harry laughed, tried to explain the last turn and discovered that nobody needed an explanation yet. They had seen the gold in his hand. Madam Hooch asked to inspect the Firebolt before he put it away; he handed it over and waited while she checked the handle and tail for damage from the Bludger. Nothing had cracked, though a few bristles needed straightening.

He loosened his hand and let the Snitch go. It hovered beside his face, bright and ridiculous, before the referee caught it.

Sirius entered the changing room as Harry was attempting to get a wet glove off with his teeth.

"That glove won't improve with chewing."

"It won't come off," Harry replied, taking his teeth away from the wet cuff. "I think it's shrunk."

His godfather took the glove by two fingers and pulled. Harry's hand emerged red and creased. Sirius inspected it for no longer than ordinary concern required, then handed him a towel.

"Good catch. Hooch happy with the broom?"

"She checked it. It's fine." Harry worked the towel between his fingers. "You saw the bit before that, though."

"I saw you recover. And I saw the catch."

Harry looked at him. Sirius's scarf had acquired rain, two loose threads and a Gryffindor badge which someone had apparently attached without asking. He leaned against the lockers, giving Harry enough space to unfasten his soaked robe. Harry had dry clothes on a hook behind him and a towel which had already slipped to the floor twice. Sirius retrieved it the second time, wrung out the corner that had landed in a puddle and handed it back without remark.

"The banner," Harry said.

Sirius nodded.

"Remus thought you might need a moment. We could see you making the turn."

"I didn't fall."

"I know."

"I didn't stop either."

"I know that too."

Harry looked down at the towel. He had expected Sirius to ask why he had stayed in the air, or tell him he should have landed immediately. Instead his godfather watched while he found the sleeve of his dry jumper.

"Were you cross?" Harry asked.

"About the recovery? No. About how low you went through the stands before the rain? A bit."

Harry glanced up.

"The banner frightened you. That isn't something to be cross about," Sirius said. "Flying that close to the stands is a separate matter. You could have clipped the railing. I'm allowed to worry about that as well."

That was easier to hear than being told every mistake belonged to the graveyard. Harry pulled the jumper over his head, caught it on his glasses and accepted Sirius's help pulling it straight. He rubbed the towel through his hair. Across the room, Ron's older brothers were loudly crediting his tactics with saving the entire season. Ron kept telling them to stop while remaining exactly where he could hear.

"You looked as though you wanted to come up," Harry said.

Sirius's mouth twitched.

"I spent most of the match wanting a broom. Not all of it for parental reasons."

"You'd lose."

"That has become a remarkably unkind assumption."

"I have a Firebolt. You have opinions."

Sirius laughed. Harry had heard him laugh on the platform, in the cottage kitchen, beside a hospital bed where nobody found anything funny. This sound belonged to a wet changing room after a match, and Harry wanted to keep it among the others. Remus came to the door and asked whether they were permitted to join the celebration. His hair had dried into untidy curls at the temples; Sirius reached over and caught the wet end of his scarf before it dipped into the bucket by the lockers. Remus thanked him while continuing to ask Angelina about the match. Angelina answered yes before Harry could mention that he had never known either man wait for an invitation where cake might be available. At the castle entrance, Draconia stood beneath an arch with a towel of her own.

"Hermione sent that?" Harry asked.

"I can see when you're wet without Granger telling me."

She put it around his neck and began drying the place beneath his hair where rain had run inside his collar. Students passed close enough that he felt their coats brush his elbow. Draconia continued, refusing to treat looking after him as something which required concealment.

"I took the wide turn," he said quietly.

"I saw."

"I thought you might appreciate it."

Her hands stopped.

"I appreciated watching you enjoy the rest."

He looked down at her. The rain had left a darker line along the shoulders of her coat. She had stood in it for the rest of the match because he was doing something he loved. Harry looked at the damp end of her sleeve, then at her face, and realised she had been smiling before he began explaining the turn.

"Tomorrow?"

She smiled.

"Unless you're planning to sleep through the whole afternoon."

"Only the morning. I can manage three o'clock."

"Good. I've found somewhere for us."

He caught one end of the towel before she could smooth it again.

"Thank you for staying in the rain."

"I was watching the match."

"I know. I'm still pleased."

She looked down at his hand over hers. A student squeezed past with a broom, apologised and interrupted whatever she had meant to say. When there was room again, she gave Harry's fingers a quick pressure and stayed beside him. Behind them, Sirius began asking Remus whether they could borrow two school brooms at Easter. On Sunday afternoon, they went to the gallery above the western courtyard, where thirteen paintings spent most of their time addressing students too hurried to answer. Harry had passed through it while looking for a classroom in first year. He remembered a knight arguing with his horse and a painted orchard which continued dropping apples after winter began outside its frame. When Harry asked where she would like to go, Draconia suggested it for their second afternoon together. The windows faced the mountains and the benches were too narrow for anyone to spread three essays across them. He had agreed to bring the tea; she had said she would make certain there was something less uncertain than the twins' biscuits to eat.

"You checked whether the benches would stop us doing homework?" he asked.

"We'd find room if I let you bring a bag. I am attempting to discourage us both."

She carried a small paper bag from the kitchens. Harry carried tea in a flask Hagrid had lent him after making a suspicious effort to discover whom he intended to give it to. Sunlight lay across the floor in broken rectangles, interrupted whenever someone moved in a painting. The bench had a polished hollow where generations of students had sat, and an old burn mark at one end which Harry suspected had begun as somebody's Charms homework. He put the flask at the other end. Below them, the western courtyard was drying in patches; footsteps sounded sharper where the stone was already pale. One elderly witch opened a curtain, inspected them and closed it again.

"A quieter audience," Harry observed.

"Give her time."

"You could use your wand."

"I could."

"You could ask for assistance."

"You could stop watching while I lose an argument with the icing."

"Future prefect," Harry said.

"She'll have those two carrying her books all term if they aren't careful."

"What?"

"That rules out very little."

"I'm pleased you're here."

The answer quieted her. She looked down into her cup, smiled before attempting to hide it and finally gave up.

"So am I."

"You are making an afternoon of this."

"I'm allowed to look at you."

He had meant it lightly. Saying it without immediately finding something else to discuss made his ears grow warm. Draconia lowered her cup, and for a moment he thought she might answer with the easy severity which usually rescued them both from embarrassment.

"Yes," she said instead.

"Have you?"

"Is that me?"

"A generic Seeker."

"With my broom."

"Fine. It's you. The hair was rather difficult to mistake."

He laughed. Below the drawing, she had put a second figure in a badly knitted scarf. Its arms reached almost to the edge of the page.

"Sirius will frame himself."

"Then he cannot have it."

"Do you do these often?"

Draconia hesitated. He returned the paper without reaching for the rest of her bag.

"Can I see one?"

"Not the Binns ones."

"Now I very much want to."

The black line of a bedpost occupied one corner. Beyond the glass, a small fish appeared suspended above the lower sill.

"Your room?"

She nodded. Harry had never been there. The drawing made its position beneath the water real in a way that corridors and House arguments had not. He could imagine Draconia sitting awake while the lake moved beyond her pillow, with the rest of them several floors and too many passwords away.

"I like the fish."

"It has been there for months. Millicent claims it recognises her."

"Does it?"

"It swims away when she opens the curtains."

"A difficult relationship."

Draconia laughed. He turned back to the drawing of Hermione, unwilling to hurry past it.

"Could you give her this one?"

"She thinks she looks dreadful when she sleeps."

"She doesn't."

Draconia watched his face for a moment. He let her. He meant what he had said. Hermione looked tired in the sketch, one hand tucked beneath her cheek, but Draconia had drawn her with a care Harry recognised. He liked knowing the two girls could see that in each other too.

"I know," she said, and put the page aside.

Harry turned back to the drawing of the courtyard. He could recognise the bench beneath the window and the lean of a tree whose roots had lifted two flagstones. "Would you draw somewhere else if you had time?" he asked.

"Somewhere I could sit without pretending to take notes."

"Hawthorn?"

She considered it. "The back door. The garden beyond it. Your broom leaning where Sirius tells you not to leave it."

"Useful reminder for him."

"I might leave that part out."

He gave her the page. She put it away carefully, preserving the one she had chosen for Hermione. A draught moved beneath the window. She moved close instead, folding the offered edge round her shoulder and resting against him. Their hips fit upon the narrow bench with very little room left over. "Are we going to keep arranging these?" he asked.

"Afternoons?"

"Dates. I'm attempting the word."

"You managed it. I heard."

"Draco."

She lifted her face. The teasing could have kept them comfortable for another hour. Harry was beginning to recognise how often comfort allowed him to avoid saying something important.

"I don't want to ask anyone else. On dates, I mean."

He had looked towards the painted orchard while beginning the sentence. Now he made himself turn back. He did not want her to have to guess whether he meant next Saturday or every afternoon they could arrange. Her fingers tightened upon his sleeve.

"Neither do I." Her eyes stayed on his. "I was hoping you meant that."

Harry looked at their hands. She was holding his sleeve rather than his fingers, and he turned his palm beneath hers until she let him take them. He had been afraid the sentence would make things solemn and difficult. It had made her smile. He breathed out, then looked at her again.

"Even when I take ages to ask?"

"I would prefer you not to make a practice of that."

"I'm improving."

"You asked before I went back to the library. I appreciate the improvement."

He laughed, and she looked pleased with herself for making him do it. Harry wanted this afternoon again: tea he had brought, a drawing she had chosen to show him, the warmth of her through his coat. He wanted to arrange the next one before some emergency decided when they would see each other.

"Next Saturday?"

"If you aren't on the pitch."

"After the pitch."

"After you have washed."

"Very demanding."

"You asked me."

She held his gaze as she said it. He nodded, still smiling. From behind the elderly witch's curtain came the soft scrape of a chair. Harry glanced towards it, then back at Draconia, who seemed entirely uninterested in what the portraits thought of them. Draconia looked at him with a brightness he could not blame upon the afternoon sun.

"That's all I meant," he said.

"It is rather a lot."

She kissed him before he could apologise for having underestimated it. Harry drew her closer, careful of the cup beside her knee. She made a small, impatient sound when he glanced towards it, took it from the bench and put it on the floor herself. Then she returned both hands to him. The kiss was easier than the sentence had been. He knew where to put his hand now; he knew she liked being touched gently at the side of her face. When they parted, she stayed close, her forehead near his and her eyes open. He could see himself reflected in them.

"I keep thinking I'm getting something wrong," he admitted.

"What?"

"This."

Draconia moved back enough to look at him properly.

"You can ask me," she said. "I like you asking. But you don't need to apologise before I've answered."

He looked at her hand, still resting against his neck.

"This is all right?"

"Yes. Come here."

He nodded. She kissed him once more, softly, then settled against his shoulder again. He let his arm lie where it had been instead of moving it away to prove he had heard her. For several minutes neither spoke. The painted orchard dropped another apple. Somewhere behind the curtains, the elderly witch made a disapproving noise and received a sharp reply from a portrait further along. Harry rested his cheek against Draconia's hair.

"Are you sleeping?"

"Better."

He felt the hesitation beneath the word and did not rush to close it.

"Mostly," she added.

"Do you want to tell me?"

She rubbed one thumb along his cuff.

"Sometimes I wake because I've moved. Rolled over. Put my hand somewhere I don't remember choosing." Her eyes remained upon the opposite painting. "It takes a moment to remember that bodies do ordinary things without asking us."

Harry thought of waking with his fingers locked around the bedpost. He had wanted the dormitory to be the place he knew; she had to be certain that the hand upon her own blanket belonged to her. He looked at the fingers curled in his sleeve, trying to imagine being afraid of them.

"Does it happen every night?"

"No. Enough that I think about it before sleeping sometimes."

He nodded. She had not taken her hand away, and he kept his still beneath it.

"Do you turn the light on?"

"Sometimes. Sometimes Pansy complains before I get that far."

"Helpful."

"Occasionally. She has a very distinctive way of saying it is three in the morning."

He smiled, then let it fade.

"Could you ask them to call me? Through Hawthorn, I mean. If you woke like that and wanted me to know."

"I could ask. They'd have to use your pair to reach you." Draconia glanced at him. "You could ask them to contact me too. It needn't only be when the stone lights."

He had been expecting to offer something. Receiving the same offer made him quiet.

"I know."

"If you want to speak to me, ask. Don't spend half the night deciding it isn't bad enough. I would rather be woken than have you wishing you'd said something."

He took her hand. Beneath his shirt, the green stone lay cool. Here, he had the person whose safety he wanted to know, warm against his shoulder and telling him something no charm could supply. They stayed until the sun moved beyond the benches. Harry asked which picture Pansy most objected to, and Draconia said it depended upon whether she had seen the latest one. The conversation returned slowly to the folder upon her knees. Before she packed it away, he asked if he could keep the drawing of the pitch.

"Including the generic Seeker?"

"Especially him. We have a great deal in common."

She pulled out the sheet and gave it to him, making him promise that Sirius would receive a copy rather than the original. Harry folded a clean piece of parchment round it. He wanted the paper to make it back to his room without acquiring pastry. Draconia packed the drawings, keeping Hermione's separate. Harry collected the cups. At the staircase, she kissed him once more, quickly enough to leave him looking startled while she walked away. The knight in the nearest painting cleared his throat. Harry looked up.

"I'm not asking what you would have done."

The horse snorted. The knight appeared offended by both of them.

The Ministry workmen came for the ward anchors on Monday, the eleventh of March. "They aren't being installed?"

"These aren't." Peake tightened the cushioning strap around one brass frame. "They're going back for examination. I can tell you where this consignment is going; I don't know what anyone has approved elsewhere."

"Will the examination include the transmission results?"

"Professor Flitwick has attached them to the return instruction. I have signed the receiving copy."

"No. Spare relay. We didn't need it."

"Changes the way a field receives its charge. It wasn't fitted to this test."

"It's reclaimed stock. Department stores sent it with the consignment."

He had to return to the first position before either could proceed.

"I was nervous," he explained afterwards.

"So was I," Susan said. "That was why I waited."

Harry looked towards Remus. His teacher had heard the distinction too; he smiled without turning it into something grander than two students discovering that fear could require different actions. For the third attempt, Cedric put Harry inside the boundary.

"No coaching."

"I wasn't—"

"You've explained three other people's decisions before they finished."

Harry closed his mouth. Ron grinned from the far end of the room.

"We'll see how long he lasts," Ron said, grinning.

The room gave Harry a narrow passage, two chairs and a door bolted on its far side. Ginny stood beside him with the message board. Draconia occupied the outside position. Neville waited further along, unable to see their end but able to reach the person holding the exit. The conditions were ordinary enough to make him impatient. A casualty, represented by a weighted cloak, could not move without help; one doorway had become inaccessible; the practice message object could hold a written warning but could not send it across the partition.

"We need the west door," Harry said.

Ginny checked the little plan.

"That's east."

"The one on the left."

"Our left. Neville won't know that."

Harry took a breath. The exercise had been his idea. Apparently that did not entitle him to remain good at it once other people began using the parts he had skipped. They revised the message. Ginny wrote the correct door number, the number of people and the obstruction. She added where they would wait: beside the high-backed chair, with the casualty between them. Harry called towards Neville, remembered that the sound-damping partition kept their voice from reaching him, and stopped. Through a clear panel above the practice wall he could see the others moving, but he could not hear their conversation either. His mirror remained warm inside his pocket. He could use it to end the representation if he wanted. He had agreed not to treat it as an answer within the exercise. Outside, Ron came into view and pointed towards a gap beneath the practice wall. Harry almost moved.

Ginny caught his sleeve.

"Message first."

He looked at the gap. It was too small for a body, wide enough for parchment and situated beside the chair they needed to move. Harry folded the board's removable sheet and pushed it through. Ron collected it, read rather than guessed, and carried it to Draconia. Waiting felt absurd. Harry could see the next task. He could begin it now, use the chair to lift the latch, make the door easier for the other group before their message arrived. He touched the back of the chair. Its top was high enough that standing upon the seat might let him reach the bolt over the shallow wall. He had noticed the possibility before the paper left his hand, and could not stop considering it now.

"They know we're here," Ginny said.

"I could save time."

"You could move the thing you've told them to expect."

He let go. Beyond the wall, Draconia pointed to the two doors she could reach. Ron checked the number on the paper, held it up for her and indicated the one nearest Neville. Harry could see her nod, though no words came through the partition. Neville moved towards the correct hinge. When the latch opened, Harry remained where he had said he would be. The weighted cloak lay between the chairs, exactly where their note had placed it. Ginny took one side, Harry the other. They lifted it together, waited while Neville widened the door and came out slowly enough not to strike either side of the frame. Cedric stopped the clock.

"Seven minutes."

"Dreadful," Harry said.

"Five faster than the first."

"The first left Hannah behind."

"Exactly. Yours brought everyone out. I'm counting that before I count the minutes."

Remus stood to inspect the door. He braced one hand against the back of his chair before putting weight on his ankle, and Harry moved the nearest message sheet out of his way. He expected Remus to mention the impulse to move the chair. Instead he asked Ginny how she knew when to interrupt.

"He stopped talking and started looking at the latch."

Harry turned towards her.

"I do listen."

"I know. That's when you usually answer me."

Ron made an approving sound.

"He does listen. Occasionally you have to pull his sleeve first."

Harry looked at Remus, hoping for assistance. Remus appeared entirely unwilling to improve the students' opinion of him.

"Did she make the task harder?" he asked.

"Briefly."

"Better?"

Harry considered the door, the paper and the position from which Neville had known how to find them.

"Yes."

"Then tell her."

He did. Ginny accepted with a flourish so exaggerated that it released the last of his irritation into laughter. They continued until everybody had occupied a position they disliked. On one attempt Neville waited with the casualty while Zacharias worked the door. On another, Pansy had to admit that the route she could see was blocked from the far end. Harry watched her hand the message to Susan, then let Susan choose a different way without following her to explain it again. Cedric had to wait while someone else worked the lock. Hermione had to pass incomplete information without apologising for what she could not know. Draconia had to accept a route chosen by Susan after the room closed the one she preferred. None of them enjoyed the requirement. Several improved. As they put the chairs away, Hannah asked Remus about the Patronus Charm.

"Harry says you taught him."

"I helped him practise. It took quite a few attempts, even when he was doing everything I asked."

"I can only make the mist."

"That is part of the charm."

"It doesn't do much."

Remus took out his wand, considered the practice floor and asked Harry whether there was time. Harry glanced at the clock above the door. They could still report back to McGonagall and reach dinner without hurrying. He looked towards Hermione, who nodded and set down the message sheets.

"For whatever you're about to do? Yes."

He cast without explaining the memory. Silver light spread from the tip, folded and gathered itself into a wolf. It landed upon the floor without a sound. Its paws were narrower than Harry expected; the ears turned towards Hannah before the rest of the body followed. The room went quiet. A little silver caught along the backs of the empty chairs. Ron leaned forward; Pansy lowered the quill she had been using to repair a mistake in her notes. Harry had seen Remus produce light before. Seeing it take this shape in front of everyone made him look at his teacher rather than the animal. Remus held his wand with an effort Harry could see in the tendons of his wrist. He did not look at the wolf immediately. Then it brushed past his chair, and his expression eased. Hannah put out a hand.

"May I?"

He nodded. Her fingers passed through the silver fur. Light moved across them, leaving nothing behind when she drew away.

"It looks interested," she said.

"It usually is."

"In what?"

Remus glanced around the benches.

"People, apparently."

Harry remembered their conversation before Christmas. He watched the wolf turn towards the painted arch, inspect it and return to the place beside its caster.

"Could it carry a message through the field?" Hermione asked.

Remus looked up.

"I don't know how it would cross that particular field," Remus said. "A Patronus can carry words once you've learnt to send them, but we would have to test it too."

She nodded. The wolf remained beside Remus's chair. Harry liked it no less because it could not answer the question. Hannah was looking at her own wand again, evidently more interested in making a little of that light than in replacing a mirror. Remus asked Hannah to show him the mist. She produced a thin silver cloud on her second attempt. He asked her to loosen her grip, then try the same memory without deciding in advance that the result would disappoint her. On the next attempt the mist reached past the first bench. Hannah looked up so quickly that it thinned again, and Remus smiled.

"You saw that?"

She nodded.

"Good. Try to let it happen without checking how far it has gone." Remus waited while she lowered her wand, then looked towards Harry.

Harry raised his own. "I can show you another one, if you want." Hannah nodded. When he cast, the stag stepped into the light beside the wolf.

Its antlers caught the glow from the room's windows. The two animals stood together for a moment, neither driving the other aside. Harry had chosen the gallery, Draconia's cup on the floor and the sound of his friends laughing at the match. The stag turned its head towards him. Harry remembered what Remus had told him about his father's Animagus form, then felt the warmth of Draconia's shoulder in the gallery return more clearly. Both memories could be there. He kept his wand steady while the stag moved beside the wolf. Remus watched until Harry lowered his wand. The stag thinned. Remus lowered his own wand a moment later, letting the wolf disappear with it.

They left in pairs before dinner. Harry waited with Remus while Cedric took the last group towards the stairs. The room cleared its practice walls, leaving an open floor and a few chairs which nobody had remembered to stack. Hermione collected the message sheets. Draconia stood beside the door, checking the corridor through a narrow opening before letting the next pair out.

"Thank you for coming," Hermione told Remus.

"Thank you for asking."

"Would you come again?"

His eyes moved across the room before returning to hers.

"Yes."

She smiled, caught Draconia's sleeve and went with her. Harry heard them discussing where to keep the test record. Their footsteps faded in the corridor. Remus sat to put the notes into his bag.

"You taught them to disagree with you."

"They brought experience."

"You allowed them to use it."

Harry picked up the remaining chair.

"I wanted to move the latch."

"I saw."

"You didn't stop me."

"Ginny did."

"What if she hadn't?"

Remus fastened the bag.

"Then I would have stopped you before you climbed. I could see the chair, and Cedric could see the other side. Ginny spoke while there was still time to listen; that was part of what we were practising."

Harry set the chair upon the stack. The room smelled of warm wood and the lemon oil it supplied when benches appeared. No injuries had occurred; no one had needed him to be better than the task required.

"I like being here," he said.

Remus looked at him.

"Teaching?"

"Yes. Sometimes I forget to like it because I keep thinking of what happens if they need it."

"It helped them today," Remus said. "Hannah knows what to ask when somebody says everyone is outside. Ginny told you to wait, and you listened. They can take those things away without waiting for somebody to attack them. And it takes courage to correct somebody they're impressed by."

"Nobody's impressed with me."

"Then I shall say nothing to spoil it. It's difficult enough getting a class to interrupt when you're wrong."

Harry laughed. Remus rose, put too much weight on the tired ankle and accepted Harry's hand before either could give the assistance a more complicated name. At the door, he stopped.

"You asked in the boathouse how I stopped thinking about people I couldn't reach."

Harry nodded.

"Today helped. I spent most of it thinking about the people in front of me."

"The practice?"

"Seeing you work together. Being asked something I could answer, and having to admit when I couldn't."

Remus looked back at the stack of chairs. "I was thinking about what it would have meant to have somebody teach us to do that properly. We were very good at assuming a plan had worked because James said it would."

"And Sirius?"

"Had three improvements ready before anybody asked."

Harry smiled. He could imagine the room filling with them: his father with an explanation, Sirius impatient to begin, Remus looking from one to the other. It made the empty practice floor feel less strange than the Christmas memory had. Harry looked back into the room. The floor was empty now, the benches stacked, the arch fading where stone belonged. He could still see Ron carrying the message, Ginny holding his sleeve and Neville opening the door from the other side.

"You have them too," he said.

Remus's hand stayed upon his shoulder.

"I am beginning to believe that."

They walked downstairs to report to McGonagall. She asked whether the tuition had been satisfactory.

"My worst student remains argumentative," Remus said.

Harry stared at him.

"You've been waiting all afternoon to say that."

"And observant."

McGonagall made a note in the visitor's book. Her mouth moved just enough for Harry to know she was enjoying herself.

Sirius had begun learning to cook things which took longer than ten minutes. Harry discovered this during their Friday evening call, after they had asked the fresh questions agreed at breakfast. The mirror showed the Hawthorn kitchen table, a bowl of chopped vegetables and an open book which had acquired a stain near the instructions. Sirius stood beyond it with his sleeves rolled up. Something in a pan made a noise of steady disagreement.

"Is it meant to do that?" Harry asked.

"It is frying."

"It sounds angry."

"It's meant to make some noise."

Remus entered the picture carrying a mug. He looked into the pan, lowered the heat with a tap of his wand and took the chair closest to the table.

"There."

Sirius glared at him.

"I was managing."

"The onions were beginning to stick. I'd prefer to eat them before they catch fire."

Harry leaned against his dormitory headboard. Ron had gone downstairs to finish an essay with Neville; the other beds were empty, curtains open around rumpled blankets. The evening still held daylight at the window. He could see the place where the sun had left a long orange line beneath the mountains.

"Is this for somebody?" he asked.

Sirius stirred the pan.

"People live here, Harry. We sometimes eat."

Remus took a sip of tea. His eyes moved towards the envelope beside the chopping board. Harry smiled.

"When is she coming?"

"The beginning of the Easter holiday. If we can arrange it. She hasn't booked anything yet."

"Perhaps she's coming, or perhaps you'll admit she's coming?"

Sirius put down the spoon.

"You were easier to live with before you began giving me advice about asking girls out."

Harry felt his face warm.

"Remus spent yesterday encouraging people to tell me when I was wrong. I think I'm getting better at returning the favour."

"They were remarkably restrained," Remus said.

"I don't remember restraint."

"You would have noticed its absence."

Sirius turned back to the pan, but his concentration had changed. Harry could see him watching the food without quite attending to it. It was the same look Ron wore when he had prepared an answer and feared the question might become something else.

"You can ask her," Harry said.

"I have."

"Then what's wrong?"

Sirius touched the edge of the book.

"I haven't seen her in a long time."

Harry looked at the photograph on his bedside table. His parents waved from a garden which belonged to another decade. Sirius had been young when he last knew Marlene in person. Harry had seen them together in the old photographs, laughing over the motorbike and looking towards each other when the camera tried to catch their faces. The Marlene in those pictures had never had to decide how to greet Sirius after fourteen years apart, knowing how much of his life she had missed. Sirius would be standing in the kitchen now, older than that boy, with years he had never been allowed to explain in between. Harry understood a little of why cooking dinner might suddenly matter.

"Does she know about the house?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Me?"

"Quite a lot about you. She says I put your examination results in a letter which was supposed to tell her what the weather had done to the garden."

Harry smiled.

"Hermione's mum wrote three pages about the weather this week. I think your letter was probably more interesting."

Sirius looked at him over the book. The tension in his mouth eased.

"Would you be comfortable meeting her here? We can arrange dinner somewhere else if you would prefer that."

"Here is fine."

"You don't have to be enthusiastic for my benefit."

Harry thought about that. He wanted Sirius to be happy. He also wanted home to remain a place in which he did not have to perform gratitude for every good thing an adult chose.

"I'm curious," he said. "I don't know her yet."

"That's all I'm asking. You don't have to decide you like her before she walks through the door."

"I am pleased you're learning to cook before she arrives, though."

"You might have left that part out."

Remus reached past the mirror, lifted the pan from the heat and inspected it. Sirius made room for him without being asked, though he continued holding the spoon as if surrendering it might be an admission of defeat.

"Recoverable."

Harry laughed. Sirius took the spoon back, objecting to the tone rather than the verdict. They spoke about the D.A. afterwards. Remus described Hannah's Patronus attempt and the time they had lost looking for the wrong exit in the practice corridor. Harry told Sirius that Ginny had prevented him from improving an arrangement so enthusiastically that everybody else would have lost it.

"Useful skill," Sirius said.

"She made it sound personal."

"She knows you. Listen when she tells you to wait."

Before the call ended, Sirius turned the mirror towards a photograph which had arrived from the match. Harry stood upon the muddy pitch between Ron and Angelina. His hair was wet, his smile enormous, his right hand still lifted as though the Snitch had remained inside it.

"Hagrid sent that."

"You look pleased," Sirius said.

"I was."

Sirius put the picture beside the letter.

"Come home with a copy of that one too."

Harry traced a thumb along the edge of his mirror. "What, the photograph?"

"And the bit where you enjoyed yourself. I'd like to hear about that turn as well as the banner."

Harry looked at the laughing boy between Ron and Angelina. He could remember the wet grass beneath his feet and everyone talking too loudly to hear a word he said. The banner had happened earlier in the same match. He did not need to leave the victory out when he told the story.

"Good enough. Tell Ron we're still expecting him for a weekend. Tell Hermione and Draco they may object to my cooking once they have eaten something else."

"I'll tell them you've begun issuing conditions."

"My house is full of excellent teachers."

The mirror darkened. Harry kept it in his hands for a moment, feeling the warmth leave the frame. Outside, the last strip of orange faded. He put the glass beside his pillow and went to find Ron before the essay required rescuing.

The room in his sleep had no window. Light came from a green-shaded lamp upon a table. It made the corners difficult to judge, leaving narrow strips of wall visible between tall cabinets. A man stood on the other side of the table with his sleeves fastened at the wrists. Harry could see the loose thread upon one cuff but not his face. Something lay between them. Grey metal. A rectangular plate. Seven little grooves surrounding a star. He knew the mark.

The knowledge arrived from his own mind, loud enough to disturb the scene. For an instant the lamp became a patch of afternoon sunlight in the stores corridor. Then the green returned, and a hand which was too pale, too long, moved into the light. Its fingers touched the edge of the plate. There was a nick beside the star. Harry had not seen that before. He concentrated on it because concentrating on anything else meant accepting the body through which the room had become visible. The man said something he could not hear. A voice answered from much nearer.

"It must remain closed."

Harry knew that voice too. The fingers stopped moving. A cold impatience entered the moment, wholly unlike the fear he brought to it. The man across the table lowered his head. Harry tried to draw back. He had practised leaving. He had practised naming the point at which a memory stopped belonging to him. Here, he had no chair beneath his hands and no teacher beside the door, only a table seen from the wrong height and the terrible familiarity of a voice moving through a mouth which was not his. He held onto the nick in the plate. Then the light snapped out.

Harry woke with his knees against the blanket and one hand pressed over his scar. Pain ran above his eyebrow, sharp enough to make the dark dormitory tilt. He drew a breath, lost it halfway and took another. The bed remained under him. A curtain ring tapped against the post where his elbow had struck it.

"Harry?"

Ron was already sitting up. Harry tried to answer. What came out sounded smaller than the room required.

"Something new." Harry swallowed. "I wasn't in the graveyard."

It was as much as he could put in order. He looked towards the foot of the bed, trying to make out the blanket folded over his trunk. The green room had held no trunk. Ron's lamp clicked on beyond the curtains. Yellow light entered beneath the curtains. Ron waited outside them.

"Can I?"

Harry nodded, then remembered Ron could not see.

"Yes."

The curtain moved. Ron's hair stood up at the back; one foot had found a slipper and the other had not. He looked at Harry's hand, the position of his shoulders and the mirror beside his pillow.

"Want me to call?"

Harry took the glass.

"Stay. Please."

"I'm here. I'm not going anywhere."

Sirius answered after the third attempt. Harry had given up too quickly on the first two, letting the glass darken before the call had time to reach him. On the third he held it still and said Sirius's name once. Ron touched his forearm on the third and asked him to wait. The frame warmed against his fingers. Sirius had been asleep; his voice thickened around Harry's name before becoming clear.

"Where are you?"

"Dormitory. Ron's here."

"We'll check first. Can you remember the question?"

Harry could. He asked it slowly, waiting while Sirius answered and gave him the other one. Ron kept his hand upon the bedpost, where Harry could see it. The answers did not settle what he had seen; they let him begin telling Sirius without having to manage that worry as well.

Sirius sat up and turned on the lamp beside his bed. Harry could see the photograph from the match resting against the book behind it, its red scarves bright in the lamplight. Sirius must have brought it upstairs after their evening call. When he asked where Ron was sitting, Harry turned the glass to show him. Ron answered directly, still holding the slipper he had not managed to put on.

Harry described the room. He left out no part because it embarrassed him. The pale hand, the voice, the effort to leave. When he reached the plate, Sirius sat forward and called for Remus beyond the edge of the picture. Ron brought water. Harry took three small swallows, looking at the lamp between them.

"I saw the stamp in the stores on Monday," he said. "It could be that. My head making something out of what I saw."

"It could," Sirius replied. "You don't have to decide before telling us."

Remus came into view and asked whether the pain continued. Harry said it had begun to ease. His teacher accepted the answer and checked the time.

"It's Saturday, the sixteenth of March. Just after midnight, Harry. You're in your own bed. Ron can see you, and we're listening."

Harry looked at the blanket folded upon his trunk, then at the curtains beside his pillow. He could see both without the green light returning. Remus took his own Order mirror to contact Snape through the separate adult channel. A ringing came from somewhere outside Sirius's bedroom. Sirius turned towards it, listened, then picked up his half of Harry's mirror.

"That's one of the answering pieces downstairs. Stay with Ron. I'll take you with me."

Harry watched a stretch of landing move behind his godfather, then the stairs and the open study door. Sirius took Draconia's answering mirror from the desk. She had called Hawthorn through her own pair. Harry heard her ask for him before Sirius had finished sitting down.

"He's on his mirror with me," Sirius told her. "He's in the dormitory, and Ron's there. Let me put these where you can see each other."

He set the two answering pieces upright upon the desk, angled towards one another, and moved the lamp. Through his own glass, Harry could see Draconia in the other piece; his voice reached her through the two open calls. Her hair lay loose around her shoulders. A point of wandlight illuminated the curtains behind her.

"Can you hear Harry?" Sirius asked.

"Yes." She moved nearer to her handpiece. "Harry?"

"The stone flashed," she said. "Grey to red. It stopped before anyone answered."

He steadied the glass against his knee while Sirius adjusted the other answering piece so Draconia could see him more clearly.

"I'm here. Ron's with me. Sirius and Remus know."

Her eyes moved across his face. He turned the glass to show Ron upon the edge of his bed. Sirius shifted Harry's answering piece towards Draconia's, passing the view on, then returned it when Harry turned back. Ron lifted the water jug into view, proving nothing more complicated than that he was there and had found it.

"A vision?" she asked.

"Something new. We haven't decided what."

She nodded, swallowing before the movement had finished.

"The warning doesn't prove the room was real," Harry said. "We haven't checked it against anything yet."

"I know. I wanted to hear you."

Harry's throat tightened. He had offered to call when she woke frightened by her own hand, and she had answered an alert from his body before he knew how much fear to put into words.

"I'm staying here," he told her.

"Good."

She pulled the wandlight a little farther from the glass. Harry could see the green edge of her bed curtain and the crease of her pillow behind her shoulder. She was in her dormitory too. He looked at those ordinary things while the ache above his eyebrow began to settle.

"Speak to Sirius about the rest," she said after a moment. "You can tell me more in the morning. Would you like me to find Hermione outside the Hall and tell her you called? Or would you rather tell her?"

"Tell her I've spoken to them. I'll explain the rest when I get there." He looked at Draconia's wandlight. "Are you all right?"

Draconia looked at him, then towards the wand in her hand.

"Frightened. I can put the light on. Pansy will complain, and I'll know she's awake."

Harry almost smiled. "Tell them if you need someone to stay on the call. Don't sit there with the light on just because I've stopped talking."

"You too."

He thanked her. Sirius stayed long enough to ask whether she wanted another adult contacted; she said she would speak to her mother in the morning and wake Pansy if she needed company. Once Draconia closed her own call, Sirius put that answering piece down and drew Harry's towards him. The guardian connection had stayed open throughout. Remus came into the study; he had spoken to Snape through his own mirror and set it aside. They were arranging for someone to come to Harry, while Snape checked what he could from Hogwarts. Nobody asked him to leave the dormitory or settle whether the room had been real before they would help. Harry put his mirror against the water jug and let Ron adjust it so they could see him without his hand beginning to shake again.

"Do you know where the room was?"

"No."

"Any person in danger?"

Harry thought of the lowered head, the frayed cuff. The man opposite the table had looked frightened, but Harry could not turn a posture into a location or a voice into a name.

"I don't know."

"Then that is what we tell him. He can compare the fitting with the stores records. It won't establish where that room is, and you don't need to invent an answer for us to keep looking."

Remus gave him Snape's message: write down what he remembered without filling the gaps, and remain with someone. Snape had contacted McGonagall. Madam Pomfrey would come to the dormitory to check him; a further examination would be arranged that morning, with Harry told what it involved before it began. They were not to wait for her if the pain grew worse or anything in the room became difficult to recognise. Ron said he would fetch the nearest prefect while Sirius stayed on the call if that happened. Harry looked towards Ron.

"Your essay?"

"Finished."

"Good?"

"Finished is all I'm promising. Hermione hasn't seen the last paragraph yet."

The joke let him breathe more deeply. He handed Ron the water, reached for the notebook in his bedside drawer and found that his fingers shook too badly to open it neatly. Ron opened it for him. Through the mirror, Sirius stayed where Harry could see him. Remus fetched his own paper. The dark room had begun to lose its grip upon the dormitory; the green lamp remained clear, separate from the yellow one beside his bed. Harry drew the small star first. Then, beneath it, he wrote what the voice had said.

It must remain closed.

Ron waited until the quill stopped before passing him a biscuit from the tin in his bedside drawer. He checked the wrapper once, then pulled it off and dropped it into his own pocket. Harry set it beside the notebook. He would eat it in a moment, when the last line stopped looking as though it belonged to somebody else's room. Sirius stayed in the glass while Remus asked him to look up and describe his own.

Harry began with Ron. That was the easiest thing to name.
