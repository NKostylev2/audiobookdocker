3

The owl in the fruit bowl refused to move for breakfast. It tucked its head farther beneath one wing when Dobby approached, leaving one yellow foot planted upon a pear as though it intended to defend the whole arrangement.

Harry stopped in the kitchen doorway. He had turned fifteen on Monday, come home, eaten cake and survived rather more fuss than he was used to. Now it was Friday, the morning after the hearing, and Sirius was preparing a second celebration for the coming Monday. Invitations and replies had joined the flood of post prompted by the Ministry's finding.

The first delivery owl had struck the drawing-room window at such speed that Sirius came downstairs carrying his wand and half wearing a dressing gown. The second descended through the kitchen chimney, scattered soot across the stove and deposited a damp envelope in the porridge. The third was intercepted by Dobby before it could attack the letterbox. By seven o'clock, twelve birds occupied the kitchen rafters and three had begun fighting over the dresser. Amelia's office was forwarding Harry's mail through an approved relay; nobody writing to him needed Hawthorn's address. Dobby admitted the screened deliveries at the boundary, but clearly considered managing the birds afterwards someone else's responsibility. Harry considered going back upstairs before any of the owls noticed him.

"Too late," Sirius said. "They've seen you."

Every round, unblinking eye turned towards Harry.

"That feels threatening."

"Most post is."

Sirius had managed to pull on the rest of his dressing gown, though he had tied it badly and his hair suggested that sleep had fought him to a draw. He sat halfway along the kitchen table with a mug in one hand and the Ministry transcript in the other. Three parchment rolls were tucked beneath his elbow. More letters covered the chairs, the mantel and the bread board.

Hermione sat at the far end, already dressed, with ink on the side of her hand. She had divided the morning's post into five piles and labelled them in a script so neat it appeared printed: SUPPORTIVE, HOSTILE, REQUESTS FOR COMMENT, POSSIBLY USEFUL and UNCLASSIFIED.

The hostile pile had begun to slide towards Hermione's plate. She pushed it back with the heel of her hand, looking increasingly annoyed that strangers had managed to occupy her breakfast before she had eaten it.

Draconia stood beside her in a pale silk dressing gown, opening envelopes with the silver knife she ordinarily used for fruit. She had slept badly again. Harry could tell from the careful arrangement of her hair and the faint stillness around her eyes, both of which meant she had spent extra time making certain nobody else could.

"We require another category," she said.

"For what?"

Draconia held up a sheet upon which someone had drawn Harry with horns, fangs and what appeared to be a forked tail.

Hermione examined it, then pushed her plate out of the way. "That goes with the hostile ones. Honestly, have they nothing better to do?"

"I was thinking of a separate pile for crimes against drawing."

"We are trying to sort the post, Draco, not give them marks."

"Then they should be grateful. I could tell them exactly where they went wrong."

Harry took the drawing from her. The version of him upon the page was standing on a heap of bodies and waving a banner which read LIAR. Someone had made his glasses far too large.

"They got my hair right."

Draconia's mouth tightened. Harry dropped the parchment into the hostile pile. A corner curled in a sudden lick of flame. He jumped backwards. Hermione snatched her wand from the table, but the fire vanished before it reached the other envelopes, leaving one neat square of ash. Sirius lowered his own wand.

"You said evidence," Harry reminded him.

"You made a copy of that one, didn't you, Hermione?"

"Yes, but that doesn't mean you should set things on fire beside my notes."

Sirius looked at the ash. "I'll move the next one."

"We should be checking them for curses before opening them," Hermione said.

"I am," Draconia replied.

Hermione stared at the knife.

"The blade changes colour in the presence of common contact enchantments."

"You might have told me that before I opened seventeen with my hands."

"I checked the bundle before you started. I wasn't going to let you open a curse with your bare hands."

Hermione's stare deepened.

"I should have told you," Draconia added, less lightly. "You were already on your third envelope before I thought of it."

"Next time, tell me before the first," Hermione said. She took the knife to inspect it, curiosity briefly winning over annoyance. "Does it catch delayed charms as well?"

"Not all of them. That's why Bill checked the relay wards too."

Harry crossed the kitchen and lifted the lid from the porridge pot. A squat brown owl immediately swooped from the curtain rail and attempted to land upon his shoulder. Its claws caught the thin fabric of his shirt. Pain flashed across the scar tissue beneath. His body moved before the thought did.

The wooden spoon struck the floor. Harry twisted, one hand reaching for a wand he had left upstairs while the other caught the bird hard around the middle. The owl shrieked. Every chair leg scraped at once. Then he was standing beside the stove with a furious bundle of feathers trapped against his chest, heart battering his ribs and the whole kitchen watching him. The owl snapped its beak near his thumb.

"Right," Harry said faintly. "You're an owl."

It appeared insulted by the reminder.

Hermione rose slowly. "Harry—"

"I'm fine."

The answer arrived with such speed that Sirius closed his eyes. Harry loosened his grip. The bird scrambled onto his forearm, shook its feathers back into order and presented one leg with vindictive dignity. A small blue envelope was tied there.

"Nobody," Sirius said carefully, "is going to make you open that."

Harry looked at him. Nobody had moved closer. Hermione held her hands where he could see them. Draconia's wand had appeared between her fingers, but its point faced the floor. Narcissa, standing near the pantry, had gone so quiet that Harry had not realised she was present. They had all learnt how not to crowd him.

Harry watched Hermione lower her hands when he loosened his grip upon the owl. They had learned all this because of him: how to approach, when to stop and which noises might turn an ordinary breakfast into something else. He was grateful, but he wished they had never needed the practice.

"It startled me," Harry said.

"It caught a sore place," Remus said from the doorway. "Give yourself a moment. The bird can wait."

He wore yesterday's shirt beneath an old cardigan and carried two mugs of tea. He looked tired, although less drawn than he had after the hearing. He set one mug near Sirius and approached the owl at the pace one might use with a suspicious dog.

"May I?"

Harry nodded. Remus untied the envelope and passed it to him. The name across the front had been written in green ink. HARRY. No surname. No title. The handwriting leaned downhill. Harry opened it.

The card inside showed a Quidditch goal hoop attempting to escape three orange balls. When he unfolded it, an untidy line of writing appeared beneath a moving Chudley Cannons crest.

Mum says invitations ought to be delivered properly, but Errol's resting and Pig keeps trying to carry the cake instead. Happy birthday for Monday just gone. I know this is late. Mum says you can't rely on the Ministry to organise anything, including calendars. — Ron

Beneath it, in a different hand, Fred and George had written:

We would like it entered into the official record that we advised against trusting Pig with baked goods.

Then Ginny:

They gave him the cake twice.

A final note had been squeezed along the edge.

Are you allowed visitors? If not, say yes anyway and we'll work around it.

Harry read the card again. It was not eloquent. It did not declare unwavering faith in his testimony, demand details about the graveyard or apologise for every year in which he and Ron had occupied the same dormitory without becoming friends. It was slightly crushed and very nearly ordinary.

"That one looks better. Shall I put it with the supportive letters?" Hermione asked.

Harry glanced at the labelled piles.

"I think I'll keep it," Harry said. "It doesn't need a pile."

He slipped the card into his pocket. Draconia noticed. She noticed everything. She said nothing.

The next owl carried six letters from strangers who believed him. The one after that delivered a request from the Daily Prophet to photograph his scars. Sirius burned the request without consulting anyone. A barn owl brought a parcel containing dried sage, a wooden stake and instructions for recognising demonic possession. Hermione began reading the instructions in disbelief until Draconia plucked them from her hand and put them with the hostile post. "You can correct their research later. Eat something first."

At eight, Lucius entered to find an owl drinking from his coffee. He stopped. The owl continued.

"I see Potter's correspondence has become democratic."

"The public has opinions," Sirius said.

"The public should be required to pass an examination first."

Lucius removed the cup from beneath the bird's beak and conjured another. He was dressed for London in dark robes with a high collar, his hair tied at the nape of his neck. Since the hearing, three separate newspapers had printed photographs of him beside words such as DEFECTOR, OPPORTUNIST and TRAITOR. He had arranged all three articles in a folder and corrected their legal terminology in red ink. Narcissa placed toast beside his hand.

"You needn't go today," she said.

"That is precisely why I do."

"You have nothing to prove to the Ministry."

"I have a great deal to prove to the Ministry. None of it concerns courage."

His gaze moved to Harry. It did not linger upon the place the owl's claws had caught him, but Harry saw the calculation happen.

"The Department of Magical Transportation has agreed to distribute copies of the finding to every licensed Floo office," Lucius said. "They have not agreed willingly."

"What changed their minds?" Hermione asked.

"I reminded the director that his department has mislaid fourteen thousand Galleons over three years."

"Did he mislay them?"

"The figures are in his department's accounts. I suggested he explain them before accusing anyone else of misleading the public."

Sirius grinned into his tea. Harry sat at the table and accepted a bowl from Dobby. The porridge was warm, sweetened with honey and sliced apple, and for once he did not have to argue with his stomach before taking the first mouthful. Conversation moved around him while Hermione read aloud the names of sympathetic witnesses, Draconia annotated a list of households likely to receive the transcript, and Lucius explained why three Wizengamot members who had applauded Fudge yesterday were privately requesting meetings today. They were doing what Harry had asked. They were copying the finding, answering people who might help and refusing to let Fudge's statement become the only version anyone read.

He had not understood that larger rooms made more space for noise. Every letter carried a version of him. Hero. Fraud. Victim. Threat. Brave child. Dangerous liar. The Boy Who Lived had survived the graveyard; Harry Potter was now expected to explain precisely how, preferably in a manner suitable for breakfast reading. He pushed the porridge around his bowl. Remus sat opposite him.

"Ten o'clock," he said.

Harry looked up.

"For what?"

"Our first lesson."

The conversation nearest them quietened. Harry remembered the promise made after the hearing: no locked doors, no surprises, no one entering his mind without permission. It had sounded manageable while everyone was discussing it in a Ministry corridor. Morning made the idea more solid.

"Today?"

"If you still want to."

Sirius set down his mug. "It can wait."

Remus did not look away from Harry. "It can."

The choice settled between them without being disguised as anything else. Harry glanced at the owls, the letters and the black Ministry seal upon the transcript. His pulse had slowed, but he could still feel the exact curve of the bird's claws through his shirt.

"All right. Ten o'clock," he said. "But we stop if I need to."

Remus chose the morning room at the back of Hawthorn because it had three windows, one door and nothing upon the walls which moved. The room had not yet found a permanent purpose. A faded settee stood beneath the longest window, its green upholstery worn pale where years of sunlight had fallen. Two armchairs faced the empty fireplace. Stacks of books occupied the floor because Sirius had declared shelves an oppressive form of organisation and Hermione had not yet forced him to reconsider. Beyond the glass, the rear lawn sloped towards a line of beeches. Rain left the garden silver and drew soft threads down the windowpanes. Remus moved the armchairs until they faced one another at an angle.

"You don't have to make it look like therapy," Harry said.

"I don't know what Muggle therapy looks like."

"Neither do I. That probably means you've done it perfectly."

Remus smiled and sat. The door remained open. Sirius occupied the corridor outside with a newspaper he had not turned for six minutes. Harry could see one bare foot beyond the frame.

"Subtle," Harry called.

The foot withdrew.

"I have no idea what you mean," Sirius said from considerably farther away than he had been.

Remus waited until Harry chose the second chair. There was no wand upon his lap. He had placed it on the windowsill in plain sight, far enough away that reaching it would require a deliberate movement. Harry noticed that too.

"Before we begin," Remus said, "I want to remove one expectation."

"Only one?"

"We have an hour. I am being realistic."

Harry settled deeper into the chair. The upholstery smelled faintly of dust and lavender. "All right."

"Occlumency is commonly taught as defence against invasion. Walls. Doors. Discipline. The ability to conceal one thought beneath another. That may eventually matter, particularly if Voldemort used Legilimency against you and attempts to do so again."

The name tightened the room. Harry fixed his eyes upon a bead of rain travelling down the nearest pane.

"But?"

"But first you need a way to recognise when the memory is taking over. If you're back in the graveyard every time you try to concentrate, making yourself push harder won't help. We'll start with getting you back into this room."

Harry looked back. Remus's expression remained gentle. It was not pity. Harry had become skilled at recognising pity because adults tended to offer it with their heads inclined and voices made falsely soft. Remus waited while Harry looked at the rain. He did not repeat himself or try to catch Harry's eye.

"So no mind reading," Harry said.

"Not today."

"And next time?"

"Not unless you agree beforehand. I won't change the lesson while you're sitting in it."

"What if I never ask?"

"Then we find another method."

Harry did not know what to do with teachers who permitted refusal. At Hogwarts, refusal became defiance, defiance became detention, and detention became an explanation of why the adult had been correct to begin with. Even kind teachers expected obedience when danger appeared. Remus had once asked him to confront a boggart, but he had also removed Harry from the lesson when he believed the shape would endanger everyone else. He made decisions. Adults did. Yet the wand remained upon the sill.

"What do I have to do?" Harry asked.

Remus reached beside his chair and produced four objects from a canvas bag: a smooth grey stone, a sprig of rosemary, a small square of chocolate and a red glass marble. He arranged them upon the low table, then glanced towards the brass key hanging beneath Harry's shirt.

"We may not need a fifth."

Harry stared.

"This is the advanced magic?"

"Terrifying, isn't it?"

"The chocolate might be cursed."

"Only by quality. Sirius bought it."

From the corridor came an offended sound.

Remus ignored it. "Pick one."

Harry drew the Hawthorn key over his head and chose it. It was ordinary, heavier than it looked, with three uneven teeth and a worn oval bow. When he rubbed his thumb across the metal, it left a dull warmth upon his skin.

"Describe it," Remus said.

"It's a key."

"I had hoped your education might have progressed farther."

Harry glanced up. Remus's mouth curved.

"Brass," Harry said. "Old. Scratched along one side. It has three teeth. One is chipped."

"What does it feel like?"

"Heavy. Warmer now."

"What can you hear?"

Harry listened. Rain touched the windows. Somewhere upstairs, a pipe knocked twice. A quill scratched in the library where Hermione and Draconia were addressing envelopes. From the garden came the faint, wet call of a wood pigeon.

"Rain. The pipes. Someone writing. A bird."

"What can you smell?"

"The chair. Dust. Rosemary."

"What year is it?"

Harry's fingers stopped moving.

"Nineteen ninety-five."

"Where are you?"

"Hawthorn."

"Who is in the house?"

Harry began with Sirius because that answer stood just beyond the door. Then Remus. Hermione, Draconia, Narcissa, Lucius, Dobby and Winky. Cedric had returned to Ottery St Catchpole with his parents after the hearing. Tonks was on duty. Kingsley had left before breakfast. The list steadied something.

"And what is happening now?" Remus asked.

"You're making me describe a key."

"Exactly."

Harry looked down at the brass in his palm.

"That's it?"

"That is the beginning. When a memory takes the shape of the present, argument is rarely useful. Your body believes before your thoughts can object. Give it information. Texture. Sound. Place. Time. People."

"And then it stops?"

Remus considered the question before answering.

"Sometimes. Other times it helps you get through the worst of it until the memory eases. I don't want to promise you a cure because you described five things correctly."

Harry rolled the key between his fingers.

"You do this?"

"Yes."

The simple answer brought Harry's gaze up again. Remus looked towards the rain. His scars appeared paler in the grey light, thin lines passing through one eyebrow and down his cheek.

"After transformations?" Harry asked.

"After many things."

"The moon?"

"The moon. The war. Certain dreams."

He might have ended there. Most adults did, offering truth in fragments small enough to preserve the boundary between their suffering and Harry's. Remus picked up the stone instead.

"For years, I woke expecting the dormitory at Hogwarts," he said. "Not because every memory there was happy. Some were humiliating, several were painful, and at least a quarter involved your father making a noise nobody should be able to produce with a trumpet. But there were people in that room. When I woke somewhere else, the absence arrived before I understood what I was missing."

Harry's grip tightened upon the key.

"Sirius?"

"Sirius. James. Peter, before we knew. Lily, in the common room whenever she had decided James required supervision."

"Did he?"

"Constantly."

Harry tried to imagine his father at fifteen: noisy, watched, irritating enough to require organised intervention. Photographs had given James a face, other people's stories a collection of gestures, but grief flattened the dead into their most important acts. James had saved Lily. James had stood before Voldemort without a wand. James had died. The boy with the trumpet felt harder to hold and therefore more real.

"What noise?" Harry asked.

Remus's smile deepened. "I will teach you many things, Harry. That will never be one of them."

"Sirius will."

"Sirius has been threatened."

"By you?"

"By everyone who has heard it."

Harry laughed softly. The key warmed further in his palm. Remus had him close his eyes after that, but he did not ask for emptiness. He asked Harry to notice where the chair supported his back, where his shoes met the carpet and how his breath changed when he attempted to control it. The exercise seemed insultingly easy until Harry discovered he could not take ten slow breaths without his mind returning to a white hand, a wand and a voice asking whether it hurt. At the fourth breath, his shoulders locked.

"Open your eyes," Remus said.

Harry did. Rain. Green chair. Brass key.

"Name the room."

"Morning room."

"The year."

"Nineteen ninety-five."

"One person nearby."

Harry looked towards the door. Sirius's foot had returned.

"An exceptionally bad spy."

"I heard that," Sirius said.

The tension loosened enough for air to enter. Remus did not praise him. Harry was grateful. Praise would have made the moment feel like failure disguised by kindness. They tried again.

By the end of the hour, Harry could not have said he possessed a stronger mind. He possessed a key, two squares of bad chocolate and the knowledge that the old house made seven distinct sounds when rain settled over it. That was less impressive than throwing off an invading Dark wizard. It was also the first lesson in months which left him feeling more present at the end than at the beginning. At the door, Remus handed him a thin black notebook. Harry looked at it warily.

"If it writes back, I'm burning it."

Draconia's quill stopped in the next room.

Remus's expression did not change, but his gaze flicked briefly towards the wall. "It won't. This one is for dreams. Dates, details, what woke you and how long it took to recognise where you were. You needn't show me anything you don't choose to."

"Then how does it help?"

"Patterns become easier to see once they exist somewhere outside your head."

Harry turned the blank book over.

"Hermione will love it."

"Hermione suggested colour coding. I refused on your behalf."

"You really are my favourite professor."

Remus's face changed at the word. The pleasure was brief, almost startled. Harry might not have caught it if he had not spent years watching adults for smaller signs of anger.

"Former professor," Remus corrected.

"Technicality."

Sirius pushed away from the corridor wall as Harry emerged. The newspaper in his hands was upside down. Harry looked at it. Sirius looked at it too.

"The news has been difficult to follow lately."

"Were you there the whole time?"

"No."

"Your foot was."

"Independent creature. Never trusted it."

Remus came through behind them and collected his tea.

"He worked hard," he told Sirius.

Harry opened his mouth.

"It was difficult," Remus added before Harry could object. "He stayed with it. That's enough for a first lesson."

The objection vanished. Sirius did not clap him upon the shoulder or ask what they had discussed. He merely nodded once, as though Harry had accomplished something Sirius understood and would not cheapen by naming too loudly.

"Good," he said. "Then you're both free to help me with a small problem."

He led them into the dining room. Twenty-six paper streamers hovered beneath the ceiling. Half were scarlet and gold. Half were black, and every third one hissed when anyone passed beneath it. An enormous banner stretched from one wall to the other. HAPPY FIFTITH BIRTHDAY, HARRY. Harry read it twice.

"Small problem?"

"The letters keep moving."

As they watched, an E detached itself from FIFTITH and settled after the second F. HAPPY FIFTEITH BIRTHDAY, HARRY. Remus pinched the bridge of his nose.

"His birthday was on Monday. The party is next Monday."

"I'm planning."

"You're losing to spelling three days before the party."

Sirius aimed his wand at the banner. The whole sentence rearranged itself. HARRY, BIRTHDAY FIFTEEN HAPPY.

"It has your instincts," Harry said.

Sirius lowered his wand.

"I was going to ask whether you wanted a party."

The room altered around the question. Harry looked at the floating streamers. One black chain had begun stalking a gold chain across the ceiling. The gold one fled behind the chandelier.

He had experienced birthdays as things which happened to other people. Dudley's were organised campaigns: mountains of wrapping paper, day trips, cake large enough to feed people Dudley had not invited. Harry's arrived quietly. A coat hanger counted the years upon the cupboard wall. Once there had been a cake in a hut upon the sea, crushed beneath Hagrid's coat and bright enough to make eleven feel like a door opening. After that came presents by owl, feasts among friends and the annual astonishment of being remembered.

His birthday fell long after the school departures. By the end of July, the castle was empty, and he had usually spent much of the month at Privet Drive. Even when friends remembered, the waiting had a way of clinging to the day. Nobody had ever asked what kind of celebration he wanted.

"How many people?" he said.

Sirius's face brightened with dangerous speed.

"As many as the house can survive."

"That isn't a number."

"It is in this family."

Remus looked at Harry. "You may choose small."

Harry imagined the kitchen with only the people already inside it. He imagined Cedric and his parents. The Grangers. Neville, who had sent a letter written slowly and without a single question about the graveyard. Luna, who had sent a feather tied around a pebble and explained that it was either protective or decorative depending upon Harry's immediate needs. The Weasleys, loud enough to overwhelm any room and kind enough to make the noise feel offered rather than imposed. He imagined the house containing them all. The old instinct warned him not to want too much. Wanting allowed people to take things away. Harry slipped a hand into his pocket and touched Ron's card.

"Large," he said.

Sirius grinned. Above them, the banner corrected itself. HAPPY FIFTEENTH BIRTHDAY, HARRY.

"See?" Sirius said. "It respects confidence."

"Or Remus fixed it while you were talking."

Remus placed both hands innocently behind his back. The E was still glowing at the tip of his wand.

The house spent the weekend pretending the party was a secret. This proved difficult when Hagrid delivered a barrel of elderflower wine through the dining-room window, Molly Weasley sent six separate instructions concerning oven temperatures, and Sirius disappeared into Diagon Alley with Narcissa and returned carrying a parcel large enough to contain a small motorcycle.

"It doesn't," he told Harry, when asked.

"That wasn't what I asked."

"Good. Then I haven't lied."

Hermione confiscated the guest list after discovering that Sirius had written Minerva? beside three different names and invited the conductor of the Knight Bus because he had once allowed Sirius to ride upon the roof. Draconia redesigned the table plan, then denied having designed one at all when Harry found it concealed beneath a copy of Transfiguration Today.

"Those are attempts to keep your godfather from seating people beside their oldest enemies," she said. "You may thank me afterwards."

"You put your father on the other side of the garden from Mr Weasley."

"Precisely. I would like the argument to remain theoretical."

"And Snape isn't on it."

"I invited him. He hasn't answered. I'm allowing myself a little optimism."

Harry leaned against the library table and examined the small diagram. Draconia had placed his chair between hers and Hermione's, with Sirius opposite and Remus close enough to speak without raising his voice. Cedric sat beyond Hermione, near enough for Harry to speak to him. Luna was near Hermione. Ron had been assigned a place between Fred and George, which struck Harry as less a seating arrangement than a punishment.

"You planned where everyone sits at my party."

"I prevented disaster at your party."

"They're very similar shapes."

Draconia reached for the parchment. Harry moved it beyond her fingers. She stepped closer. The library windows stood open to the warm afternoon, allowing the scent of cut grass and the steady hum of insects to enter. Sunlight reached through the leaves outside and broke across Draconia's face in shifting pieces. She had abandoned formal robes for a sleeveless green dress because the house was hot, and the repaired bracelet slipped towards her elbow when she reached, its clouded stone catching the light. Harry's gaze caught upon it. Draconia lowered her arm. He looked away too late.

"Give it back," she said.

"Say please."

"You require manners now?"

"It's one of the many changes confidence has made."

She advanced another step. The edge of the table pressed into Harry's hip. He could have passed the paper over her shoulder. He could have moved. Instead, he held it above his head and discovered that Draconia had to rise onto the balls of her feet to reach. Her hand caught his wrist. The movement brought them too close. Neither seemed to have calculated that part.

Harry became conscious of absurd details: the cool pressure of her fingers around his pulse, a loose strand of white-blonde hair against her cheek and the fact that her eyes were not one colour. Grey gathered around the pupils, paling towards the edges until the sunlight made them almost silver. Draconia's attention dropped to his mouth.

She looked away almost at once, but Harry had seen the glance. His raised arm suddenly felt awkward, and he could not decide whether lowering it would make the distance between them easier or harder to manage. Harry's heart performed something unreliable.

"I could summon it," she said, quieter than before.

"You could."

"Or hex you."

"That would prove you made a seating plan."

"Everyone knows I made a seating plan."

"Then this is about pride."

"Some things are. Keeping this list out of your hands is becoming one of them."

"That explains your friendship with me."

Her mouth nearly became a smile.

"No," she said, her fingers easing around his wrist. "You know perfectly well that isn't why I stayed."

Hermione entered carrying a stack of folded napkins. She stopped. Harry and Draconia moved apart so quickly that the table shuddered. A bottle of ink tipped upon its side. Hermione caught it with a wordless spell before a single drop reached the wood. Her gaze travelled from Draconia's face to Harry's raised arm, then to the table plan still held above his head.

"Am I interrupting?"

"He stole the seating plan," Draconia said. "I'm trying to recover it without damaging the furniture."

"She wouldn't say please," Harry said at the same time.

Hermione placed the napkins upon the table.

"How reassuring. For a moment I thought something foolish was happening."

"He was being irritating. That's all," Draconia said, smoothing a corner of the plan she hadn't yet retrieved.

"Yes, I can see how irritating he was," Hermione said. She looked at Harry's raised hand, and he hurriedly lowered it.

There was humour in Hermione's voice, but something else passed beneath it too quickly for Harry to name. She took the plan from his unresisting hand, examined it and made two adjustments with Draconia's quill.

"Neville shouldn't sit beside Professor Moody," she said. "He'll spend the entire meal preparing for an interrogation."

"Moody is not coming," Draconia replied.

"He has been invited."

"That is not the same thing."

"With Moody, it usually is."

Harry watched Hermione bend over the page. Her hair had been pulled into a loose knot, but the heat had freed curls around her neck. She chewed the end of the quill while considering whether Arthur Weasley could safely be placed beside Xenophilius Lovegood.

He liked the line which appeared between her brows when she concentrated. He liked that she corrected plans she claimed not to approve. He liked the ease with which her shoulder settled against his when he leaned over the table, as though neither of them needed to consider the contact first. Two different kinds of quiet existed around the girls. With Hermione, Harry could forget to measure the distance. With Draconia, he seemed capable of measuring nothing else.

Knowing how they felt had not taught him what to do when Draconia looked at his mouth or Hermione's shoulder rested against his. They had spoken by the lake; nothing had been settled there. At fifteen, most of his examples of courtship involved Dudley lying over the telephone or couples disappearing behind tapestries at Hogwarts. Neither offered much help with the people Harry most wanted to keep. Hermione glanced sideways and caught him looking.

"What is it? Have I put someone else beside Moody?"

"No. I was just watching you work."

"That sounds suspiciously like staring."

Draconia folded her arms.

"He's developed a habit of it," Draconia said. "It's quite distracting when one is trying to be cross with him."

"I was wondering whether that quill's edible," Harry said.

Hermione took it out of her mouth. "I don't actually eat them."

Draconia reached past Harry to inspect the chewed end. "You might have mentioned that before borrowing mine."

Hermione's cheeks coloured. She set the quill beside the ink bottle with unusual care. Harry took the table plan and escaped before either girl could demand it back. Confidence, he decided in the corridor, was useful but dangerously temporary.

In the dream, a hand closed around Harry's throat before he could turn away from the headstone. For a moment, he could feel each finger against his skin. For one breath, the graveyard existed whole.

Cold fingers forced his head against stone. The night above him was black and enormous. Blood filled his mouth. Somewhere close, Cedric made no sound at all, and that silence was worse than screaming because it meant Harry had failed to keep the binding alive.

Look at him, Voldemort whispered. Look what your weakness costs.

Harry struck at the wrist. His knuckles hit plaster. Pain opened across his hand. The bedroom rushed into place around him: blue-grey dawn, twisted sheets, the wardrobe standing open and his own brass key clenched so tightly in his other fist that its teeth had marked his palm. It was Monday, the morning of the party. He could hear a bird beyond the window and someone moving downstairs, sounds that belonged to a house waking for a celebration rather than another night in the graveyard. He dragged air into his lungs. Hawthorn. Nineteen ninety-five. Rain had stopped. A blackbird was singing somewhere beyond the glass. The room smelled of cold ashes and the soap Dobby used upon the bed linen.

Someone was outside the door. Harry reached for his wand. Three soft knocks followed.

"Harry?"

Sirius. Harry released the wand.

"Come in."

The door opened immediately. Sirius wore loose black trousers and a shirt fastened with the wrong buttons. He carried a tray containing tea, toast and something beneath a silver cover which rattled of its own accord. His gaze moved first to Harry's face, then to the dent in the plaster.

"Bad wall?"

Harry looked at his scraped knuckles.

"Started it."

"They do. No sense of boundaries."

Sirius entered, nudged the door wider with one foot and set the tray across Harry's knees. He did not ask what the dream contained. He sat upon the edge of the bed and waited while Harry wrapped both hands around the tea.

The mug warmed his fingers slowly. Harry held it against the heel of his scraped hand and listened to the tray rattling whenever he shifted, letting those small, ordinary sensations take the place of the stone at his back.

"Hawthorn," Harry said.

Sirius nodded.

"Nineteen ninety-five."

"Unfortunately. The music has been dreadful."

"You're here."

The joke disappeared from Sirius's face.

"Yes."

Harry swallowed.

"I'm fifteen."

"For a whole week now. You're bearing up remarkably well."

"I thought I'd be taller."

"Give it until breakfast."

Sirius lifted the silver cover. A small black cake sat beneath it. The icing had been applied with more enthusiasm than control. Fifteen narrow candles leaned in different directions, and the words HAPPY BIRTHDAY HARRY curved down one side because the top had run out of space. Near the final Y, something moved. Harry squinted.

"Is that a paw print?"

"No."

"It has claws."

"Decoration."

"Did you stand on my cake?"

"Not in this form."

Harry laughed. It came out rough after the dream, but it came. Sirius lit the candles with his wand. Their flames burned blue at the centre.

"Make a wish."

Harry looked at him. There had been years when wishes were not even worth the breath they required. A cake did not make them safer. Hope remained a thing with an exposed throat.

Still, fifteen small lights waited upon a crooked cake made by someone who had risen before dawn because Harry had once admitted that nobody had brought breakfast to his bed. Harry closed his eyes. He wished for nothing to change.

The foolishness of it hurt at once. Everything changed. Parents answered doors and died. Friends boarded trains and returned covered in blood. People could disappear between one promise and the next. Homes stopped being safe the moment one loved them enough to fear their loss. Harry opened his eyes before the graveyard could reclaim the room.

"You have to blow them out," Sirius said.

"I know how birthdays work."

"Evidence suggests otherwise."

Harry took a breath and extinguished every flame. Smoke curled towards the ceiling. Sirius applauded.

"Astonishing. Natural talent. James once set his fringe on fire."

"Really?"

"No. But he did try to eat the candles when he turned sixteen."

"Why?"

"Peter told him they were sugar."

"And he believed him?"

"James was very committed to being first at things."

Harry smiled at the cake.

"I want to know the stupid stories."

Sirius went still.

Harry continued before he could lose the sentence. "Not just how they died. Or what they did during the war. I want to know what they were like when nothing important was happening."

The morning remained pale around them. Sirius looked down at his hands. Azkaban had left itself upon them in the prominent bones and fine tremor which appeared whenever he was tired. It had taken twelve years of ordinary memories from him too. Twelve birthdays, arguments, bad jokes and afternoons nobody had thought important enough to preserve.

"Your father cheated at Exploding Snap," he said at last.

"I knew there was something wrong with him."

"He called it tactical card management."

"Draconia's going to love him."

"Lily always knew when he did it. She let him win three times, then challenged him in front of the entire common room and took twelve Sickles off him."

"How did she know?"

"She cheated better."

Harry cut the cake with the breakfast knife. It was slightly burnt on one side and dense enough on the other to defeat the knife at first. They ate it anyway while Sirius told him about James attempting to enchant every staircase in Gryffindor Tower to announce Lily's arrival and accidentally causing them to sing whenever Professor McGonagall passed. Harry laughed until his ribs hurt. Once, the sound caught unexpectedly behind his breastbone, too close to grief, but Sirius waited and the room did not vanish. After the second piece, Sirius stood.

"There's something else."

"More cake?"

"I love you too much."

He retrieved a long parcel from the corridor. Harry recognised the paper from Diagon Alley, though Narcissa had replaced Sirius's knots with a dark green ribbon tied so precisely it seemed severe.

"This is why you needed help?"

"I didn't need help. Your ribbon is judgemental."

Harry pulled it loose. Inside lay a black leather jacket. For several seconds, he only looked. The leather was soft but heavy, cut close through the body with silver fastenings and a high collar which could be turned against the wind. It was not dressy in the polished Malfoy manner or practical in the shapeless Dursley sense. It looked like something Sirius might have worn at seventeen while escaping school rules upon a motorcycle. Harry touched one sleeve.

"Was it Dad's?"

Sirius answered without hesitation.

"No."

Harry glanced up.

"I thought about giving you his," Sirius said. "I've got one in storage. James wore it the summer after sixth year, mostly because Lily said she hated it and he knew she was lying. It would probably fit."

"But?"

Sirius lifted the new jacket from the box and held it out.

"You have enough things which belonged to the dead."

The words were not unkind. That made them land more deeply. Harry owned his father's face, his mother's eyes, a cloak handed through generations and a vault filled because two people had expected to return. He had inherited a war before he understood what one was. Adults kept searching him for proof of James, Lily or Voldemort, as though Harry's body were a room other people had left possessions inside. The jacket smelled of new leather.

There were no worn creases belonging to someone else's arms and no story he was expected to remember whenever he put it on. Harry turned the sleeve over in his hand, tracing the clean stitching.

"It's yours," Sirius said. "Not his."

Harry took it. The sleeves were fractionally long, but the shoulders fit. When he pulled it on, the weight settled around him like armour which had not yet been tested. Sirius smiled. Not because Harry resembled James. Harry could tell the difference now.

"Well?" Harry asked.

"You look alarmingly like someone old enough to form opinions."

"I've had those for years."

"Yes, but now strangers may listen to them. Terrible responsibility."

Harry turned towards the mirror inside the wardrobe door. The boy looking back remained too thin. Dark circles still lived beneath his eyes, and the scar at his temple caught pale against untidy hair. The jacket did not conceal any of it. It gave the thinness edges and made the untidiness look nearly deliberate. He looked older. Not healed. Not whole in any simple way. His.

"Thank you," Harry said.

Sirius's smile became smaller and more real. "Happy birthday."

Harry hugged him. It was still not something either of them did easily. Sirius froze for half a heartbeat, as if affection remained an ambush he had not trained himself to survive. Then his arms closed around Harry with careful force. Harry pressed his face into Sirius's shoulder and breathed leather, smoke and the strong tea he had spilt down his shirt. The dream lingered at the edges of the room. It did not own the morning.

Hermione and Draconia were waiting in the kitchen. This was obvious before Harry entered because their argument had reached the landing.

"It changes the nature of the gift," Hermione said.

"It makes the gift usable."

"It was already usable."

"Not while wearing that."

Harry descended the last three stairs. Both girls fell silent. Sirius, following behind him, made a pleased sound under his breath. Draconia sat at the table in a fitted summer robe the colour of dark ivy. Hermione wore a blue dress Harry had never seen, with short sleeves and a row of small white buttons at the front. Her curls fell loose around her shoulders. Harry forgot what he had intended to say. Hermione's eyes moved slowly over the jacket. Colour rose in her face, though the kitchen was cool. Draconia did not blush. She simply stopped breathing for long enough that Harry noticed.

"Is something wrong with it?" Harry asked.

"No, not at all," Hermione said, then realised how quickly she had answered. "I was looking at the jacket."

"You've been looking for quite a while."

Hermione looked up at him properly. "It suits you. The jacket."

"You could have said that first."

"I was going to."

Sirius covered a laugh with a cough.

Draconia recovered first. "Turn around."

"Good morning to you too."

"The jacket. Turn."

Harry obeyed, partly because he was curious and partly because Draconia's voice had taken on the tone she used when expecting the world to arrange itself correctly. Her fingers touched the back seam, moved across one shoulder and paused at his side.

"Sirius," she said.

"Yes?"

"You bought him a jacket without a wand opening."

"It has pockets."

"Not where he can reach them quickly. Let me show you what I mean before you tell me how well you managed at school."

"Mine has been very useful in duels."

"That explains several things about your criminal history."

She stepped in front of Harry and held out a narrow parcel wrapped in silver paper.

"Happy birthday."

Inside was a wand holster made from black dragonhide, lined with dark green velvet and fitted with a silver clasp no larger than Harry's thumbnail. Unlike the school holsters sold in Diagon Alley, it was shaped to sit beneath a sleeve or along the inside of a coat. Fine protective runes had been stitched into the leather with silver thread. Harry traced one.

"Did you make this?"

"I bought the leatherwork. The release charm and the fastening are mine, with Bill checking the runes before I gave it to you."

"She destroyed three," Hermione said.

"Those were prototypes, and one of them worked perfectly until someone startled me."

"One attached itself to your bedpost."

"I know. I was there when it refused to come off."

"You slept in the chair."

Draconia looked at Hermione. "Did you want to give him your present?"

Hermione smiled serenely. "Not yet. This is interesting."

Harry held out the holster. "How does it go on?"

Draconia's confidence faltered for the smallest instant.

"The inner seam," she said.

"Can you show me?"

Sirius leaned against the dresser with shameless interest. Hermione suddenly became fascinated by the sugar bowl. Draconia rose.

"Take it off."

Harry removed the jacket and handed it to her. She laid it upon the table, opened the lining with a whispered spell and fastened the holster into place. Her movements were practised enough to suggest she had measured more than the jacket. When she finished, she held it while Harry pushed his arms into the sleeves again.

"May I have your wand? I need to check the fit with it inside," she said.

He gave it to her. She slid the wand into the concealed holster along his right forearm, then tightened the fastening. Her fingertips moved beneath the leather, cool against the inside of his wrist. Harry's pulse betrayed him at once. Draconia felt it. Her gaze lifted.

Harry could hear Sirius set down his cup behind them. Hermione was examining the sugar bowl with an attention it could not possibly deserve, and Draconia's fingers were still against his wrist. He wished everyone would either say something or leave him a little less aware of their silence.

"Try drawing it," she said. "Slowly the first time. It should release when you turn your wrist."

Harry flexed his hand. The holster released his wand smoothly into his palm.

"Once more, so you know how it feels. Then I'll stop fussing."

He returned the wand and tried again, finding the small turn of his right wrist easier the second time.

"That's much easier than reaching into a pocket," he said.

"That was the idea. Although you might try looking a little more impressed after three prototypes."

"I meant the gift."

"I know what you meant."

But her thumb lingered once over the fastening before she stepped away. Harry looked at the jacket. Sirius had given him something which belonged to no one else. Draconia had altered it so it could protect him.

"Thank you," he said, and allowed the seriousness beneath the words to remain visible.

Draconia's expression softened.

"You're welcome. I wanted it to be something you'd actually use, not another thing people expect you to keep safe."

Hermione placed her own present in front of him. It was a book bound in russet-brown leather, larger than the dream journal Remus had given him and fastened with a simple brass clasp. Harry opened it cautiously. On the first page, Hermione had written:

This book is not evidence.

Beneath it, in smaller letters:

You are permitted to remember things because they were good.

Harry read the lines twice. The pages which followed were not blank. Hermione had filled the first several with dates and fragments gathered from the past four years: the exact wording of a joke Hagrid had made in their first winter; the time Draconia transfigured Pansy Parkinson's hair ribbon into a grass snake and insisted it had been an error; Harry's first Quidditch victory; a list of every sweet they had rated upon the Hogwarts Express, including Draconia's formal objection to liquorice wands. Some entries contained moving photographs. In one, the three of them stood beside the lake in second year, Hermione speaking too quickly while Draconia rolled her eyes and Harry laughed at something beyond the frame.

There were empty spaces after each memory.

"For corrections," Hermione said. "Or anything you remember differently. I know memories aren't records. I thought—"

She stopped, suddenly uncertain. Harry turned another page. A pressed sprig of heather lay beneath a preserving charm. Scotland, third year. The day after Sirius became free.

"You thought what?"

Hermione folded her hands. "We keep asking you to record nightmares, symptoms, evidence and things people have done to you. I thought you might like somewhere for the good things too. You don't have to write every day, or show anyone. I just wanted there to be room for them."

Harry closed the book carefully. Then he hugged her. Hermione made a small sound of surprise before her arms came around him. She was warm, softer than the leather against his chest, and for a few seconds Harry let the safe familiarity of her hold him without making a joke of it.

"Thank you," he murmured into her hair.

"You're welcome."

When they separated, Hermione kept one hand upon his sleeve. Draconia watched them. Her face revealed nothing to anyone who did not know her. Harry knew her. He reached out, caught two of her fingers and tugged her towards them.

"Absolutely not," Draconia said.

Hermione seized her other hand.

"You slept in a chair for that holster."

"If either of you mentions this at school, I'll deny every word."

"Birthday rule," Harry said.

"There is no birthday rule."

"There is now."

Draconia looked from one to the other, then let herself be drawn closer, still pretending that the whole affair was beneath her. Draconia remained rigid for three seconds. Then one arm circled Harry's waist and the other settled across Hermione's shoulders. Sirius cleared his throat noisily.

"Should I leave?"

"Yes," all three said.

He grinned. The front bell rang. Hawthorn woke around them. Doors opened. Dobby raced through the hall shouting that guests had arrived too early. Winky shouted that the guests were precisely on time and someone else was late. Narcissa descended the stairs fastening one earring. Lucius emerged from his study with the expression of a man who had hoped birthdays could be resolved through correspondence. Hermione released them first.

"That will be my parents."

"Or an assassin," Draconia said, smoothing her robes.

Harry returned his wand to the new holster.

"Either way, we should answer it."

He reached the front door before Dobby. Harry opened the door and found people who had come to celebrate him. He was still learning how to greet them without looking past their shoulders for some other reason they might be there.

The Grangers stood upon the step beneath a sky too bright for English reliability. Mrs Granger carried a flat parcel wrapped in blue paper. Mr Granger held a potted lemon tree against his chest and peered around its leaves with the patient resignation of a man whose wife had assured him the plant would fit through the door.

"Happy birthday," Mrs Granger said.

She hugged Harry before he could prepare. That no longer made him freeze, though some part of him still registered every adult embrace as an event requiring explanation.

"Thank you."

Mr Granger shifted the tree into one arm and offered Harry his hand. Harry took it.

"Fifteen," Mr Granger said. "An excellent age for developing strong opinions and pretending they appeared overnight."

"He has made an early start," Hermione called from the hall.

Her father looked past Harry and smiled. "I wondered where he'd learnt efficiency."

The lemon tree was a gift for Hawthorn rather than Harry, Mrs Granger explained as they manoeuvred it inside. Every home required something living in a pot, particularly when most of the other living things arrived by chimney. She said this directly to Sirius, who came down the stairs in a black waistcoat embroidered with silver stars and accepted the statement as if it were a customary housewarming law.

"Does it do anything?" he asked.

"It produces lemons."

"Voluntarily?"

Mr Granger set the pot beside the drawing-room window. "With water and patience."

"Muggle herbology is brutal."

The next knock belonged to Neville and his grandmother. Augusta Longbottom wore a tall green hat decorated with a stuffed vulture which turned its head independently and regarded Lucius Malfoy with personal suspicion. Neville hovered behind her carrying a paper-wrapped parcel and the expression of someone entering a room where all the furniture might test him.

"Potter," Augusta said.

"Mrs Longbottom."

She examined him from shoes to scar.

"Your testimony was clear."

"Thank you."

"Your refusal to answer the sensational questions was clearer. Frank would have approved."

Neville's head came up. Augusta had already moved past Harry to greet Narcissa, leaving the sentence behind as though it had cost her nothing. Neville watched his grandmother cross the hall.

"She practised that," he said quietly.

Harry looked at him.

"Twice," Neville added. "The first version was longer."

"I liked this one."

"She said you would. Happy birthday."

His parcel contained a small plant in a clay pot. Four blue leaves curled around a central bud with tiny white teeth.

"It doesn't bite hard," Neville said quickly. "And only if it's thirsty. The leaves change colour when the weather shifts. I thought it might be useful if you're flying."

The nearest leaf snapped at Harry's thumb.

"It likes you."

"That explanation follows me around."

"Don't feed it meat," Neville said. "It learns."

Harry placed the pot on the entrance table where it immediately attempted to eat Sirius's glove. Within twenty minutes, Hawthorn ceased to resemble any house Harry had known and became something more like a railway station during an unusually affectionate emergency.

The Weasleys arrived through the Floo in a sequence of green flame, soot and shouting. Molly appeared first, carrying two covered dishes and correcting Fred before he had technically entered. Arthur followed with Ginny, three more dishes and a Muggle electric whisk he had discovered in a charity shop. George stepped through holding the whisk's severed plug. Fred emerged with the cable, insisting that this counted as a complete object when they stood together. Ron came last with Pigwidgeon trapped beneath one arm and an orange parcel under the other.

"He got away," Ron explained.

Pigwidgeon shrieked in triumph.

"From what?" Harry asked.

"Common sense."

Ron released the owl. Pigwidgeon flew three circles around the chandelier, struck a paper streamer and vanished towards the kitchen trailing black-and-gold links.

"Happy birthday," Ron said.

He pushed the parcel into Harry's hands with his eyes fixed somewhere near Harry's shoulder.

"Thanks for the card."

"Mum made me send it before the party."

"The part about Pig trying to carry the cake was more memorable."

"Right."

They stood in the sudden awkwardness of two boys who knew a great deal about each other and almost nothing together. Ron noticed the jacket.

"That's brilliant."

"Sirius gave it to me."

"Looks expensive."

Harry glanced towards Draconia, who was greeting Molly with the wary composure of a diplomat approaching disputed territory.

"Apparently it was useless until this morning."

He drew his wand from the concealed holster. Ron's eyes widened.

"That's even more brilliant."

"Draconia made it."

"Oh." Ron looked across the hall. "Don't tell her I said that."

"Why?"

"She'll know I approved of something."

"She's very discreet."

Draconia, without turning, said, "Thank you, Weasley."

Ron went red. Harry laughed and opened the orange parcel. Inside was a second-hand book entitled Keeping for the Catastrophically Outnumbered. The cover showed a sweating goalkeeper defending six hoops from an army of Quaffles. Several pages had come loose and been repaired with Spellotape. Ron had filled the margins with cramped notes and small diagrams.

"It's rubbish about professional formations," he said. "Most of them don't work unless the Chasers do exactly what they're told, and Chasers never do. I corrected those. There's a chapter on reading dives, though. That bit's good."

Harry turned to a page where Ron had drawn seven possible Chaser approaches to the left hoop. Each had an arrow predicting the Keeper's movement.

"You did all this?"

"Some of it. Bill had the book first. Then Charlie. The notes in green are Charlie's. Mine are blue. The rude ones are Fred and George."

"We prefer the term editorial," Fred said, appearing beside them.

George examined Harry's jacket. "Our influence spreads."

"You didn't buy it," Harry said.

"Our spiritual influence."

"We've always encouraged leather."

The twins wore matching dragonhide jackets despite the heat, though George's had one sleeve charred to the elbow and Fred's emitted a faint whistle whenever he bent over. They presented Harry with a square box marked PROTOTYPE: OPEN AWAY FROM EYEBROWS. Hermione intercepted it.

"Before Harry opens that, tell me what it does," Hermione said.

"It isn't dangerous," Fred said.

"The label says eyebrows."

"That is responsible labelling."

"What does it do?"

"We don't want to spoil the surprise," George said.

"You don't know."

"Knowing would spoil it for us too."

Hermione tucked the box beneath her arm.

Harry looked at the twins. "You realise she's going to open it somewhere controlled, document the result and give it back with corrections."

Fred studied him.

"He's got sharper."

"Near-death experiences," Harry said. "Terribly educational."

The humour escaped before he considered it. Ron's expression changed. Ginny, standing behind him, went still. Molly turned from the Floo. The silence lasted half a beat too long. Harry felt himself retreat behind the joke. Then George nodded gravely.

"We keep recommending them to Percy."

Fred sighed. "No commitment to self-improvement."

Air returned to the hall. Harry looked at them with sudden gratitude. The twins did not pretend the joke had not been dark. They had simply refused to leave him alone inside it.

"Maybe he needs a prototype," Harry said, nodding towards the confiscated box.

"Harry," Hermione warned.

"For education."

Fred placed a hand upon Harry's shoulder.

"Sirius, may we borrow your godson later?"

"For what?"

"Education."

Sirius looked delighted. "Return him with the same number of limbs."

"That's surprisingly restrictive."

Molly seized both twins by their ears and steered them towards the kitchen. The Lovegoods arrived through the front door wearing matching yellow flowers. Luna had woven hers into a chain around her neck. Xenophilius wore his behind one ear and carried a parcel which occasionally hummed. Luna hugged Harry lightly.

"You look less dead than the newspaper drawing," she said.

"That's what I was hoping for."

"They made your nose too narrow."

"I noticed."

She gave him a small bell strung on blue thread. It made no sound when he shook it.

"It only rings when you have forgotten something important," Luna explained.

"How does it know?"

"If I knew, I wouldn't need one."

Harry tied it around his wrist. The bell remained silent, which was more confidence than he felt he deserved. Cedric arrived with Amos and a smiling woman Harry had met only twice. Mrs Diggory brought flowers for Narcissa, wine for Sirius and no visible uncertainty about which gesture belonged to whom. Amos began shaking Harry's hand and changed his mind halfway through, pulling him into a fierce hug which crushed the air from his lungs.

"Fifteen," Amos said when he released him. His eyes were bright. "Good lad."

Cedric stood behind him, taller than nearly everyone in the hall and trying not to laugh.

"He practised a speech," Cedric said.

Amos frowned. "It was a fine speech."

"It was nine minutes long."

"A boy only turns fifteen once."

"That was minute three."

Mrs Diggory steered her husband towards Arthur before he could begin again. Cedric waited until they were alone enough to lower his voice.

"How are you?"

It was not a birthday question. Harry heard the graveyard beneath it.

"I punched a wall this morning."

Cedric looked at his knuckles. "Did it apologise?"

"Sirius says walls don't understand boundaries."

"Sounds like Sirius."

"How are you?"

Cedric glanced around the hall. No one was looking directly at them. Several people were listening with professional subtlety.

"I slept until six yesterday."

Harry knew what the number meant. Cedric had been waking before four, always at the hour when he remembered being bound and unable to move.

"That's good."

"It felt lazy."

"You can do it again tomorrow. Build a habit."

Cedric smiled. "Yes, Healer Potter."

His gift was a brass chain enchanted to tighten or loosen at a word. At one end hung a small clasp shaped like a badger; at the other, a lion.

"Binding magic," Cedric said. "The harmless kind. It can hold your glasses on when you're flying or secure something to your wrist. Mum found it in a market."

Harry touched the clasp. Neither mentioned the rope which had tied Cedric to him in the graveyard, or the binding Harry had maintained until his magic began tearing itself apart. The gift took that memory and gave it a different use.

"Thank you."

"Don't bind yourself to another person without asking."

"That sounds like experience."

"Cho was not impressed."

Harry laughed. Hagrid arrived last because he tried to avoid catching his umbrella upon the gate and stepped backwards into a hedge instead. The ward recognised him readily enough; the thorns were less accommodating. He entered through the garden with leaves caught in his beard, Fang at his heels and a wooden crate balanced upon one shoulder.

"Nothin' alive," he assured everyone.

Something inside the crate scratched.

"Little things. Harmless ones. I checked."

Narcissa closed her eyes. By noon, every lower room had filled. Portraits which had refused to speak during the first week emerged from their frames to count visitors. Children occupied the stairs. Adults migrated towards the garden with glasses in hand. The lemon tree found a place upon the terrace beside Neville's weather plant, which had turned all four leaves brilliant gold in approval of the sun.

The awning and benches had been charmed to fit the terrace, while the field beyond the rear lawn offered space for three long tables, a canvas awning and a rough Quidditch pitch marked by hoops Arthur constructed from old barrels. Black-and-gold streamers wound between the branches. The banner floated above the terrace, spelling every word correctly under Remus's supervision.

Harry stood upon the terrace long enough to watch a tray of sandwiches make its way past Arthur's elbow. There were people on every bench and several more looking for chairs. He had expected all that noise to make him want to escape indoors; instead, he found himself listening for the conversations he recognised.

Lucius and Arthur began the afternoon on opposite sides of the lawn. By half past twelve, Arthur had discovered the silver fastening upon Lucius's travelling cloak and was asking whether it responded to pressure or intention. Lucius endured three questions, answered the fourth and eventually removed the clasp so Arthur could examine the runes. Narcissa watched from beneath the awning with the expression of a woman observing two unfamiliar animals approach the same watering place.

Molly and Mrs Granger took control of the food. Dobby took offence. Winky sided with Molly, apparently for reasons which predated breakfast. Their argument filled the terrace with accusations concerning pastry thickness while trays moved from kitchen to garden under their own power.

Neville showed Hermione and Luna the weather plant. Draconia remained close enough to correct Luna's claim that it predicted emotional storms, then spent five minutes testing whether this might be true. Ginny joined them, describing the kitchen before Molly discovered the missing cake. Hermione laughed and proposed bringing the plant into the next argument about homework. Draconia said it would need a larger pot to survive that. Neville looked at his plant with increasing concern, which only made the girls laugh harder. He stood near the drawing-room doors and watched.

There had been a time when seeing Hermione and Draconia belong easily with other people made him afraid. In first year, their friendship had felt like a small, impossible country which existed only while all three guarded its borders. Loss had sharpened that instinct. After the Chamber, after Sirius, after the graveyard, Harry sometimes caught himself measuring every room for the moment one of them might choose a life beyond him. The ugliness of it embarrassed him. He did not want the girls lonely enough to need him. He wanted them safe enough to leave. He merely wanted them to come back. Draconia glanced across the lawn. Her attention found him at once. The distance between them seemed to narrow.

She said something to the others and crossed the grass.

"Why are you hiding?"

"I'm standing in a doorway."

"You have been standing in this doorway for seven minutes."

"Monitoring conditions."

"Counting the guests?"

Harry looked past her at the crowded lawn. "I didn't think this many would come."

Draconia glanced back too. Hagrid had tipped his chair backwards to let Fang crawl under the table. Ron was trying to explain the rules of the game he wanted to play while the twins each proposed a different improvement. Almost everyone had found someone to talk to.

"You did invite them," she said, but more gently than he had expected. "Come outside."

"Is that an order?"

"No. I'm asking." She looked down at his hand against the doorframe. "Hermione noticed you hadn't come out. She thought you might find it easier with someone."

"She could've come over."

"She asked me to."

Harry looked past her. Hermione stood beside Luna, deliberately examining the plant. The moment Harry caught her eye, she pointed towards the lawn with unmistakable authority.

"Subtle," he said.

"She has other gifts."

Draconia held out her hand. Harry took it. She led him into the sun.

Lunch became dangerous when Sirius attempted a speech. He rose at the centre table with a glass in one hand and struck a spoon against it until the garden quietened. Harry sat between Hermione and Draconia as predicted by the tactical placements. Cedric occupied the chair at his right, beyond Hermione. Remus sat opposite, already wearing the resigned look of a man who had seen Sirius hold a glass before.

"Fifteen years ago," Sirius began, "I was introduced to a very small person with no hair and terrible conversational skills."

"He was an infant," Remus said.

"No excuse. James had been speaking for years by then. Usually over everyone else."

Laughter moved along the tables. Harry stared down at his plate. Warmth crept beneath his collar. Sirius looked at him.

"I was asked to promise something that day."

The garden grew still in a different way.

"James called it being a godfather. Lily called it a legal safeguard against James's influence. I thought it meant teaching Harry to ride a broom, helping him escape punishment and making certain his first word was something Minerva couldn't repeat in class."

Professor McGonagall, seated beside Augusta beneath the awning, raised one eyebrow.

"James and Lily meant more," Sirius continued. "They meant that if the world changed, I was to make sure Harry knew he was wanted in it."

Harry stopped breathing. The summer sunlight pressed upon the garden. Somewhere in the hedge, an insect buzzed. His fingers had tightened around his fork hard enough to ache. Sirius's voice remained steady, but only because he forced it.

"I was late," he said. "By twelve years and several catastrophes. Harry has informed me that dramatic apologies are inefficient, so I won't offer one now."

"I said exhausting," Harry managed.

"I edited."

A few people laughed softly. Sirius raised his glass.

"To Harry. Who survived more than he should have been asked to survive, who has become kind without becoming obedient, and who has finally acquired a jacket worthy of his poor judgement. May fifteen contain fewer graveyards."

"Sirius," Molly whispered fiercely.

Harry laughed because the alternative had risen too close to tears. Around him, glasses lifted.

"To Harry."

Voices repeated his name. For one terrible second, the sound became another circle. Figures surrounded him beneath a black sky. Masks shone. Voldemort paced beyond the headstone and Death Eaters laughed while Harry knelt in blood. The garden tipped. His fork struck the plate. Hermione's hand found his beneath the table. Draconia pressed two fingers against the pulse in his wrist, directly above the fastening she had made. Neither looked at him. That saved him from everyone else doing it. Harry felt the grass beneath his shoes. Sun against his face. Leather around his shoulders. Hawthorn. Nineteen ninety-five.

Hermione upon one side. Draconia upon the other. Sirius standing with a glass raised and fear concealed behind a smile because he had seen Harry disappear for half a heartbeat. Harry lifted his own glass.

"To fewer graveyards," he said.

The toast ended in relieved laughter. Conversation returned carefully at first, then with increasing force. Arthur asked Xenophilius whether Wrackspurts could survive electricity. Molly told him not to encourage either subject. Hagrid attempted to cut the cake with his belt knife and was driven from the table by Winky. Hermione did not release Harry's hand.

"You all right?" Cedric murmured.

Harry looked at him across Hermione. Cedric's face told him he had recognised the same transformation because he had made it himself.

"Yes," Harry said.

It was not entirely true. It was not entirely false either. Draconia's fingers remained over his pulse until it slowed.

The Quidditch match began because Fred insulted Cedric's flying. Molly warned that she would summon them all to the ground by their ankles if a single ball came near the food. Between those events, the rear field became the most badly regulated pitch in Britain.

Arthur enlarged six apple barrels into hoops and floated them at opposite ends. Sirius found three school Quaffles in a cupboard upstairs and chose the one least distorted by age. Ginny produced two Beaters' bats from somewhere inside her robes. Fred had brought a practice Bludger. George insisted he had not. The ball escaped its case before anyone could determine which statement was worse, striking a deck chair hard enough to collapse it.

"No Bludgers," Hermione said.

"Singular," George corrected as the iron ball smashed through a deck chair.

"No singular Bludger."

"That hardly sounds like Quidditch."

"It sounds like Harry's birthday ending without surgery."

Madam Pomfrey had not been invited, a fact Sirius was beginning to regard as a tactical error. Hagrid caught the Bludger while Arthur opened its own reinforced case. They secured it inside and set a second locking charm over the lid, well away from Hagrid's crate and the food.

Teams formed through a process involving accusation, bribery and the twins declaring themselves captains before anyone else understood there would be captains. Fred selected Ginny, Cedric and Ron. George chose Harry, Draconia and Sirius.

"You've picked an adult," Ron said.

"Sirius counts as half," George replied.

"I'm offended," Sirius said.

"Your last school match was before we could walk."

"Now I'm insulted."

Remus declined to play on medical grounds, historical grounds and the fact that Sirius had once flown him into a church tower. He joined Hermione and Luna beside the pitch. Neville agreed to keep score until he discovered both twins expected him to use different rules.

Harry removed the leather jacket reluctantly. Draconia took it before he could lay it on the grass and folded it over the back of a chair, careful not to bend the holster.

"Don't leave that where someone can sit on the holster," she said. "I spent far too long getting it to release properly."

"I'm not wearing it."

"Then perhaps try keeping both hands on your broom."

"Possessive," Hermione remarked.

Draconia looked at her.

"Of the craftsmanship."

"Of course."

Harry pretended to adjust his broom so neither saw him smile. He had not flown properly since the third task. The realisation came only when he mounted the Firebolt. He had ridden during short training sessions in spring and crossed the maze upon it in nightmares, but this was different: open sky, no task, no crowd demanding victory. The broom responded to his grip with a familiar tremor. For an instant, memory returned not as terror but as speed. First year. Wind tearing tears from his eyes. The Snitch closing beneath his fingers. Home had once been a castle. Freedom had once been flight. Harry kicked away from the ground.

The ground fell away beneath the Firebolt, and Harry felt the familiar change in his stomach as the broom answered his hands. He rose slowly enough to see Hermione move her chair back from the pitch, then turned towards the open field.

Air struck his face, clean and warm. The field opened beneath him in green bands. Hawthorn's roof appeared beyond the trees, dark slate cut by chimneys, every window bright in the afternoon. People shrank to colour around the tables. Harry climbed until sound thinned.

A laugh escaped him before he could think about how it sounded. He had missed this: the broom moving exactly where he asked, the warm air and the field stretching wide enough for him to choose a direction without checking a doorway first. The Firebolt rolled beneath him. He followed, turning upside down above the pitch before pulling into a dive so steep the ground rushed at his glasses.

"Harry!" Hermione shouted.

He levelled out a foot above the grass. The movement dragged exhilaration through every part of him. It was not the blind, brittle feeling which came when he ran towards danger. It was control at speed, risk answering the smallest shift of his hands. He rose again. Draconia matched him upon a sleek black broom.

"Show-off."

Wind pulled strands of her hair free.

"You followed."

"To assess the quality of the mistake."

"And?"

"I've seen you do better. You'll have to work harder if you want applause."

She dropped beneath him without warning, called for the Quaffle from George and accelerated towards the western hoops as he passed it to her. Harry chased. The match possessed no Snitch, no referee and very little connection to ordinary Quidditch. Each side had one Keeper and three Chasers, at least until Sirius decided positions were less important than interesting conversations. The bats had been set aside with the locked Bludger. Sirius ignored his assigned position and flew wherever the conversation appeared most interesting. Fred called fouls invented seconds earlier. George rejected every one upon constitutional grounds. Ron guarded the western hoops. Harry expected him to be competent. He did not expect Ron to read the pitch.

From the centre line, Ron shouted a warning before Ginny had begun moving. She turned at once, intercepted a pass intended for Sirius and sent the Quaffle beneath Draconia's broom. Later, when George approached from the left, Ron left the central hoop exposed.

"That's open!" Fred yelled.

George saw it too. He changed direction, raised his arm and threw. Ron was already moving. He caught the Quaffle against his chest at the exact point George expected to score.

"You left it open on purpose," Harry said as he passed.

Ron grinned. "He always takes the easy shot."

"I heard that!" George called.

"That's why I said it!"

Harry laughed and banked away. The next time he approached Ron's hoops, he did not take the easy shot. He climbed high, Quaffle tucked beneath one arm, and watched Ron's shoulders instead of the targets. Ron watched Harry's broom. Neither moved first. Below, Ginny moved to cut off a pass to George. Cedric swept across the centre hoop to block Harry's approach. Harry feinted right. Ron shifted left. Harry rolled, dropped the Quaffle from one hand to the other and dived beneath Cedric. For three glorious seconds, there was only the hoop, the wind and Ron's surprised face. Harry threw low. Ron pushed hard against his broom's footrest and lunged sideways, keeping one hand upon the handle.

His fingertips struck the Quaffle and changed its path by an inch. It clipped the inner rim, spun back and fell. Ginny caught it before it reached the grass.

"Save!" Ron yelled.

"Technically the hoop saved it," Harry called.

"Keeper's hoop. Counts as part of me."

"That explains the personality."

Ron made an outraged noise. Harry accelerated away, grinning. At the pitch boundary, Hermione was trying not to smile. Draconia swept past Harry on her way back towards the centre, grinning openly when he blamed the hoop. The game grew faster.

Cedric and Harry began competing without discussion, each testing how close he could pass the other at speed. Ginny flew with the fierce economy of someone accustomed to being underestimated. Draconia intercepted three passes, twice plucking the Quaffle from Cedric's reach, and accepted each accusation of cheating as evidence of success. When she broke into another counterattack, Harry climbed behind her, ready for a pass. She glanced over her shoulder.

"You are predictable."

"You keep looking back."

"To see how far behind you are."

"And?"

"Disappointingly close."

She smiled into the wind. Harry forgot the Quaffle. It dropped from her left hand. George caught it below and shouted something congratulatory that Harry could not hear over the rush in his ears. Draconia flew nearer until their broom handles almost touched.

"Predictable," she repeated.

Then she turned sharply and left him staring after her. From the ground, Hermione cupped her hands around her mouth.

"Harry! Behind you!"

Cedric's pass struck Harry between the shoulder blades. The impact drove him forward. His fingers lost the handle. For a fraction of a second, nothing supported him. The ground waited thirty feet below. Harry caught the broom with one hand.

Shouts rose from the field. The Firebolt swung beneath his weight, the handle wrenching his shoulder as his feet kicked at open air. His vision narrowed. Leather beneath his palm. Wind. Sun. Not falling. Not yet. He could have called for help. Instead, Harry laughed. The sound was breathless and wrong even to him.

He used the broom's momentum to swing his legs upwards, caught the handle between both knees and hauled himself onto it as the Firebolt continued moving. By the time he sat upright, his arm burned and everyone on the field had stopped.

"Still here," he called.

Hermione did not appear reassured. Draconia flew towards him, face bloodless with fury.

"Harry, come down. You're holding that shoulder oddly."

"I'm fine."

"That phrase has been formally prohibited."

"You can't prohibit a phrase."

"I'm asking you to land, not win an argument."

She brought her broom level with his, leaving enough distance that neither handle could catch the other. Harry pulled away on instinct. The movement opened the distance between them. Something broke across Draconia's expression before anger covered it.

"Don't pull away like that," she said. "Please. You nearly fell."

The single word ended the game more effectively than any whistle. Harry hovered. His heartbeat had not begun to slow. Exhilaration still burned through him, but beneath it lay another impulse, colder and harder to admit: for the moment he had hung above the earth, the choice had been simple. Hold on or fall. No newspapers. No testimony. No memory asking him to decide whether surviving had been worth its cost. He looked towards the ground.

Hermione stood beneath them with her arms folded tightly. Sirius had moved to her side. Remus watched Harry with the same grave attention he had given him in the morning room. Ron remained before the hoops, Quaffle forgotten under one arm. No one looked impressed. Harry landed.

Draconia touched down so close behind him that her broom struck the grass. She set it down carelessly and stopped in front of him, her hands closing briefly at her sides before she trusted herself to touch his sleeve.

"What was that?"

"Cedric has terrible aim."

"I mean what you did afterwards. You were hanging by one hand, and you laughed as though nothing had happened."

"I got back on."

"You laughed."

"It was funny."

"No." Her voice dropped. "It wasn't."

Everyone else remained several paces away. Hermione came closer, watching the way Harry kept his shoulder still before she asked to examine it. She came to Harry's other side and took his injured arm with careful hands.

"Can you move your shoulder?"

He demonstrated. Pain cut down to his elbow.

Hermione's eyes narrowed. "You strained it."

"I didn't fall."

"That is not the highest possible standard."

"It worked."

"So did jumping in front of a dragon," Draconia said. "Getting away with it doesn't mean it was safe. You frightened me, Harry. I don't think you noticed."

Harry looked between them. Heat, embarrassment and the remains of flight crowded beneath his skin. Part of him wanted to make another joke until everyone stopped treating the moment as evidence of damage. Another part saw the colour still absent from Draconia's face and the faint shake in Hermione's hand where it supported his arm.

"I knew I could catch it," he said.

Remus approached.

"Did you?"

The question was quiet. Harry opened his mouth. He remembered the split second after his fingers slipped. The clean emptiness. The lack of fear.

"Mostly."

Sirius looked away across the field. That hurt more than anger would have. Harry exhaled.

"All right," he said. "It was stupid."

"Reckless," Hermione corrected.

"Deeply," Draconia added.

"I've apologised."

"You have not," both girls said.

Harry considered.

"I acknowledged vocabulary."

Ron snorted. The sound surprised everyone, including Ron. He covered it with a cough. Harry looked at him.

"Keeper's hoop?" Harry asked.

Ron shifted the Quaffle beneath his arm. "Counts as part of me."

The absurdity loosened the circle.

Harry turned back to Hermione and Draconia. "I'm sorry I frightened you."

Draconia released his shirt.

"You are fortunate it's your birthday party."

"Why?"

"Consequences begin tomorrow."

Hermione still held his arm. "We're putting ice on this."

"Magical household."

"Fine. Charmed ice."

"Excessive luxury."

"Be quiet, Harry."

He allowed them to guide him towards the tables. Behind him, Fred picked up the Quaffle.

"Does this mean we won?"

"No," Neville called from the score board. "None of your rules make sense."

"A draw, then."

"You lost by forty points."

"A very narrow draw."

Hermione's charmed ice was not ice at all. It was a blue linen compress which crawled independently around Harry's shoulder, locating tender muscles and becoming unpleasantly cold upon contact. Hermione claimed this was more effective. Harry suspected she appreciated giving the cloth permission to hunt him.

He sat beneath the awning while the abandoned match dissolved into smaller games. Ginny, Ron and the twins began throwing the Quaffle through the lower hoops from the ground. Neville kept score now that scoring involved numbers he recognised. Cedric remained upon his broom, teaching Luna how to turn without closing her eyes. Draconia occupied the chair beside Harry with his jacket across her lap.

"You can put that down," he said.

"Someone spilt pumpkin juice upon the grass."

"It can survive dragons."

"The holster can. Sirius chose the jacket."

"I heard that," Sirius called from the cake table.

"Then perhaps you'll remember it before buying the next one."

Hermione tightened the compress with a flick of her wand.

Harry flinched. "You're enjoying this."

"Only the complaining. I'd rather you didn't need the compress at all."

"No compassion anywhere."

"We used it all while you were dangling from a broom."

Draconia inspected the jacket's inner seam. "The fastening held."

"Your affection for the fastening is moving."

Her eyes rose to his. "If I begin discussing everything else which moved, your party will become awkward."

Harry's reply disappeared. Draconia returned to the seam. Hermione looked from her to Harry, then returned to adjusting the compress. It caught against a tender patch, and he flinched.

"Sorry," she said at once, loosening it. "I didn't mean to pull that side. Is this better?"

Harry nodded, and she checked the cloth again before letting it go. Professor McGonagall rescued him by approaching from the lawn. She had removed her pointed hat, though her hair remained secured in the same uncompromising knot. A plate containing one untouched piece of cake rested in her hand.

"Mr Potter."

"Professor."

"I had intended to wish you a happy birthday. I see you have celebrated by nearly separating your shoulder."

"It remains attached."

"A standard you share with several insects."

Harry looked at Hermione. "She's been speaking to you."

"Professor McGonagall doesn't require help."

"Quite." McGonagall lowered her voice. "There is another matter."

Draconia's attention sharpened. Harry sat straighter despite the compress.

"The Minister's review?" Hermione asked.

McGonagall glanced towards the tables. The nearest adults had drifted into separate conversations, but Lucius stood with his back to them in a position which allowed him to hear every word.

"A preliminary notice reached Professor Dumbledore this morning," McGonagall said. "The Ministry intends to appoint a representative to observe certain lessons and review school procedures. The appointment has not been formally announced."

"Dolores Umbridge," Harry said.

McGonagall's mouth thinned. That was answer enough.

Hermione removed the compress from Harry's shoulder and forgot to replace it. "What does observe mean?" Hermione asked. "Watching a lesson is one thing. Telling the teacher what they're allowed to teach is quite another."

"The Ministry oversees educational standards," Draconia said. "Under statutes no one has examined in decades because every Minister preferred to leave Dumbledore responsible for the consequences."

"My guardian says old laws are where cowards hide when new ones would need defending," Harry said.

Everyone looked at him. The word had escaped naturally.

My guardian.

Not his godfather spoken of as a promise made long ago. Not an escaped prisoner living inside whatever time the Ministry allowed them. His guardian: the man responsible for him now, cutting cake with Hagrid's belt knife because Winky had stopped watching. McGonagall followed his gaze. Something softened behind her spectacles.

"Your guardian is correct," she said. "The Ministry's authority is not unlimited. Neither, however, is Professor Dumbledore's ability to refuse it without jeopardising the school."

"She wants us silent," Harry said.

"Almost certainly."

"Then this isn't an educational review."

"No."

The honesty settled coldly despite the afternoon heat. At the hearing, Umbridge had not needed to win. She had only needed to learn how Harry answered, where his friends sat and which truths made him lose control of his voice. Now she was coming to the one place he could not avoid.

Hogwarts had once been escape. Then it became home. The idea of the Ministry installing itself inside the classrooms felt like watching a hand reach across a threshold. Draconia laid the jacket over Harry's shoulders. The new leather closed around him.

"When is the appointment announced?" she asked.

"Likely before the end of August. You did not hear it from me."

"Obviously," Hermione said. "We heard it from a source close to the school."

McGonagall gave her a severe look which failed to conceal approval.

"You will do nothing illegal."

Harry considered the wording.

"How illegal?"

"Mr Potter."

"There are categories."

"There are not."

From the table, Fred called, "There absolutely are!"

McGonagall closed her eyes.

Draconia stood. "We have no reason to act before we know what authority Umbridge receives."

"That's sensible," Harry said.

"It is."

"I don't like it."

"I thought you might not. That's why I'd rather you didn't answer her alone."

Hermione nodded. "We gather information first. If she intends to restrict what students are taught, we document it."

"And then?" Harry asked.

Hermione looked towards the house, where copies of the transcript still waited in ordered stacks.

"We let other people see what she's doing. Parents, teachers, students who aren't in our classes. She shouldn't be able to give everyone a different story."

McGonagall's gaze passed over all three of them.

"I frequently wonder," she said, "whether sorting you into separate Houses accomplished anything at all."

"It improved our reach," Draconia replied.

McGonagall departed before her expression could be called a smile. The warning remained. It did not spoil the day. Harry had spent too long allowing danger to claim every hour surrounding it. Umbridge would come in September. This was August. Cake still existed. Ron had begun arguing that the final Quidditch score should be recalculated because Neville had failed to count emotional victories. Harry stood.

Hermione caught the compress as it fell. "Where are you going?"

"To establish whether emotional victories are in the rules."

"They aren't."

"Ron seems passionate."

Draconia rose with him. "You're not getting back on that broom today. You can tell Ron exactly what you think from a chair."

"I can argue from the ground."

"Your natural position."

Harry offered both girls an arm.

Hermione stared at it. "Your left shoulder is injured."

"I'll keep it still. You can let go if I start being irritating."

Her mouth opened. Draconia took his right arm before Hermione could answer.

"An unusually transparent strategy," she said.

"Did it work?"

"Temporarily."

Hermione shook her head and accepted his other arm with exquisite care. Together, they crossed the lawn. Harry became aware of Sirius watching from the cake table. His godfather looked between the two girls, then at Harry, and raised both eyebrows. Harry nearly walked into a chair.

The party lasted until evening softened the edges of the garden. People left in groups. Augusta Longbottom took three slices of cake wrapped in cloth and denied this to anyone who asked. Xenophilius departed wearing Arthur's electrical plug as a pendant. Hagrid's crate opened while he was saying goodbye, releasing six furry creatures with wings too small for their bodies. Five were recovered. Sirius maintained that one had chosen freedom and therefore belonged to the grounds. The Weasleys remained longest.

Molly packed food into Hawthorn's cupboards despite Dobby's protests. Arthur recovered the electric whisk but forgot the plug, which was already on its way to Ottery St Catchpole around Xenophilius's neck. Fred and George gave Harry a whispered address in Diagon Alley and told him to visit when he was ready to participate in serious academic work.

"He is not testing anything upon himself," Hermione said from behind them.

"You move silently," George told her.

"You were whispering quite loudly. I could hear eyebrows from halfway across the hall."

"See?" Fred said to Harry. "Natural talent."

Ginny hugged him goodbye. Ron waited beside the cold fireplace, turning one fist inside the other.

"The book's really second-hand," he said when Harry approached. "About fourth-hand, probably."

"That makes the notes better."

"Some of mine are wrong."

"I'll tell you which ones."

Ron looked up.

Harry smiled. "Next match."

It was not an invitation to become someone they had failed to become at eleven. Neither boy needed to pretend the missed years did not exist. It was smaller than that, and perhaps more possible.

"You'll have to get past me first," Ron said.

"I did."

"Hoop saved it."

"Part of you, apparently."

Ron grinned. "Exactly."

Ron tried to retrieve Pigwidgeon from the chandelier. The owl escaped his hands twice before Hermione promised to send him home once he had stopped attacking the streamers. Ron thanked her, stepped into the Floo after his family and vanished in a burst of green.

The Grangers left through the front door. Mrs Granger hugged Hermione for a long time and made her promise to come home before school began. Mr Granger shook Harry's hand again, then abandoned formality and pulled him briefly into an embrace.

"Good house," he said, looking back at Hawthorn. "Look after it."

Harry thought of the lemon tree inside the drawing-room window.

"I will."

Cedric and his parents were last. Amos had found Sirius's elderflower wine and recovered the birthday speech he had been prevented from delivering. Mrs Diggory Apparated him home during minute seven. Cedric remained upon the drive.

"You had a good day," he said.

Harry glanced back through the open doorway. Streamers sagged in the hall. Dirty glasses occupied every flat surface. Hermione and Draconia stood together beneath the chandelier, attempting to untangle Pigwidgeon's stolen chain while the owl attacked their fingers.

"Yes."

"You're allowed."

Harry's gaze returned to Cedric.

"I know."

Cedric waited.

Harry sighed. "I'm learning."

"So am I."

They embraced quickly, shoulder to shoulder.

"Happy birthday, Harry."

"Thanks. Get some sleep."

"Bossy."

"I finally got the party. Authority arrived with it."

Cedric laughed and Disapparated beyond the gate with a soft crack. Quiet did not return when the guests left. Hawthorn retained them in overturned cushions, abandoned wrapping paper and the warmth of rooms which had held too many bodies. The old portraits discussed everyone at once. Somewhere upstairs, the escaped winged creature knocked over a lamp. Harry closed the front door. He felt suddenly exhausted.

Even after the last goodbye, Harry could feel the noise of the afternoon lingering in his head. He wanted to keep every part of it and could scarcely bear another person asking whether he had enjoyed himself. Every gift, touch and voice remained inside him, good enough to become painful because accepting any of it meant admitting how much had been absent before. He pressed his forehead against the cool wood.

"Too much?" Remus asked.

Harry turned. Remus stood at the foot of the stairs carrying a narrow parcel under one arm.

"A bit."

"We can sit somewhere quiet."

Harry looked towards the hall. Hermione and Draconia had disappeared into the kitchen with Pigwidgeon. Sirius was helping Molly's dishes wash themselves and losing. Lucius had retired to his study after discovering icing upon his sleeve. Narcissa sat upon the terrace with her shoes removed.

"The morning room?"

Remus nodded. Evening had changed it. The windows now stood open upon a violet sky. Moths turned around the garden lamps. Remus's stone, rosemary and red marble remained upon the table where he had left them on Friday; Sirius had eaten the remaining chocolate. Harry returned to the chair he had used for their lesson.

"Another present?"

"A small one."

Remus handed him the parcel. Inside was a book bound in brown cloth. Unlike Hermione's beautiful journal, it looked homemade. The pages had been stitched together unevenly, and the title upon the cover had been written by hand.

Practical Defence: First Principles, Bad Situations and Other People's Mistakes.

Harry opened it. Remus's narrow handwriting filled the first page.

For Harry. A first edition, and therefore full of errors. I expect you to identify them before Sirius does.

"You wrote this?"

"I began it after your third-year Defence exam. It was meant to be a collection of lessons I would have taught if the year had ended differently."

Harry turned the pages. The book contained practical spells, diagrams, questions and warnings written without textbook politeness. One chapter was entitled When Running Is the Intelligent Form of Bravery. Another began, If an adult tells you a duel will be honourable, assume he is either lying or incompetent. Notes crowded the margins in several inks.

"Sirius wrote in it."

"He found the draft."

Beside a paragraph about concealment, Sirius had added: Moony is correct but refuses to admit that curtains are excellent cover. Remus had written beneath it: Curtains burn. Sirius: Only the first time. Farther down, a different line read: James attempted this charm in fifth year and removed every button from his own clothes. Harry laughed.

"I thought you might want the stories," Remus said. "Including the stupid ones."

Harry rested a hand upon the page.

"It's not finished."

"No. I thought we could add to it as we go. There are lessons I'd rather test with you than pretend I got right the first time."

The mentorship in the words was not announced. Remus did not call Harry a son or James's legacy or the last piece of anything. He offered time, knowledge and future pages. Harry closed the book around one finger.

"You really were my favourite professor."

"Former."

"Still a technicality."

They sat while the last light left the windows. Remus told him the true story of James and the trumpet. It involved a stolen instrument, a Silencing Charm placed upon the wrong end and Professor Flitwick hearing the resulting note from three floors away. Sirius entered halfway through to correct every detail in his own favour. By the conclusion, neither account remained credible, but Harry no longer cared. These were not memorials. They were memories alive enough to be argued over.

Near midnight, Harry found Hermione and Draconia upon the roof. Access was through the attic window onto a broad, shallow section of roof above the rear terrace. Sirius had fitted a handrail and a low protective barrier along its edge after discovering Harry liked sitting there; with his shoulder still sore, Harry used the rail and took the step out slowly. Sirius had shown him the route two nights earlier and sworn him to secrecy. Hermione had discovered it within a day. Draconia claimed the location had been obvious.

They sat side by side beneath the chimney with a blanket around their shoulders. The remains of the birthday banner floated crookedly between the beeches below. Garden lamps had gone dark one by one, leaving the lawn blue beneath the moon. Hermione shifted to make space. Harry sat between them.

"You stole my hiding place," he said.

"You showed me the window," Draconia replied.

"I showed you the attic."

"And left it open."

Hermione pulled the blanket across Harry's knees. "How is your shoulder?"

"Better than earlier. It still aches if I lift it too high."

"Then sit on this side," Hermione said, moving the blanket. "You won't have to reach across us."

"A useful answer at last," Draconia added. "We were preparing another argument about insects."

Harry looked from one to the other.

"McGonagall's ruining my material."

For a while, they watched thin clouds move across the stars. Hawthorn stretched below them, rooflines and dark chimneys enclosing all the lit rooms Harry now knew: Sirius's bedroom facing east; the library where Hermione worked past midnight; the guest room Draconia had quietly begun calling hers; the kitchen in which Dobby left food warming whenever Harry missed dinner.

"Was it good?" Hermione asked.

Harry knew she meant the whole day.

"Yes."

"That sounded painful," Draconia said.

"It was."

Neither demanded an easier answer. Harry looked at his hands. The small silent bell Luna had given him circled one wrist. Cedric's chain held his glasses securely at the back of his neck. The brass key rested against his chest beneath the jacket. He had spent years learning to recognise danger from the objects people carried. It felt strange to inventory kindness instead.

"I kept waiting for something to happen," he admitted.

Hermione leaned against his left shoulder, careful of the strain. "Something did. You had a birthday party."

"Very dangerous age," Draconia said.

"Sirius says I can form opinions now."

"Heaven help us."

Harry smiled.

"When everyone raised their glasses," he said, "I thought I was back there."

Hermione's fingers found his beneath the blanket. Draconia did not reach for his other hand. She rested her head against his shoulder instead, the one which did not hurt, and allowed the weight of it to remain.

"I noticed," she said.

Harry looked down at her pale hair beneath the moonlight.

"Was it that obvious?"

"You stopped looking at us. Just for a second. I remembered the hospital wing."

"I thought I was hiding it."

"You're dreadful at hiding things from us," Hermione said. "Especially when Draconia walks into a room wearing—"

Draconia lifted her head. "Hermione, you promised you wouldn't bring that up."

Hermione smiled sweetly. "Green."

Harry was grateful for the darkness.

"I was looking at the dress," Harry said.

"Of course you were," Draconia replied.

"Every time she turned round," Hermione added.

"You are inventing evidence."

"This is not evidence," Harry reminded her.

He drew Hermione's journal from inside the jacket.

"You brought it onto the roof?" she asked.

"Apparently I keep books now. Terrible influence."

He opened to the first empty page. Moonlight was not enough to write by, so Draconia lit her wand and held it above his shoulder. Harry dipped a quill into the small self-sealing bottle Hermione had fastened inside the cover. He hesitated.

The day offered too much. Sirius at dawn with flour upon his sleeve. The new jacket. Draconia's fingers against his wrist. Hermione's handwriting preserving laughter he had forgotten. Ron guarding an open hoop. Cedric sleeping until six. Remus promising unfinished pages. A garden filled with people who had come because Harry existed, not because he had survived publicly enough to become interesting. He wrote:

Fifteen. Sirius made a cake. It was terrible. I ate two pieces.

Hermione read over his arm.

"That is not the most important thing that happened."

"You said it wasn't evidence."

"It isn't."

"Then I decide."

Draconia's light shifted across the page.

"From your description, the cake deserves its own warning," she said. "You might add the paw print."

Harry added:

Draconia agreed with me about something.

She took the quill from his hand.

Under duress, she wrote.

Hermione reclaimed it.

He nearly fell from a broom and apologised afterwards.

Harry stared. "This has become evidence."

"Historical balance," Hermione said.

He reached for the quill. She held it away. Draconia raised the wand higher, abandoning useful light in favour of obstruction. Their whispered struggle disturbed a loose tile, which slid six inches and stopped against the gutter with a crack. All three froze. Below them, a window opened.

"If you're moving about up there," Sirius called into the darkness, "use the steps, not the gutter. I didn't put that rail there for decoration."

"Nobody's falling!" Harry called. "And you should be in bed."

"Excellent advice!"

The window closed. Hermione began laughing first. Draconia tried to look offended on behalf of the roof, then gave up and leaned against Harry's uninjured shoulder. He recovered the quill before either girl could add another account of his flying and drew a small, hopelessly crooked cake beneath the first entry.

"That looks like a cauldron," Draconia said.

"Then we ought to draw steam," Hermione said. "It might help."

Harry handed her the book. "Go on, then. You draw it."

She bent over the page while Draconia held the light steady. Below them, Sirius shut his window, and the three stayed where they were, arguing softly about whether a paw print belonged on the icing.
