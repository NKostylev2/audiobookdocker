Order Of The Pheonix

The warehouse had been quiet for twenty minutes, long enough for Kingsley Shacklebolt to hear the river slapping against the pilings beyond the yard. Light showed beneath the office blind. Somewhere inside, a chair scraped across the floor, then fell silent as he climbed the shallow entrance step. He had scarcely raised his hand to the door when the first curse struck it from within.

He dropped behind the brick pier, dragging Tonks down by the shoulder as green light tore through the wood. The bolt on the other side glowed white. For an instant he could see the whole iron frame through the planks; then the door burst outwards, flinging splinters across the yard and driving a rusted hinge into the wall above his head. Somewhere beyond the river, a car alarm began to wail. Tonks landed hard against his knee.

"They've decided against opening it, then."

"Stay down."

Another curse crossed the doorway. It struck the cobbles where her hand had been, leaving a patch of stone that bubbled like boiling milk. Kingsley raised his wand without leaning into the opening. His shield filled the space between the piers, clear enough to show the warehouse beyond it. A man in shirtsleeves stood halfway down an aisle of crates with his wand extended. Behind him, a hanging lamp swung wildly over a workbench. Nothing else moved.

The stillness troubled Kingsley more than the raised wand. A man expecting Aurors ought to have been looking for cover, an exit or someone to stand beside him; this one seemed content to wait where they could see him.

Six people had entered the building since dusk. He had counted them from a disused office across the road while Tonks watched the rear entrance and the other two Aurors inspected the wards. None had left. Now one man stood in the middle of an otherwise empty floor, holding a position which offered him no cover at all.

"Left-hand loft," Kingsley said.

Tonks turned her wand towards the upper windows. "I've got it."

He sent a red spell through the doorway. The man vanished before it reached him. The lamp stopped swinging as abruptly as the man had vanished. Kingsley had time to register the change before a latch clicked above his left shoulder.

"Decoy," he warned.

The loft window opened, its lower edge catching the light from his shield. Tonks fired first. Her Stunning Spell passed through the narrow gap and struck something with a sound like a plate breaking. A figure fell against the glass. Kingsley heard feet scrambling across boards above them, then a shout from the back of the warehouse.

The answering shout came from one of their colleagues. The rear team had made it inside, and waiting in the yard would leave them facing the occupants alone. Kingsley stepped over the splintered door, keeping close to the brickwork until the nearest shelf gave him cover.

Rain had begun less than an hour earlier. It dripped from his coat onto a floor stained by years of oil and river mud. The building smelled of damp timber, old rope and something bitter enough to catch at the back of his throat. Tall shelves divided the ground floor into narrow passages. Most held ordinary freight: folded canvas, lamp fittings, tins of paint with manufacturers' names printed upon their sides. A wooden sign nailed above the office advertised storage by the week.

Kingsley had passed a dozen buildings like it along the river. Nothing about the paint tins or the weekly storage sign invited a second glance, and the ordinary freight would have given anyone inspecting a delivery a perfectly reasonable place to stop looking. Kingsley kept his shield raised.

"Department of Magical Law Enforcement," he called. "Lower your wands."

Something scraped along the ceiling.

"That sounded promising," Tonks murmured behind him.

"Watch the stairs."

He reached the first row of crates as a voice shouted from the loft.

"Burn it!"

Flame ran along the warehouse floor, passing beneath the shelving before Kingsley could get a clear view of where it had begun. Orange light climbed the underside of the crates and threw a shifting lattice of shadows onto the rafters.

It did not catch the timber or the spilled oil. It followed a line of copper nails driven between the boards, turning at each corner until the whole building had been divided into bright squares. A thin, shrill noise rose from the nearest crate. Its lid shuddered beneath the iron straps. Kingsley stepped back.

"Tonks. The crates."

She had seen it. Her wand swept downwards, smothering the nearest line beneath a layer of frost. Steam burst against their legs. Three squares went dark, but the others burned more fiercely, and somewhere on the far side of the shelves wood began to crack. A man ran across the aisle ahead.

Kingsley caught him at the turn with a Stunning Spell. The man struck a stack of folded canvas and slid out of sight. Before Kingsley could reach him, a second figure appeared above the office, wand pointed towards the floor. The curse hit the shield with enough force to numb Kingsley's arm.

He staggered into the shelving. Paint tins fell around his feet. Tonks fired over his shoulder; the figure ducked, and the office wall exploded instead, scattering glass across the workbench below.

"Back door's sealed!" one of the Aurors called. "They're trying the roof!"

Kingsley looked up. The warehouse's pitched ceiling was a mess of rafters, pulleys and hanging ropes. Rain showed through a broken pane near the ridge. A woman in dark robes clung to a ladder beneath it, trying to push a small leather case through the opening ahead of her. He aimed at the ladder.

It folded against the wall. The woman fell six feet, caught a beam with one hand and swung above the office. The case dropped onto the workbench. She let go before his next spell reached her, landed badly, and disappeared behind the shattered wall.

A sharp crack followed from behind the office wall, then a second before Kingsley could reach the gap. The sound carried oddly in the enclosed space, leaving no footsteps to tell him which direction the fugitives had taken.

"Apparition wards?" Tonks demanded.

"Still holding at the yard," the Auror answered. "They've got an internal route."

Kingsley swore under his breath. The survey had found concealment, fire alarms and a ward intended to discourage Muggles from noticing deliveries after midnight. It had found no transport spell. Someone had gone to considerable trouble to make the exits appear ordinary too. The copper lines flared beneath his boots.

"Office," he told Tonks. "There's a case on the bench. Don't open it."

She moved at once, keeping low behind the shelves. Kingsley passed the fallen man and turned towards the aisle where the fire had spread. The unconscious suspect lay with his arm twisted beneath him. His wand had rolled into a puddle. Kingsley kicked it clear, bound the man's wrists and placed a containment charm around him before going on. The next crate burst.

There was no blast. Its boards simply fell apart, and the contents collapsed into grey powder. Paper labels disintegrated with them. One iron strap softened, curled upon itself and dropped through the floorboards.

They were destroying the evidence along with its containers. There would be no contents to identify, no labels to trace and, if the spell finished its work, very little to prove that these crates had ever held more than ordinary freight.

Kingsley sent a freezing charm across the copper nails. The line broke beneath the crate ahead of him. Fire continued on the other side, finding another route through the grid. Whoever had laid it had expected someone to try that. He could preserve the warehouse or stop the people leaving it. For a second he stood between those choices, listening to footsteps overhead and the steady collapse of wood. Then he heard someone coughing. The sound came from behind a door at the end of the aisle. A heavy padlock hung through its hasp. Smoke seeped beneath it.

Kingsley blasted the lock away and caught the handle before the broken metal reached the floor. The door would not move. He pulled once more, feeling it hold against him while another cough came from the far side.

He touched his wand to the hinge, felt the answering resistance of an enchantment and cut through the fastening instead. The whole frame came loose. He pulled it towards him, braced it against the shelves and found a cupboard scarcely wide enough to hold a person sitting down.

A young woman crouched inside with her knees against her chest, wedged between the wall and a shelf of cleaning rags. She had pushed herself as far from the smoke as the cupboard allowed, but there was no window and scarcely enough room to turn her head.

Her blouse was grey with dust. One sleeve had torn at the elbow. Both hands were pressed over her mouth, though the coughing continued behind them. She looked at Kingsley's wand and scrambled farther into a space that offered nowhere to go.

"I'm an Auror," he said. "My name is Kingsley. Can you stand?"

She shook her head. Flame reached the skirting board beside him.

"All right. I'm going to lift you."

He waited only long enough for her to see his hand, then crouched, put an arm behind her back and brought her clear of the cupboard. She weighed less than he expected. Her fingers fastened in his coat. He could feel the effort she made not to struggle.

"Tonks!"

"Coming."

She appeared between the shelves carrying the leather case inside a transparent bubble. One cheek was bleeding. Behind her, a doorframe glowed with the sickly blue outline of an abandoned transport charm.

"Two out," she said. "Route's dead. Vane's sealing the office."

"Get her outside."

Tonks looked at the woman, then put the case down where Kingsley could take it.

"You're coming with me," she said gently. "Mind the tins. This place is a disgrace."

The woman made no answer. Tonks supported her beneath both arms and guided her towards the broken entrance. Kingsley watched until they reached the doorway. Then he turned back to the burning grid.

He had seen a version of this room during the first war. Even with Tonks leading the woman towards daylight, he could not shake the recognition: the hurried escape, the expendable witness and the ordinary business frontage left behind for the investigators.

Different river. Different boxes. The same hastily abandoned meal on a workbench, the same carefully ordinary labels, the same certainty that the people responsible would already be assembling an explanation. During the first war, they had called places like this isolated offences until the offences connected across half the country. Kingsley raised his wand over the shelves.

Water struck the warehouse floor in a broad sheet. It filled the gaps between the boards, ran beneath the broken crates and hissed where it met the copper. He held it there while the other Aurors worked through the surviving rows. Twice the fire broke through. Twice he pushed it back, changing the angle of the spell until his shoulder shook with the strain.

The shrill noise dwindled beneath the hiss of water, then stopped altogether. One by one, the bright squares went dark, leaving copper nails glowing briefly between the boards before they, too, cooled.

When Kingsley lowered his wand, rain was falling through the roof onto what remained of the shipment. Grey powder floated upon the water. Labels had become little islands of pulped paper. Three rows of crates still stood at the far wall. Vane approached him through the wreckage.

He was young enough that a night's work still showed upon his face as surprise. His robes were soaked to the knees. He carried a short iron spike wrapped in a handkerchief.

"Found these in the last row. Two hundred, perhaps more."

Kingsley studied the spike without touching it. Fine cuts circled its head. A pale filament ran from one cut to another, shifting whenever Vane moved his hand.

"Separate them. No more than one to a containment box."

"Do you know what they are?"

"I know I don't want the whole crate waking up together."

Vane looked at the spike again and held it farther from his body. Beyond the door, Tonks called for a healer. Kingsley took the leather case from the floor and went outside.

The rain had washed most of the splinters into the gutter. Tonks sat upon the warehouse step beside the woman they had found. An emergency blanket covered her shoulders. The bruise at her wrist looked almost black beneath the light from Tonks's wand. Their fourth Auror stood near the gate, speaking quietly into a communication mirror while Muggle traffic passed beyond the concealment charm. No one on the road looked towards the ruined door. Kingsley set the case upon a dry patch of stone and knelt in front of the woman.

"What's your name?"

Her gaze moved to the Ministry badge at his collar.

"Miriam."

"Miriam what?"

"Bell."

"Did they bring you here tonight?"

She shook her head.

"Tuesday." Her voice caught. "I work at the shipping office. I was supposed to copy the manifests. Just the manifests."

Tonks pulled the blanket more securely around her.

"You can tell the rest at St Mungo's," she said.

Miriam looked past them into the warehouse. "He told me to put the wrong addresses on."

"Which man?" Kingsley asked.

"The one you caught. I said the shipments wouldn't arrive. He said I wasn't paid to worry about that."

Kingsley waited.

"Then someone came for the blue boxes. I saw the name on the receipt. It was different. Everything was different. I asked—" She stopped and pressed her lips together. "I shouldn't have asked."

"You're allowed to ask about your work."

"He said that too."

Kingsley let the reassurance rest. She had already heard the same words from someone who had locked her in a cupboard; repeating them more firmly would give her little reason to believe him.

Tonks's expression had gone very still. She kept her hand upon the blanket, where Miriam could see it. On the road beyond the gate, a lorry passed with its radio playing loudly enough to reach them through the rain.

Miriam stared at the ordinary traffic as though she had forgotten it could still be there. A bus moved past the gate, its upstairs windows lit and its passengers oblivious to the broken warehouse a few yards from the road.

"Is this really the Ministry?" she asked.

Kingsley took the warrant from his coat. Its protective cover had kept the parchment dry. He unfolded it and showed her the seal, the date and Amelia Bones's signature beneath the authorisation.

"You can keep that copy."

She looked at it for a long time before taking it.

"They said nobody would come."

Behind Kingsley, Vane carried a black containment box out of the warehouse. It was small enough to hold a pair of shoes. Something inside struck its lid with a dull, impatient knock. Miriam flinched.

Tonks turned towards Vane. "Other side of the yard."

"Sorry."

He hurried past them. The healer arrived two minutes later. Kingsley watched her identify herself before he stepped away. Miriam still held the warrant when Tonks helped her to her feet.

There would be a formal statement, several medical examinations and questions about why she had not escaped earlier. Kingsley could already hear the careful voices asking them. He wrote a note upon the back of his own copy of the warrant, specifying where she had been found, the lock upon the door and the spell securing its hinges. Vane witnessed it. Tonks added her name beneath his. By the time anyone asked Miriam to explain the cupboard, three people would have recorded the answer.

The leather case contained twelve delivery slips, two brass keys and a folded set of instructions whose ink vanished whenever Kingsley tried to read the second line. He did not force it. He placed the document beneath a preservation charm, made a record of the changing text and moved on to the slips. Each carried a different company name. Four contained the same misspelling. Seven listed weights which did not match the crates still standing inside the warehouse.

From the inside of his coat, Kingsley took the note which had brought them here. Its envelope carried a French postmark, but the address beneath it had been typed in English. There was no signature. Three copied freight entries had been attached, each with a date, a weight and a company name. Someone had circled the same warehouse address on all three. He set one recovered slip beside the copies. The misspelling appeared in both.

The last recovered delivery slip had no destination. Kingsley checked its reverse and held it nearer the wandlight, but there was no faint impression of an address and no fold concealing another line.

It had a date, a collection window and a signature drawn with an unsteady hand. A series of small holes ran along the lower edge where it had been fastened to something before being removed. Tonks returned while he was examining it.

"She's on her way. Healer's staying with her."

"Good."

"Prisoner's awake. Says he's never seen the woman before."

"Record it."

"Already have. Along with the key we found in his pocket." She rubbed her sleeve beneath the cut on her cheek. "He wants to know which newspaper told us to come."

Kingsley looked up.

"Does he?"

"Very concerned about political interference. Less concerned about the lock."

Across the yard, the prisoner sat beneath a containment charm with his knees in the rain. He was older than Kingsley had first thought, perhaps fifty, with expensive boots beneath his work clothes. His shirt had come untucked during the fight. A dark strip of fabric was tied around his left wrist. He watched Kingsley watching him.

The prisoner smiled slowly, watching for a response. Kingsley had seen that expression in interview rooms: a man trying to suggest that the people questioning him had mistaken their own position.

Tonks followed Kingsley's gaze. "Want me to remove that?"

"At the office. With witnesses."

"Thought so."

Her communication mirror warmed inside her coat. She took it out, listened for a moment and handed it to Kingsley. Amelia Bones appeared in the glass.

"Report."

Kingsley gave it to her. One person arrested. Two seen escaping through a concealed internal route. Three others from the evening's arrivals still unaccounted for; the route might have been used before the Aurors entered. A civilian recovered from confinement. Contraband secured. Much of the consignment destroyed by a spell built into the floor. Amelia listened without interruption.

"Injuries?"

"Minor among the team. The civilian needs assessment."

"Your warrant covered stolen materials and prohibited transport equipment. Keep your report within what you can establish."

Kingsley glanced towards the prisoner.

"He's concerned about newspapers."

"He may read them in custody."

Tonks turned her face away.

Amelia continued. "Every item goes into a sealed register before it enters the evidence store. Two signatures on every transfer. Send copies directly to my office."

"You're expecting a problem."

"I'm expecting someone to take an interest once they discover I signed the warrant."

The prisoner shifted beneath the charm. Rain had plastered his hair against his forehead, but the smile remained.

"What do you want the seizure classified as?" Kingsley asked.

"What is it?"

"Magical goods under false shipping names. Some of it intended to interfere with defensive enchantments. We'll need the laboratory to tell us how."

"Then write that."

"And the pattern?"

Amelia's expression tightened behind her monocle.

"We investigate it. We don't give anyone an excuse to discard the physical evidence while arguing about the name at the top of the page."

Kingsley looked down at the delivery slips. Somewhere, people had supplied the warehouse, paid its staff and arranged for the crates to leave. Destroying the labels had not stopped the shipments already made. One arrested man would not tell them where the others had gone merely because a Ministry seal appeared upon the question.

"Understood."

"Shacklebolt."

He returned his attention to the mirror.

"The woman's statement is to be taken when the healer permits it. No sooner."

"I've put that in the report."

"Good. Finish there. I'll deal with the rest."

"One more thing." Kingsley glanced at the unsigned note. "Whoever supplied the shipping records should know they were right."

Amelia considered him through the glass. "I can send a confirmation through the contact who passed them on."

"Tell them we found the consignment. And that the woman is safe."

"Nothing identifying her."

"Of course."

Amelia nodded. "I'll have it sent."

The glass darkened. Tonks took it back and slid it into her coat.

"You look as if you want to say something."

"I'm deciding where to put it."

"Supplementary report. They're lovely. Nobody can complain you forgot anything, and they're too long to read comfortably."

Kingsley almost smiled. He set the slips beneath the preservation charm and stood. Across the yard, the prisoner's smile had finally begun to fade. Vane was explaining the conditions of his detention in a voice made steady by careful practice. The warehouse clock showed a little after two.

Kingsley folded the source's note and returned it to his coat, separate from the evidence being taken into custody. Whoever had copied those records had noticed a connection the Ministry had missed. He hoped the confirmation would reach them before they decided that sending it had been a mistake. He turned back towards the warehouse.

"Photograph the floor before anyone lifts those nails," he told Tonks. "We need to know how it was laid."

Tonks raised her wand to light the doorway, revealing the blackened grid beneath the standing water. She paused beside Kingsley while Vane fetched the camera from their equipment bag.

"I'll let Marlene know the tip paid off," she said. "She's been waiting to hear."

Kingsley glanced at her. "The same confirmation Amelia is sending. No names from tonight."

Tonks nodded, her usual smile absent as she looked towards the spot where Miriam had sat. "Just that someone came."

She turned the light back towards the floor. Kingsley followed her inside, keeping the unsigned note dry against his chest while the camera flashed over the first surviving line of copper nails.
