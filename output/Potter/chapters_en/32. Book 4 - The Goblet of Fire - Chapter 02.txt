2

The motorbike climbed until Surrey became a pattern.

Roads narrowed into dark threads between fields, and houses gathered into tidy blocks before loosening into woodland, reservoirs and villages still quiet beneath the early hour. Morning mist lay in the hollows of the countryside. Where the sun reached it, the vapour turned gold; beneath the long shadows of the trees, it remained grey enough to resemble water.

Harry watched Privet Drive disappear without being able to identify the moment it was gone. One second, Little Whinging spread beneath them in familiar rows of tiled roofs and fenced gardens; the next, every suburb looked alike. Number four became indistinguishable from any other square of brick built to contain a life Harry did not want.

He kept looking backwards anyway.

Wind pressed his clothes against him, while the engine's vibration travelled through the sidecar and up his spine with a steadiness that felt almost soothing. Hedwig sat in her cage behind him, feathers flattened indignantly by the speed despite the protective screen. Every few minutes, she clicked her beak in Sirius's direction, making her opinion of the arrangements perfectly clear.

"She says you're flying badly," Harry called.

Sirius glanced over his shoulder. "She's an owl."

"That sounded prejudiced."

"She has wings and still agreed to sit in a sidecar."

Hedwig struck the bars once with her beak.

"I wouldn't continue," Harry advised.

Sirius faced forwards again, and the motorbike banked gently above a bend in the river, then left the water behind and turned north. A shimmering distortion enclosed them, scarcely noticeable except where sunlight caught its edges. From within it, the world remained sharp; to anyone looking up from a farmhouse or an early train, the bike would resemble nothing more unusual than a patch of sunlight moving across thin cloud. Sirius had layered the concealment charms before leaving Privet Drive, checking each one twice and making Harry repeat the emergency release sequence.

He continued checking. His eyes moved to the mirrors, his left hand tested the altitude control, and whenever they encountered turbulence, his attention flicked towards the sidecar before returning to the horizon. Harry noticed on the fourth occasion and began keeping count.

"I'm still here," he said.

"Good."

"You've checked seven times."

"Six."

"That isn't better."

"The seventh was for traffic."

They were three hundred feet above an empty meadow. Harry leaned back and looked at the sky.

"Terrible congestion."

Sirius smiled, though his shoulders remained tight beneath the leather jacket. Neither of them mentioned the dream, but it travelled with them nonetheless, undisturbed by the increasing distance between Harry and the bedroom where he had woken.

Whenever he blinked, he saw the old man falling. His mind had preserved smaller pieces with greater cruelty than the whole: a hand opening near the floor, rain shining upon one shoulder, the instant his face emptied after the green light passed through him. The images arrived without warning, between a glimpse of sunlight and the next bend in the river.

Harry had seen death before. Quirrell had crumbled beneath his hands; the basilisk had died around a sword he held. Dementors had tried to hollow Sirius into something worse than dead while Harry watched from the edge of the lake. Yet the old man had not known Harry was there. He had not been fighting him or attempting to hurt anyone Harry loved. One moment, he stood in a room he had entered for reasons Harry could only guess; the next, Voldemort decided he should no longer exist.

Harry had felt the decision.

That was the part he could not fit into words, however carefully he arranged everything else. Sirius turned his head again, and Harry raised both hands.

"Eight."

"You went quiet."

"I'm on a flying motorbike. Most people would consider that reassuring."

"You are not most people."

"Hermione says that when she's about to criticise me."

"Hermione is a perceptive girl."

Harry settled lower in the seat. "Traitor."

The bike crossed another stretch of cloud. Cold damp touched Harry's face, and the countryside vanished into white until the only visible things were the black curve of the sidecar, Sirius's back and droplets racing across the windscreen. Without the fields beneath him, he could no longer tell how far they were from the ground.

His breathing shortened. Stone moved behind his eyes, and a vast, scaled body passed through darkness. Draconia lay beneath the carved face of Salazar Slytherin, her pale hair spread across the wet floor; behind hospital curtains, Hermione did not move.

Then the bike emerged into sunlight, and the memories lost their edges. Harry drew air carefully into his lungs. Sirius seemed to feel the change before he had quite managed it, reducing their speed without looking back.

"We can land."

"I'm fine."

"That wasn't the question."

"You didn't ask one."

"We can land," Sirius repeated. "Do you want to?"

Below them, a railway line curved through the hills. A train moved along it, smoke trailing low above the carriages, while sheep occupied a neighbouring field with the intense stillness of animals pretending they had not been watching the sky. Harry concentrated on the small, ordinary shapes until his surroundings felt solid again.

Landing would delay them. That thought arrived first, automatic and familiar; only afterwards did he understand that Sirius had offered him a choice. There had been no impatience in the question, no suggestion that the quicker route mattered more than whatever Harry might need. He tested the air in his chest.

"No," he said. "But can we fly lower?"

Sirius descended at once. The motorbike settled above the treetops, and leaves moved beneath them in broad green waves. Harry could distinguish individual stone walls now, cattle tracks worn into the grass and the white flash of water in a stream. The ground no longer seemed impossibly distant.

"Better?" Sirius asked.

"Yes."

He did not ask why, which helped more than Harry expected.

They flew north for another hour. Harry tried to remain awake, but he had slept only in fragments, and each one had ended with pain. Gradually, the rush of air became less bracing. His head tipped against the padded side of the seat; he corrected it, blinked, and discovered that the landscape had changed without his permission.

The hills were steeper, with dense oak and beech woodland gathered along their lower slopes. Villages appeared less frequently, their stone buildings clustered around narrow roads rather than laid out in obedient squares. Sirius followed a river until it passed beneath an old bridge, then turned north towards a ridge. Harry meant to ask how much farther they had to go, but his eyes closed before he could form the question.

When they opened again, the sun stood much higher and the gentle country of the morning had given way to Scottish hills. Harry had slept through most of the long journey north. The engine had quietened. The motorbike hovered above a lane barely wide enough for a car, enclosed by high hedges with white flowers pushing between their leaves. Ahead, the road ended at a wooden gate. Sirius brought them down onto the gravel with less violence than Harry had expected, and the concealment charms released one by one, each leaving a faint pressure in his ears.

Harry removed his helmet. The paper hat had disappeared somewhere over Surrey, leaving only a strip of Spellotape across the top.

"You fell asleep," Sirius said.

"I was observing with my eyes closed."

"Did you learn anything?"

"Your flying improves when no one is conscious to complain."

Sirius switched off the engine, and birdsong filled the sudden quiet almost immediately. Water moved somewhere beyond the hedge; overhead, leaves shifted in a breeze too gentle to feel. The air smelled of damp earth, crushed grass and something flowering nearby. Harry listened for a moment, waiting for engines, lawnmowers or a door being opened to check whether a neighbour's car had been washed. None came.

He climbed out of the sidecar, and his legs folded beneath him. Sirius caught his arm before his knees reached the gravel.

"Elegant," Harry said.

"No one saw."

Hedwig made a low sound from her cage.

"No one whose opinion matters."

Sirius kept one hand beneath Harry's elbow until he stood steadily. Even then, the grip merely loosened.

"Scar?"

"Motorbike."

"Head?"

"Still attached."

"Nauseous?"

"Only by the questioning."

Sirius studied his face. Harry returned the look, taking in the shadows beneath his eyes.

"You didn't sleep at all, did you?"

"I slept while Remus fixed the shelves."

"That explains why they're straight."

"They were always straight."

"You said they had character."

"Character can be geometrically sound."

Sirius released him and turned towards the gate. It appeared ordinary enough: weathered oak, an iron latch and moss growing along the lower edge. A small wooden sign hung crookedly from one post.

NO THROUGH ROAD

Beneath it, someone had added in smaller paint:

NO UNINVITED DARK WIZARDS EITHER

Almost hidden by the summer growth, a second plaque had been fixed to the stone at the base of the post. Lichen silvered its edges, and years of rain had deepened the letters cut into the wood.

HAWTHORN COTTAGE

Harry read the name twice. Privet Drive had always sounded like an instruction: a road leading through other people's lives, numbered so nothing could be mistaken for belonging. Hawthorn Cottage was less exact. It suggested roots beneath the boundary and branches allowed to grow in inconvenient directions. He raised an eyebrow at Sirius.

"The sign came with the cottage."

"Both lines?"

"The useful one is recent."

"What about the name?"

Sirius glanced at the hedge. "Either the cottage was named for the hawthorn or the hawthorn became territorial after reading the plaque. I've decided not to ask."

He opened the gate, revealing a lane that continued for twenty feet before meeting a wall of hawthorn so thick that not even light passed between its branches. Two wood pigeons lifted from the hedge as Harry approached. Beyond them, there was no glimpse of a roof or a window, nor any sign that a house stood nearby.

Sirius stopped beside the first gatepost, where a narrow strip of silver had been set into the wood, and placed his palm against it.

"Sirius Orion Black," he said. "Resident, owner and regrettably responsible adult."

The silver warmed. Harry felt a breath of wind pass through the hedge, though not a single leaf moved. Sirius glanced towards him, and some of the humour left his face.

"Your turn."

Harry stepped closer. "What do I say?"

"Your name. Then what you are to the house."

"A visitor?"

Sirius did not answer. As the quiet stretched, Harry looked past the open gate towards the impenetrable hedge, his stomach tightening for reasons that had nothing to do with the motorbike. The wards required truth; he understood that much. Old defensive magic listened to intention more carefully than language, which made clever phrasing risky and outright lies dangerous.

"What did you tell them?" he asked.

"When I set them?"

Harry nodded. Sirius put one hand inside his jacket pocket.

"That this was your home whenever you wanted it."

The statement took time to reach him. Whenever was a generous word, broad enough to contain September, the end of guardianship and days when Harry might prove considerably more difficult than a room prepared upstairs could justify. He could find no condition attached to it, however hard he tried.

"That's not a legal relationship," he said.

"The wards aren't solicitors."

"They might reject it."

"Then we'll find different wards."

Harry stared at the silver strip. Sirius remained beside the open gate without reaching for him, allowing the decision to belong entirely to Harry. At last, he lifted his hand. The metal felt cool against his palm.

"Harry James Potter," he said, his voice sounding too loud in the stillness. "Resident."

Nothing happened. Embarrassment heated his face, and he began to draw his hand away, already regretting the word. Then the silver caught him. Warmth passed beneath his skin, deep enough to touch bone without burning, and the scar upon his forehead tingled. For one breath, he felt the shape of the protection surrounding the land: lines beneath the earth, charms threaded through the trees, a dome of attention rising above the canopy.

The wards examined him. Then they opened.

The wall of hawthorn became transparent from the centre outwards. Branches thinned into mist, and sunlight appeared between them, followed by a narrow drive, two leaning apple trees and the edge of a stone roof. Harry lowered his hand. Where the hedge had stood, a second gate waited open upon the drive.

"Resident," Sirius repeated.

Harry faced him. "It worked."

"I had every confidence."

"You looked ill."

"That is my natural complexion."

Sirius turned quickly towards the motorbike, leaving Harry smiling behind him. Together, they wheeled it through the wards.

The cottage waited at the end of the drive, larger than Harry had expected and less impressive than Sirius had tried to imply. Two storeys of honey-coloured stone sat beneath a roof patched with at least four shades of slate. Ivy climbed one side and had made a determined attempt to reach the chimney, which leaned slightly to the left. That explained why Sirius had been awake threatening it.

A narrow porch sheltered the front door. One supporting beam was new; the other had been repaired with a strip of brass and optimism. Uneven windows reflected the surrounding garden, and the uppermost pane stood open beneath the eaves, allowing a pale curtain to stir in the breeze. Nothing matched at all, and Harry liked it immediately.

The garden had not yet decided whether it belonged to the cottage or the countryside around it. Lavender crowded the path, foxgloves rose beside the wall in tall purple columns, and farther back, nettles fought with roses, mint and herbs labelled in Remus's precise handwriting. At the rear of the property, a low stone boundary disappeared beneath brambles. Someone had begun cutting the grass, but the mower stood abandoned halfway through the task.

"It stopped working," Sirius said, noticing Harry's attention.

"The lawn or the mower?"

"Both became difficult."

A table and three chairs occupied the centre of the unfinished strip. One chair had fallen over, and a coffee cup rested on the grass beside it. Evidence of Sirius's night at Privet Drive lay everywhere: the cottage wore his departure like an interrupted sentence. Harry's gaze returned to the open upper window.

"Which room is that?"

Sirius lifted the trunk from the motorbike. "You'll see."

"That means mine."

"Possibly."

"Hermione already told me there are four."

"Hermione has been removed from all future conspiracies."

"She lasted longer than you."

Sirius carried the trunk towards the porch. It looked almost light in his hands, but Harry could see the effort in the angle of his shoulders.

"I can take that."

"I know."

"Then give it to me."

"No."

Harry followed with Hedwig's cage. "You were in prison for twelve years. I carried it down seven flights of stairs in June."

Sirius stopped beside a rosebush, and Harry realised how the words had sounded. The morning continued around them, full of birds and moving leaves, while he searched unsuccessfully for a better way to say what he meant. His gaze dropped to the gravel.

"I only meant—"

"I know what you meant." Sirius adjusted his grip. "And you are correct. I am demonstrating poor lifting technique."

"You don't have to carry it because you're my guardian."

"I'm carrying it because it is your first morning here and I have spent three weeks imagining doing so."

Harry looked up. There was no accusation in Sirius's expression, only a tired honesty that made refusal feel less like independence and more like taking something away from him.

"All right," Harry said.

"Thank you."

"If you drop it, I'm laughing."

"That seems fair."

Sirius reached the porch without incident and set the trunk down to open the door. There were six locks, which Harry counted with increasing interest.

"The previous owner collected them," Sirius said.

"And you use all six?"

"The previous owner had an excellent idea."

He opened the top lock with a brass key, the second with his wand and the third by pressing two fingers against a carved knot in the wood. The remaining three released when the wards recognised him.

"Overdone?" Sirius asked.

"Draconia will approve."

"I was afraid of that."

The door swung inwards. Sirius stepped aside.

"After you."

Harry entered cautiously. Stone flags covered the floor of a front hall narrow enough for the trunk to occupy most of it. Hooks lined one wall beneath a shelf crowded with scarves, dragon-hide gloves and a pointed hat he could not imagine Sirius wearing. Near the stairs, an umbrella stand contained three brooms, two actual umbrellas and what appeared to be a Muggle cricket bat. A framed notice hung above it.

CHECK FOR A TAIL BEFORE LEAVING THE HOUSE

Harry glanced back.

"Remus," Sirius said.

"Does it happen often?"

"Once was enough for a sign."

The hall opened into a sitting room warmed by sunlight. Two worn sofas faced a broad stone fireplace; one was green, while the other had probably been red before years faded it towards brown. Between them, a low table held a wizard chessboard, several Ministry envelopes and a mug containing screws. Nothing had been arranged to discourage people from sitting down.

Books filled the alcoves on either side of the fireplace, standing upright, sideways and in several unstable piles. Harry recognised titles on defence, magical law, curse-breaking and household repairs. A copy of Do It Yourself: Domestic Charms for the Recently Independent lay open on the floor, with an entire page crossed through in Sirius's handwriting.

Photographs covered the mantel, and Harry stopped in front of them.

He knew some of the older pictures. James, Sirius, Remus and Peter stood beside the Hogwarts lake, their school robes untidy and their faces bright with a future none of them could see. In another, Lily sat on the steps of the cottage in Godric's Hollow, laughing while the baby in her lap attempted to catch falling leaves. Sirius held Harry upside down by the ankles in a third, while James objected and photographic Harry shrieked with delight.

Newer photographs had been placed among them. Hermione and Draconia stood on either side of Harry outside the hospital wing after the Chamber. Hermione looked exhausted, Draconia was pale enough to seem translucent, and Harry's hands were bandaged. None of them smiled, but all three leaned towards one another, close enough for their shoulders to touch.

Another picture showed them beside the lake that spring, caught in an argument over something outside the frame. Draconia pointed at Harry, Hermione held a book against her chest, and Harry appeared to be laughing. As he watched, the figures repeated the quarrel soundlessly. Hermione raised her book as evidence; Draconia dismissed whatever it contained with one elegant movement. Yet when Harry doubled over, Draconia's attention left Hermione and remained upon him. A moment later, Hermione's indignation broke, and she lowered the book with a smile.

Harry could not remember anyone taking the photograph.

"Where did you get that?" he asked.

Sirius followed his gaze. "Professor Lupin. He claims a camera is useful when three people are incapable of standing still and admitting they are happy."

"We were arguing."

"You do most things while arguing."

The little Harry in the frame straightened and said something that made both girls turn on him at once. He immediately began laughing again. Harry suspected the photograph might be accurate.

At the centre of the mantel stood an empty silver frame, polished more carefully than the others. White space moved inside it like blank parchment.

"What goes there?" Harry asked.

"I thought we might take one today."

Sirius made the answer sound casual, then became very interested in the screws on the table. Harry looked back at the frame. There had been a place waiting before he arrived, among people who already belonged here.

He had spent his childhood learning that belongings were permitted only when they could be cleared away. Toys in the sitting room belonged to Dudley; shoes in the hall belonged to the Dursleys. Harry's things stayed in the cupboard or the smallest bedroom, where their presence did not force anyone to remember him. Here, his face already occupied the centre of the house.

"The empty one is crooked," he said.

Sirius glanced at it. "Remus hung it."

"Then you moved it."

"Baseless accusation."

Harry straightened the frame. When he stepped away, Sirius was smiling.

"Kitchen?" Harry asked.

"Safest to inspect it before the next structural event."

They continued through an archway into a kitchen that was exactly as it had appeared in the mirror, only larger and more chaotic now Harry could see beyond its edges. Cream cabinets lined the walls, and copper pans hung above a broad wooden table. The flagstone floor dipped near the back door; one window refused to shut fully despite three separate charms attached to its frame.

The table had been set for two. Plates waited beside cloth napkins, a jug of orange juice stood in a bowl of ice, and bread, fruit and several covered dishes occupied the centre. Most bore labels written in different hands.

EGGS — REMUS. DO NOT LET SIRIUS IMPROVISE.

PASTRIES — NARCISSA. HEAT FOR FOUR MINUTES. FOUR, SIRIUS.

FRUIT — THE GRANGERS. YES, HARRY, YOU HAVE TO EAT SOME.

Hermione had added the final sentence beneath her parents' handwriting. Harry touched the edge of the label, recognising the firm downstroke and the emphasis she had given some.

"They were all here?"

"Yesterday."

"Together?"

"Lucius and Mr Granger spent twenty minutes assembling a cupboard."

Harry stared at him. "Why?"

"Neither trusted the other to do it correctly."

"Did they?"

Sirius opened the cupboard, and the door immediately came off in his hand.

"They achieved diplomacy."

Harry laughed. Sirius propped the door against the wall as though its removal had been part of the intended design.

"Breakfast first or tour?"

Harry considered the food. His stomach still felt tight from the flight and the lingering dream, with hunger somewhere beneath both, distant enough to ignore.

"Tour."

Sirius noticed the hesitation, but all he said was, "Tour. Then breakfast."

He left the food on the table without asking Harry to promise, and they returned to the hall. The downstairs bathroom contained a deep tub, a shower installed with visible difficulty and a row of towels in four colours. Beside it, a small room served as a study. Remus had claimed one wall with books and organised files, while Sirius had claimed the other with maps, newspaper clippings and a dartboard bearing Cornelius Fudge's face. Three darts occupied the Minister's hat.

"You missed," Harry said.

"I was aiming for the hat."

"Why?"

"It offends me."

At the end of the hall, a back door opened into the garden. Muddy boots stood on a mat, and beside them waited a pair of trainers in Harry's size. He noticed them without asking; it seemed safer, for the moment, to let some things remain unexplained.

The narrow staircase complained beneath every step. Sirius avoided the third board, so Harry deliberately placed his foot upon it. A long, dramatic groan travelled through the wood.

"Remus hates that," Sirius said.

Harry stepped on it again.

"You'll fit in well."

Five doors opened onto the landing, where a square window overlooked the orchard. Sunlight fell across a rug patterned with moons and stars. Someone had charmed the moons to move through their phases, and the full ones rolled away whenever Sirius's shadow crossed them.

"Remus's idea?" Harry asked.

"Mine."

"That's terrible."

"He laughed."

"At you."

"Details."

Sirius indicated the first door. "Bathroom. The pipes object if both taps are opened at once. Hot water takes a minute. It does not mean you've done anything wrong."

Harry studied him. Sirius continued before that last sentence could be examined.

"My room."

The second door stood open. A broad bed sat beneath the sloping roof, half-made and surrounded by clothing, books and several boxes that had not been unpacked. Heavy curtains framed a window facing the drive. On the bedside table, a photograph of James and Lily rested beside the twin of Harry's two-way mirror. Nothing looked settled, and Harry understood that Sirius had prepared the rest of the cottage first.

"Remus sleeps there," Sirius said, pointing across the landing.

The third room was smaller and far neater, its bed made and its books arranged on the shelves in careful groups. A locked cabinet stood beneath the window, and a battered case rested at the foot of the wardrobe. Even the few possessions laid out in view seemed ready to be packed away at short notice.

"Does he live here?"

"When he allows himself."

"What does that mean?"

"It means he has decided needing a place and accepting one are unrelated matters."

Harry's gaze moved to Sirius, who looked back without softening the observation.

"He sounds difficult," Harry said.

"Impossible."

They both knew the conversation had ceased being exclusively about Remus. Sirius closed the door, leaving two unexplored rooms. The larger stood beneath the moving curtain Harry had seen from outside. Its paint was new, dark blue against the pale landing wall, and a small brass plate had been fixed at eye level. Nothing had been written on it.

Sirius set the trunk down.

"I didn't put your name on it," he said. "I thought you might want to choose."

Harry touched the blank plate. "Choose what?"

"Harry. Potter. Keep Out. Beware of Owl. Something dramatic in Latin."

"Do you know anything dramatic in Latin?"

"I know several words individually."

"Dangerous."

"That too."

Sirius opened the door, and the room beyond was full of light.

Two windows met in the far corner, one overlooking the orchard and the other the garden. The walls were a muted blue-grey that changed with the sun. Beneath the lower slope of the roof stood a broad bed covered with a dark green quilt; beside it, a table held a lamp, a water jug and enough space for the mirror.

The desk faced into the room rather than towards the wall. From its chair, Harry would be able to see both windows, the door and most of the landing beyond it. He stood on the threshold for a moment, taking in the arrangement. Sirius had noticed, and had done something about it without making Harry explain.

Shelves covered one wall. They were straight. The wardrobe opened without sticking, revealing empty coat hangers above drawers lined with clean paper. At the foot of the bed, a low stand waited for his trunk, high enough that he would not need to kneel to reach it. Nothing had been crowded in to use up the space; there was room for things he did not own yet.

For one unguarded second, he forgot to be afraid of wanting all of it.

He entered carefully. The floorboards did not creak, and a rug softened the space beside the bed. Mounting brackets for his Firebolt had been fixed above the desk, well away from the window and high enough to keep the broom safe without placing it out of reach. On the bedside table sat a shallow wooden dish. Harry picked it up and turned it between his hands.

"For your glasses," Sirius said.

"I have a table."

"You also have a habit of falling asleep with them on and waking convinced they've been stolen."

Harry set down the dish. "Remus told you."

"Hermione provided documentation."

The window above the desk stood open. Beyond it, the orchard sloped towards a stream partly concealed by willows, with sunlight moving on the water. A low hill rose beyond the boundary wall, its summit crowded with pines. No neighbouring window faced this one. Harry rested both hands upon the sill and breathed in the cool air coming through it.

"The wards extend to the stream," Sirius said behind him. "The hill is outside them, but we can add it if you want somewhere to fly. Hogsmeade is a few miles west, beyond the ridge. There is another village about two miles east. Muggle, mostly. The nearest magical house belongs to a retired apothecary who keeps attempting to sell me a goat."

"Why?"

"He says it can detect curses."

"Can it?"

"It dislikes me, which proves nothing."

Harry watched the stream. The room was quiet in a way Privet Drive had never managed. Silence there had always seemed to listen for him, waiting for the scrape of a chair or a footstep that might count against him. Here, it held birdsong, water, the movement of curtain fabric and Sirius waiting near the door.

"What don't you like?" Sirius asked.

Harry turned. "What?"

"About the room."

"Nothing."

"That is not possible. Remus dislikes the colour of the rug. Hermione said the desk required a larger drawer. Draconia objected to the window locks."

Harry examined them. "What's wrong with the locks?"

"They weren't complicated enough."

"She has standards."

"She has paranoia."

"That too."

Sirius leaned one shoulder against the doorframe. "The colour can change. So can the furniture. If you want the bed somewhere else, we move it. If you hate all of it, we begin again."

Harry looked around once more, unable to find a single thing he could imagine having chosen better.

"I don't hate it."

"Then tell me one thing you would change."

The request felt like a test for which he had not prepared. At Privet Drive, preference had been another word for ingratitude. Food was eaten or refused; clothes were accepted or went without replacement. Dudley chose the wallpaper, the furniture and which possessions occupied two rooms, while Harry received what remained. Being asked for an opinion did not come with much practice in giving one.

Sirius continued waiting. Harry studied the walls, the quilt and finally the bedside table.

"The lamp," he said.

Sirius looked at the small brass lamp. "What about it?"

"It's ugly."

Relief crossed Sirius's face so plainly that Harry nearly laughed.

"Hideous," Sirius agreed. "Remus chose it."

"You're lying."

"Completely. We'll throw it into the stream."

"We could move it downstairs."

"Cruel. I like it."

"Then why is it in here?"

"I panicked."

Harry smiled as Sirius stepped into the room, removed the lamp and carried it out onto the landing with unnecessary solemnity. When he returned, the empty patch on the table looked more important than it should have. Something had changed because Harry wanted it changed, and the rest of the room had survived the request.

He ran one thumb along the windowsill.

"How long is it mine?"

The question stopped Sirius near the bed. Harry wished he could take it back; he had not meant to expose the thought so directly, especially after being given so much.

"I mean, when school starts—"

"It remains yours."

"What if you move?"

"Then it moves with us."

"What if Remus needs it?"

"Remus has one."

"What if—"

"Harry."

Sirius crossed the room. He did not touch him, but stood close enough for Harry to see the faint grey beginning at his temples and the darker shadows exhaustion had left beneath his eyes.

"This is not a guest room," Sirius said. "I am not lending it to you until a better use appears. You do not have to earn another week by being easy to live with."

Harry's throat tightened. "You don't know if I'm easy to live with."

"Neither are your friends. We'll survive."

"Draconia doesn't live here."

"Tell her that. She has already replaced my window locks."

Harry tried to smile, though it arrived unevenly. Sirius's voice softened.

"You can stay every holiday the wards allow. Weekends, if the school permits. After Hogwarts, if that is what you want. If we argue, it remains your room. If I'm angry, it remains your room. If you make a mistake so catastrophic that half the roof disappears, we repair the roof around your room."

"Only half?"

"I have confidence in your restraint."

Harry lowered his eyes and discovered that his hands had closed into fists against the windowsill. He opened them slowly, letting his fingers lie flat upon the wood.

"All right."

Sirius exhaled. "All right?"

"The lamp goes."

"Savage."

Harry nodded towards the trunk. "Put that on the stand before you collapse."

"I am perfectly capable—"

"Your left hand is shaking."

Sirius looked down at it. The tremor was slight and had probably begun downstairs. Harry would not have noticed a year ago; now, he could see the effort Sirius made to close his fingers more firmly around the handle.

"Motorbike," Sirius said, setting the trunk down.

"That excuse is taken."

"Lack of sleep."

"Also taken."

"I'm older. I receive additional excuses."

Harry took one end of the trunk. Together, they lifted it onto the stand, where it fitted perfectly.

Breakfast began with smoke.

"The note said four minutes," Harry reminded Sirius.

Sirius opened the oven, releasing a black cloud that rolled into the kitchen and gathered against the ceiling.

"It has been approximately four."

"It has been twelve."

"Time behaves differently in magical households."

"Not that differently."

Sirius used his wand to draw out the tray. The pastries had fused into six dark shapes that retained only theoretical connections to food. Harry opened the back door, allowing cool air to move through the room and carry the smoke towards the garden.

"Narcissa will know," he said.

"She cannot possibly."

One of the labels on the table turned scarlet.

I KNOW.

Harry bent over the table laughing. After a few seconds, the sound hurt his ribs, and he straightened, trying to breathe. Sirius stood beside the oven with flour on one sleeve and the ruined tray suspended between them, watching him.

"What?" Harry asked.

"Nothing."

Sirius's smile held too much, and Harry was the first to look away.

They abandoned the pastries and attempted eggs. Sirius understood the theory of frying them, but possessed no patience for the practice. The first stuck to the pan; the second landed on the floor after an ambitious flipping charm. The third reached a plate with its yolk intact. Sirius placed it before Harry with the satisfaction of a man who had overcome serious odds.

"Triumph."

"You're eating the floor one."

"It was there for less than five seconds."

"Excellent. The bacteria didn't have time to organise."

Harry took a piece of toast. The kitchen had warmed, and sunlight crossed the table, illuminating scratches left by previous occupants. The smoke thinned near the ceiling. Outside, a robin landed on the fallen garden chair and examined the abandoned coffee cup with considerably more interest than Sirius had shown it.

Sirius sat opposite Harry with the damaged egg and a mug of coffee strong enough to smell bitter from across the table.

"House rules," he said.

Harry's hand stopped near the butter. Of course there were rules. He had expected them sooner, and the ease of the morning had almost allowed him to forget that every house required something of the people living in it.

"Fine."

Sirius heard something in the answer. "You could at least wait until I invent them before objecting."

"You said house rules."

"I did."

"That usually means I'm about to be told what I've already done wrong."

The humour left the kitchen. Sirius put down his fork.

"Not here."

Harry spread butter across his toast with unnecessary attention. "I know."

"Do you?"

"You've said."

"That was not the question."

Harry bit into the toast rather than answer. Sirius considered him for a moment, then leaned back, leaving his fork where it was.

"Rule one," he said. "If you are hurt, ill, frightened or in trouble, you tell me."

"That is four rules."

"It is one rule with examples."

"Broad examples."

"You may submit an appeal in writing."

"Hermione has forms."

"Of course she does."

Sirius lifted one finger.

"Rule two: if either of us leaves the wards, the other knows where and roughly when to expect them back."

Harry glanced up. "Either of us?"

"Yes."

"You have to tell me too?"

"It would be unreasonable otherwise."

Harry stared at him. Uncle Vernon announced his movements only when doing so created a task for someone else. Adults left and children waited, an arrangement Harry had accepted so completely that it seemed less like a household choice than a rule of nature.

"What if you're late?" he asked.

"You call my mirror. If I don't answer, you contact Remus. If neither of us answers, use the emergency mirror and alert whoever is on the list."

"What if I'm late?"

"I call your mirror. If you don't answer, I begin with reasonable concern and progress towards dramatic rescue."

"How long does reasonable concern last?"

"Depends whether you are with Hermione."

"What if I'm with Draconia?"

"Dramatic rescue begins immediately."

Harry smiled. Sirius lifted a second finger.

"Rule three: closed doors are knocked upon."

"Even mine?"

"Especially yours."

"What if I don't answer?"

"If there is no reason to believe you're in danger, I leave you alone."

Harry had not expected that answer. He turned it over for a moment, looking for the part that made permission temporary.

"And if there is?"

"I enter, apologise later and accept whatever you throw at me."

"I'm keeping books near the door."

"I'll wear a helmet."

Sirius lifted a third finger.

"Rule four: food in this house belongs to everyone unless labelled otherwise. You do not need permission to take it, and no one will count what is missing."

Harry set his toast down. Sirius's gaze did not stray towards the stairs or the wardrobe above them, but Harry understood all the same. He knew about the biscuits. Perhaps he had known before today; perhaps Hermione had included them in her documentation, or Narcissa had noticed replacements disappearing into Harry's sleeve during visits.

Heat crept up his neck.

"I don't steal food."

"I didn't say you did."

"Then why make it a rule?"

"Because cupboards exist to be opened."

"I know how cupboards work."

Sirius held his gaze. Harry pushed back from the table, and the chair legs scraped loudly across the stone.

"May I be excused?"

The words were out before he heard them. Sirius flinched as though Harry had thrown something, and for a moment neither of them moved. Harry stood beside the table with his shoulders tight, waiting for permission he had just been told was unnecessary. Sirius looked down at his coffee.

"You don't have to ask that," he said.

"You wanted rules."

"Not that one."

"I'm not hungry."

"All right."

There was no warning attached to it, no demand that Harry finish his egg, no apology required for wasting food. He hated how much that unsettled him. Leaving should have been the simple part, yet he crossed the kitchen with the uneasy feeling that he had missed something essential.

The hall seemed narrower on the way back. He climbed the stairs, stepped deliberately over the third board and entered his room, where the trunk waited on its stand. Harry shut the door without locking it. For several minutes, he remained in the centre of the room, unable to remember why he had come.

The window was still open, and the stream continued moving beneath the willows. Nothing in the view had changed because he had left the table badly. He sat on the bed, then straightened when the mattress gave beneath him. Even its softness was unfamiliar.

Downstairs, a chair moved and water ran into the sink. Sirius did not follow.

Harry opened his trunk. The top layer contained school robes, folded parchment and two jumpers Mrs Weasley had sent at Christmas. Beneath them lay the paper bag from the Dursleys' wardrobe, packed before dawn with two biscuit packets and an apple. Proof that he did not trust a kitchen he had not yet seen.

He removed the bag and placed it on the bed. The apple had bruised during the flight, and he pressed one thumb into the soft mark, feeling the skin yield beneath it.

A knock sounded. Harry's body tightened before he could stop it.

"It's me," Sirius said through the door.

"I know."

"May I come in?"

The paper bag seemed to occupy half the bed. Harry could hide it beneath the blanket or return it to the trunk and close the lid before Sirius entered. Either would take only a second, and he had spent years learning to move quickly.

Instead, he said, "Yes."

Sirius opened the door carrying Harry's plate in one hand and a small wooden box beneath the other arm. The egg had been placed between two pieces of toast, with several apple slices arranged beside it. His gaze found the paper bag, and he did not look surprised.

Harry's shame sharpened into anger.

"Who told you?"

"No one."

"You knew."

"I suspected."

"Because I'm so disturbingly predictable?"

Sirius placed the plate on the desk.

"Because I hid food beneath my bed until I was sixteen."

Harry stopped. Sirius held up the wooden box, which was the size of a large book and had a hinged lid and small brass handles.

"Remus found this in the shed."

He set it on the bedside table where the ugly lamp had stood and opened it. Inside lay wrapped biscuits, two chocolate bars, dried fruit, crackers and several packets of nuts. The supplies fitted neatly together, with space at one end for more.

"This is yours," Sirius said. "I'll replace whatever you use. You can add anything you like."

Harry stared at the box. "You just said food belongs to everyone."

"It does."

"Then why do I need this?"

"You don't."

Sirius sat in the desk chair, leaving the space beside Harry empty.

"But knowing food exists downstairs and believing it will still exist when you need it are different things. You may believe the first before the second. Until then, keep the bag. Keep the box. Keep both."

Harry pressed his thumb deeper into the apple's bruise.

"Your parents didn't feed you?"

"They fed me."

Sirius's mouth twisted around the distinction.

"Meals at Grimmauld Place were formal. Being late meant missing them. Arguing meant losing them. My mother occasionally decided hunger encouraged obedience. After I left, the Potters kept more food in their kitchen than usual because I woke at night convinced someone would tell me I had eaten enough."

Harry tried to imagine Sirius at sixteen, newly arrived in another family's house with all his belongings and no idea which generosity might disappear. The Sirius in front of him had once sat in a room like this and kept food hidden where nobody could take it. Harry looked down at the paper bag.

"Did it stop?" he asked.

"Eventually."

"How?"

Sirius considered before answering.

"Your grandmother stopped pretending not to notice. Every night before bed, she asked me to help prepare breakfast for the next morning. I could see the food. I knew where it would be. If I woke hungry, she left the kitchen unlocked."

"The kitchen had a lock?"

"The Potters had a very determined Kneazle."

Harry touched the edge of the box. "I don't wake hungry."

"All right."

"I only kept those because Vernon used to change his mind."

"All right."

"I know you won't."

Sirius's eyes softened. "Knowing is allowed to take time."

Harry turned towards the window, though the orchard had begun to blur. He rubbed his glasses with the heel of his palm before anything could fall far enough to become visible. Sirius became interested in the trees, giving him time to stop without requiring him to admit he had started.

"The egg survived," Sirius said after a moment. "Mostly."

Harry glanced at the plate. "Is eating upstairs allowed?"

"It is now."

"You're bad at rules."

"I've had limited practice making reasonable ones."

Harry took half the sandwich. It was still warm. Sirius stayed in the chair while he ate, looking occasionally towards the open window, and when Harry finished one half, he placed the second inside the wooden box. Sirius saw. Neither mentioned it.

After a while, Harry glanced towards the landing.

"You skipped a door."

Sirius's expression became blank with theatrical speed. "Did I?"

"There were five."

"Old houses contain many doors."

"Hermione said there was a fourth bedroom."

"Hermione also said I needed to replace the sitting-room curtains. We cannot trust her judgement."

"What's in it?"

"Nothing."

"Then show me."

"No."

Harry narrowed his eyes. Sirius rose from the chair and collected the empty plate.

"Breakfast has made you argumentative."

"I was argumentative before breakfast."

"Then imagine how much worse you might become if properly fed."

Harry followed him into the passage. The final door stood at the far end of the landing, beneath the lowest part of the roof. It was narrower than the others and painted the same cream as the wall, with a shiny new lock beneath the handle. Three fine silver lines crossed the keyhole, intersecting in the angular pattern Draconia used when marking wards in the margins of Hermione's notes. Harry knew who had designed it without needing to touch it.

"She was here," he said.

Sirius kept walking.

"Both of them were here."

"Many people have been here."

"Planning my birthday."

Sirius stopped at the stairs. "You know, most people pretend not to understand a surprise when presented with overwhelming evidence."

"Why?"

"Kindness."

Harry considered the locked door. "Sounds inefficient."

"That is because you associate with Malfoys."

"You associate with a Malfoy."

"Under supervision."

"You invited Lucius to dinner."

"Remus supervised."

Harry stepped deliberately on the third stair, and its groan followed them downstairs.

Once the plate had been returned to the kitchen, they went back up to unpack together.

Harry had not intended to unpack at all. His trunk could remain closed for a month without inconveniencing him; he had lived from it throughout three school years and twice as many temporary rooms. Clothes could be lifted from the top, worn, washed and returned without drawers becoming involved. Emptying it felt unnecessarily permanent.

Sirius disagreed.

"Jumpers?" he asked from beside the wardrobe.

Harry sat cross-legged near the trunk. "In there."

"I can see that. Where do you want them?"

"Anywhere."

"Top drawer or middle?"

"Whichever."

Sirius folded his arms. Harry looked up.

"What?"

"You have opinions about everything from Ministry policy to the moral character of my curtains. You must possess one regarding drawers."

"The middle one."

"Excellent choice."

Sirius opened it, and Harry passed over the two jumpers. He paused before a faded shirt that had belonged to Dudley, its fabric too broad through the shoulders and mended beneath one arm. He had worn it during the previous summer because it attracted less attention than school clothes. Sirius took it, his fingers finding the repair before he had finished unfolding it.

"This is enormous."

"It's comfortable."

"Harry."

"What?"

"I can see through the elbow."

"Ventilation."

Sirius placed the shirt on the bed rather than in the drawer.

"We'll go into the village this week."

"I don't need clothes."

"You need sleeves."

"Robes have sleeves."

"Muggle clothes."

"I own those."

Sirius lifted another of Dudley's shirts. It could have contained both of them during a strong wind.

"This is less clothing than a portable shelter."

Harry tried not to smile. "Narcissa bought me things last summer."

"School things. Three shirts and two pairs of trousers are not a wardrobe."

"They are if you put them in one."

Sirius stared at him. Harry held the look for three seconds before laughing.

"That was terrible," Sirius said.

"You walked into it."

"I am reconsidering the room."

The newer shirts went into the middle drawer. Sirius made a separate pile of the old ones, leaving them there when Harry refused to make a decision immediately. Books filled the first shelf, parchment and ink occupied the desk, and the trunk's contents began to spread through the room in small, recognisable groups. Hedwig watched from her cage with the air of someone awaiting a more competent removal service.

Harry put his letters in the top drawer, hesitated, then moved them to the second when he realised the first did not have a lock. Sirius pretended to be arranging quills.

"The desk locks," he said.

He bent to inspect the underside. A small brass keyhole was hidden beneath the right edge; when he pressed it, three drawers sealed at once.

"You don't have a key?" Harry asked.

"The ward recognises your hand."

"Can you open it with magic?"

"Probably."

The honesty surprised him. Sirius placed the quills in a cup.

"I won't."

Harry tested the lock, and it released beneath his thumb. He returned the letters to the top drawer. The choice felt larger than it was, though the parchment fitted just as neatly there as it had before.

They found places for his broom case, his school cauldron and the objects that had accumulated without Harry noticing: a smooth stone from the edge of the Black Lake; a Chocolate Frog card of Morgana which Draconia insisted had no value while repeatedly attempting to trade for it; a paper bird Hermione had charmed during a History of Magic lesson; and a small hippogriff Hagrid had carved before anyone explained that the wings were backwards. Taken out of the trunk, they looked less like things he happened to have kept and more like possessions.

Sirius handled each as though Harry had given him evidence.

"You kept this?" he asked, holding up the paper bird.

"It bites."

The little parchment beak closed on his finger.

"I see."

"Hermione made it by accident."

"She said that?"

"She said the biting was an unexpected expression of the charm."

"A lawyer's answer."

Sirius placed the bird on the shelf. It ruffled its folded wings and settled beside the stone. Harry's possessions filled less than a quarter of the available space, but the emptiness no longer looked accusing. It looked patient.

A chime sounded beyond the windows. Sirius straightened, and the note repeated twice, low and clear, like a glass touched at the edge of hearing.

"What's that?"

"The boundary."

Sirius's wand was in his hand before he left the room. Harry followed him onto the landing, where the square window revealed a figure walking up the drive with a battered case and a brown paper parcel. Remus looked towards the cottage. Sirius lowered his wand.

"He could have used the gate mirror."

"Maybe he wanted to test whether your wards work."

"Then he could have brought less suspicious luggage."

They met him at the front door. Remus's robes were clean but rumpled, and his greying hair had been combed without enthusiasm. Faint scars showed against skin made pale by the recent moon. He looked tired enough for Harry's questions about the dream to retreat behind concern.

"Happy birthday," Remus said.

Harry smiled. "You came early."

"Sirius sent eight messages."

"Three," Sirius corrected.

"Eight pages."

"Details were necessary."

Remus looked from him to Harry. "How is your scar?"

"Everyone asks that before anything else."

"Would you prefer I ask after your wardrobe?"

"We've had difficulties."

"The wardrobe or the people discussing it?"

"Both."

Remus set down his case and opened his arms. Harry crossed the final step to hug him. The embrace felt different from Sirius's; Remus held him carefully, as though mindful of old bruises even when none remained. One hand rested between Harry's shoulder blades.

"Fourteen," he said.

"Apparently."

"Your father was convinced fourteen made him an adult."

"Was he?"

"No."

Sirius took the case. "James was unusually mature."

"James attempted to charm a suit of armour into following Slughorn for three days."

"Academic inquiry."

"It sang."

"An imperfect charm."

Remus released Harry. "He was banned from the dungeons for a month."

"Was that before or after you became prefect?" Harry asked.

Remus's expression changed by one degree.

"After."

"Did you punish them?"

"Extensively."

Sirius laughed and carried the case inside. Harry watched him go.

"You didn't."

"I wrote a very stern report."

"What happened to it?"

"James corrected the spelling."

They entered the sitting room, where Remus stopped at the sight of the empty frame on the mantel.

"He moved it," Sirius called from the kitchen.

"It was crooked," Harry said.

"It was straight when I left."

"You hung it. That seems unlikely."

Remus smiled. For a few minutes, the cottage became busy around him as he removed the house-warming wards from his case, handed Sirius the paper parcel and inspected the repaired cupboard door. He moved through the rooms with the familiarity of someone who knew where things should be, even if he had not quite agreed to belong there.

"Where are the pastries?" he asked.

Sirius put the parcel on top of a dish towel that did not entirely conceal six burnt shapes. Remus lifted the towel.

"Twelve minutes?" he guessed.

"Approximately."

"Narcissa said four."

"I was distracted."

Harry sat at the table. "He was making rules."

Remus looked at Sirius.

"Reasonable ones," Sirius said.

"Harry?"

Harry considered. "Three reasonable. One suspicious."

"Four excellent," Sirius replied.

Remus poured water into the kettle. The ordinary movement did not conceal why he had come, and Harry's attention drifted back towards the battered case. It held more than spare clothes. He had seen Remus pack medical instruments into it before full moons: silver measures, phials, a charmed stethoscope and strips of parchment designed to record changes in magic.

He felt Sirius watching him work it out.

"We can do it after the party," Harry said.

"No," Sirius answered.

Harry studied him.

"It will take ten minutes," Remus said. "We check the scar, record what you remember and stop if you want to stop."

"Dumbledore already knows."

"Dumbledore cannot examine you from Scotland."

"He usually manages."

Sirius's hand tightened around the parcel.

"Harry."

The kitchen lost its warmth again. Harry knew the reasonable answer: he had watched someone die through magic nobody understood, and his scar connected him to Voldemort in a way that had hurt twice within a day. Refusing an examination because he wanted the morning to remain ordinary would not make it so. Knowing that did very little to stop the resentment rising in him.

"Ten minutes," he said.

"Ten," Remus agreed.

They used the downstairs study. Harry sat in a wooden chair beside the window while Remus moved the Ministry files off the desk and unpacked his case. Sirius remained near the door with his arms folded, occupying considerably more of the room than the furniture accounted for.

"You can wait outside," Harry told him.

"I can."

He did not move. Harry turned to Remus, who was adjusting a small silver instrument.

"You may remove either of us," Remus said.

"And spend an hour convincing Sirius it isn't personal?"

"Thirty minutes," Sirius said.

Harry sighed. "Stay."

Remus washed his hands with a movement of his wand, then drew the curtains halfway across the window. The study remained bright, but the softened light caught the thin silver instruments on the desk without reflecting sharply from them. Harry recognised some from Remus's months teaching at Hogwarts. A narrow glass disc had once measured the magical damage left by a Grindylow bite. A jointed silver wand, no longer than Harry's palm, unfolded into a triangle when held above cursed objects. The smallest instrument resembled a compass without directions, its black needle restless beneath the glass.

"Glasses off," Remus said.

Harry obeyed, and the room blurred immediately. Sirius became a dark shape near the door; Remus's face lost its scars and most of its expression.

"Look at the window."

"I can't see it."

"The bright area, then."

Remus passed his wand slowly across Harry's eyes. Cool blue light followed its tip, making his pupils ache without quite hurting. For an instant, the scar tightened as though the magic had brushed a thread tied behind it. Harry's fingers closed on the edges of the chair.

"There," Remus said quietly.

Sirius took a step forwards.

"I'm fine," Harry told the shape near the door.

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

Remus returned Harry's glasses. "Again. Tell me when you feel anything."

This time, he held the jointed instrument above Harry's forehead. Its three silver arms opened, and a drop of blue light gathered where they met, suspended inches from the scar. Nothing happened at first. Then the drop stretched, drawing itself into a filament finer than spider silk. It stopped before touching Harry's skin and trembled above the lightning-shaped mark.

On the desk, the compass swung towards it so violently that the needle struck the edge of its case. Pain flickered behind Harry's right eye.

"Now," he said.

Remus withdrew the instrument. The blue thread snapped soundlessly and recoiled into the silver arms. Sirius had crossed half the room without Harry seeing him move.

"Was that expected?" he asked.

"A response was." Remus folded the arms with careful fingers. "Its strength was not."

"That is a particularly poor answer."

"It is the honest one."

Harry put his glasses on properly. "Did it show what caused it?"

"No. Diagnostic charms can reveal that curse magic has moved; they cannot tell us who moved it or what it carried." Remus set down the instrument. "That is why I need the questions."

The examination had taken less than three minutes, and Harry already felt less like a person than a problem placed beneath good light. He crossed his arms, conscious of both men waiting for him.

"Ask."

Remus began with ordinary questions: headache, nausea, lost time, changes in vision or hearing. Harry answered each one, letting his gaze drift around the study as the quill moved across the parchment. Then the list reached dreams.

"Tell me the second one from the beginning."

Harry turned towards the window. The study faced the side garden, where foxgloves moved beneath the glass. A bumblebee climbed inside a purple flower and disappeared entirely.

"I already wrote it down."

"I read what you wrote."

"Then why do I need to say it?"

"Because language can change what a memory reveals. The details you avoid while speaking may differ from those you omitted when writing."

"That sounds invasive."

"It can be," Remus said. "You may refuse."

Sirius shifted near the door. Harry almost did refuse, with the words already forming at the back of his throat. Then he remembered the old man's hand opening beside the floor.

"There was rain," he began.

He described the room, the floating candles and the man with the ring. He explained how the dream had moved without him, carrying him first beside the dead man and then somewhere behind his eyes. Saying it aloud made the images sharper. The wet shoulder, the stone and the green light seemed closer than the flowers beyond the window, and his voice faltered when he reached the curse.

Sirius stepped away from the door. Harry held up one hand, and Sirius stopped, allowing him to finish.

"Voldemort looked at me."

"Are you certain?" Remus asked.

"No."

"What made it feel that way?"

Harry searched for the distinction. "Everyone else in the dream looked at the room. He looked through it."

Remus wrote that down.

"Did he speak to you?"

"He said 'begin.'"

"Could he have been speaking to Nott?"

"Yes."

"But you don't think he was."

"No."

Remus's quill paused. Sirius crossed the study and crouched beside Harry's chair.

"What aren't you saying?"

Harry watched the bumblebee push backwards out of the foxglove. Its legs found the rim of the flower, and a moment later it lifted into the sunlight.

"I think he knew I could hear him."

Sirius's face hardened.

"The connection may work both ways," Remus said.

"No," Sirius replied.

"We don't know."

"Then we do not tell him that man might be looking through his eyes."

"I didn't say that."

"You implied—"

"Stop."

Harry's voice cut between them. Both men fell silent, but the study seemed smaller than it had before. He looked from one to the other.

"If it's possible, say it."

Remus placed his quill down.

"We have no evidence that Voldemort can see through you," he said. "We have evidence that powerful emotion or magic on his side of the connection produces pain and images on yours. Until last night, every known transfer travelled in one direction."

"Known transfer?"

"Your mother's death. Quirrell. The diary."

Harry's hand rose towards the scar and stopped before reaching it.

"The diary wasn't Voldemort."

"It carried his magic," Sirius said. "Dumbledore believes destroying it may have disturbed the curse that left your scar."

"He never told me that."

Anger came more easily than fear. It gave Harry something solid to hold, something he could direct towards a person instead of an unanswered question.

Sirius stood. "He should have."

"He may not be certain," Remus said.

"Then he should have said he wasn't certain."

Sirius began pacing between the shelves and the desk, though the room did not provide enough distance for it. Every turn brought him back towards Harry's chair.

"A connection which reaches the boy in his sleep, and Dumbledore allowed him to return to a house where no one could recognise a magical seizure."

"Sirius," Remus warned.

"Petunia heard him. Petunia. She left him in a cupboard and we expected her to identify Dark magic in time to help?"

Harry's stomach tightened. The argument was about him, and that made it his responsibility to end it. The certainty arrived before he could question it.

"I was safe," he said.

Neither man seemed to hear.

"The wards were necessary," Remus replied. "You agreed."

"I agreed because every alternative was described as fatal."

"And if you had removed him?"

"I would have protected him."

"From magic which survived thirteen years? With wards you began studying three weeks ago?"

Sirius's face changed. Harry stood so quickly that his chair struck the wall, the sharp sound bringing both men round to face him.

"It doesn't matter," he said.

Remus frowned. "What doesn't?"

"Any of it. The wards renewed. I'm here. Stop arguing."

"Harry—"

"I said I'm fine."

He moved towards the door, and Sirius stepped aside immediately. Harry crossed the hall, passed the sitting room and went out through the back door, keeping his pace measured until there was no one left to see it.

The garden had brightened beneath the climbing sun. Sirius's abandoned mower remained where he had left it, and a narrow path led through the orchard towards the stream. Harry followed it, letting the cottage fall behind him. Grass brushed his trainers, and bees moved among clover at the roots of the trees. He walked until branches concealed the house and the voices inside could no longer reach him.

Only then did he stop.

The stream was shallow, its water clear enough to reveal the small stones beneath the surface. Ferns crowded one bank. On the other, a willow extended low branches across the water, sheltering a patch of shade. Harry sat on a flat rock and pressed his trembling hands between his knees.

The argument had not been loud. Sirius had not threatened Remus, Remus had not left, and neither man had blamed Harry. His body had failed to understand any of those facts. Raised voices belonged to closing doors; anger moved through houses until it found the person least capable of stopping it. At Privet Drive, peace had been something Harry maintained by becoming useful, quiet or absent.

He had chosen absent.

Water folded around a broken branch near the bank. Harry counted the ripples, following each one until it thinned and disappeared. Behind him, leaves shifted. He reached for his wand before turning, then lowered his hand as a large black dog emerged between the trees.

Padfoot stopped several feet away, ears lowered and something wrapped in a napkin held between his teeth.

"If that's another egg sandwich, I'm leaving."

The dog approached and placed the bundle on the rock. Harry opened it to find a slightly crushed slice of cake with blue icing along one edge.

"You hid cake before breakfast?"

Padfoot sat, looking remarkably untroubled by the accusation.

"That violates rule four."

The dog sneezed. Harry broke off a corner and ate it. The sponge tasted of lemon; Mrs Figg had been more successful than Sirius. Padfoot rested his head on Harry's knee, and Harry's fingers found the thick fur between his ears before he decided whether he wanted the contact. The dog leaned into his hand.

"You were arguing about me."

Padfoot whined.

"I know why."

The dog's eyes lifted towards him.

"That doesn't make it better."

Padfoot remained still. Harry ate another piece of cake, brushing loose crumbs from his fingers.

"You don't get any. Chocolate is poisonous."

The icing was blue, not chocolate, and Padfoot looked pointedly at it.

"You also burnt the pastries."

A paw landed on Harry's shoe.

"Emotional manipulation."

Padfoot's tail moved once through the grass. Slowly, the tremor left Harry's hands, and they sat beside the water until the cake was gone. The dog made no attempt to hurry him back towards the cottage.

Footsteps approached along the path. Remus appeared carrying two mugs, with a napkin-wrapped slice of cake balanced on top of one, and stopped at the edge of the shade.

"May I join you?"

Harry shrugged. Remus accepted that as permission and sat on the grass rather than the rock, placing one mug beside him. Steam rose from hot chocolate despite the warmth of the day. Padfoot remained against Harry's knee.

"We should not have argued in front of you," Remus said.

Harry watched the stream. "You don't need permission."

"No. But we do need judgement."

"Sirius was angry at Dumbledore."

"And frightened for you."

"Everyone keeps saying that as if one excuses the other."

Remus studied him. "It doesn't."

Harry lifted the mug. "Then why say it?"

"Because a reason isn't an excuse," Remus said. "But if we understand what caused the reaction, we have a chance of changing it without pretending it did no harm."

The chocolate was too hot to drink. Harry held the mug between both hands, feeling its warmth against his palms.

"Vernon gets angry because I exist."

Remus's face tightened.

"Sometimes," Harry continued, "Petunia says he's tired from work. Or worried about money. Like the reason means I should make it easier for him."

"You should not."

"I know."

Harry picked at a rough patch near the handle.

"I know now."

Padfoot's head grew heavier against his knee. Remus waited until Harry's fingers had stopped moving.

"When Sirius and I disagree," he said, "neither of us is required to leave. Anger does not end the relationship. It does not become your task to repair it."

"You say that now."

Remus nodded slowly. "Words are the beginning of proof, not the end."

Harry turned towards him. Remus did not appear offended, nor was he looking for an apology. He sat beneath the willow with his mug in one hand and waited for Harry to decide what to do with the answer.

"What proof?" Harry asked.

"We remain."

It was almost the same thing Harry had understood at the end of the previous year, but knowledge behaved differently when tested inside a home. He had found it easier to believe that people would remain through danger than through breakfast, badly chosen words and ordinary disappointment. Danger at least gave them a reason to stay close. He did not yet know how to trust the quieter occasions.

Padfoot raised his head. The transformation travelled through him in a ripple of dark fur and lengthening limbs, and a moment later Sirius sat on the grass with one hand still resting against Harry's knee.

"I'm sorry," he said.

Harry turned back to the stream. "You were right."

"That does not make the way I handled it useful."

"Remus was right too."

"A temporary administrative error."

Remus drank his chocolate. Sirius drew one knee towards his chest, his expression quieter than it had been in the study.

"You are not responsible for making the room comfortable whenever adults fail to control themselves."

"It wasn't that bad."

"You left your own house to escape it."

Harry looked at him. Sirius seemed to hear the words only after speaking them, and neither of them hurried past them. Your own house. The cottage stood beyond the orchard, hidden by leaves but present at Harry's back.

"I went into the garden," he said.

"Which is also yours."

"The stream?"

"Until the wards end."

"The hill?"

"Not yet."

"Then I'll go there next time."

Sirius's mouth twitched. Remus sighed.

"We have taught him the wrong lesson."

"A proud tradition."

The tension loosened. Harry took another drink; the hot chocolate had cooled enough not to burn.

"What did the examination find?" he asked.

Remus's attention sharpened.

"The curse in your scar is active."

Sirius went still.

"Wasn't it always?" Harry asked.

"Active does not mean worsening. It means the magic responded recently. I found no sign that anything passed into you or altered your thoughts."

Harry thought of Draconia beneath the diary's influence: her stillness, her pale face and the terrifying certainty that something else had been using her. Remus saw where his mind had gone.

"This is not possession."

Sirius's hand closed on the grass. "Then what is it?"

"A channel," Remus said. "Narrow, involuntary and dangerous primarily because we do not understand its limits."

"Can we close it?"

"Perhaps."

"How?"

"Dumbledore has suggested mental defensive training."

"For a fourteen-year-old?" Sirius asked.

"He has survived worse at eleven, twelve and thirteen."

"That is not an argument for adding difficulty."

"I agree."

Harry studied them both. "What training?"

"Occlumency," Remus said. "It teaches the mind to resist intrusion and regulate what rises under magical pressure."

"Can you teach it?"

"Not properly."

"Sirius?"

Sirius shook his head. "My family considered privacy a character flaw."

"Snape?"

The name arrived because Harry could think of no one more private. Both men reacted: Remus considered it, while Sirius looked as though the stream had offered him a dead rat.

"He is skilled," Remus admitted.

"No," Sirius said.

"You said I'm not responsible for adult arguments."

"Correct."

"Then you can have that one with Dumbledore."

Remus hid a smile. Sirius pointed at him.

"This is your influence."

"Harry produced sarcasm independently."

"James," Sirius said darkly.

The name no longer brought only grief. Harry looked down at the water, where his reflection broke around the stones and drew itself together farther along the bank.

"What was he like on birthdays?"

Sirius's finger lowered. "Your father?"

"You said he thought fourteen made him an adult."

"He thought every birthday made him an adult. At fifteen, he announced he had reached his intellectual majority and attempted to take over the Quidditch team's training schedule."

"He wasn't captain."

"That was the central objection."

Remus settled more comfortably against the tree, his shoulders easing as Sirius turned towards Harry.

"At sixteen," Sirius continued, "James decided birthday presents were childish and asked us not to buy any."

"He spent breakfast staring at the owl post," Remus said.

"By lunch, he accused us of emotional neglect."

"You bought something."

"We had gifts hidden beneath his bed."

"What did you get him?"

Remus's expression grew distant with memory. "A set of enchanted goal hoops for the dormitory."

"McGonagall confiscated them within an hour," Sirius added.

"Because you used Peter as the Quaffle."

"He volunteered."

"He believed you were describing a passing drill."

Harry laughed. The picture was so easy to imagine that his next question followed before he had considered how it might sound.

"What about my birthdays?"

Both men quietened. Harry almost withdrew the question, but Sirius picked a blade of grass and folded it between his fingers, searching through a memory rather than for a way to avoid it.

"Your first birthday is the only one I remember," he said. "You spent most of it attempting to eat the wrapping paper."

"James gave you a toy broom," Remus said.

"Lily said you were too young. James said talent required early encouragement."

"He charmed it to hover six inches above the floor."

"Seven," Sirius corrected. "Safety mattered."

Harry imagined the cottage in Godric's Hollow, warm with late-summer light. His father knelt beside a tiny broom, Lily stood close enough to catch him, and Sirius laughed somewhere behind the camera. The familiar photographs offered pieces of the scene; their voices supplied the rest.

"Did I fly?"

"Directly into a chair."

Remus smiled into his mug.

"You blamed the broom," Sirius continued. "Your vocabulary was limited, but the accusation was clear."

"What did Mum get me?"

"A stuffed stag," Remus said. "You dragged it by one leg everywhere."

"Padfoot ate an antler," Sirius added.

Harry stared at him. "You ate my toy?"

"Padfoot was young."

"You were twenty-one."

"Very young."

"Did she know?"

"Your mother knew everything. She made me sew it back."

"Could you?"

"No. The stag spent the remainder of its life with one antler attached backwards."

Harry laughed again. The image formed without effort: Lily placing a needle in Sirius's hand, James making matters worse, Remus arriving after the damage and pretending he had nothing to do with it. Harry did not know whether every detail he imagined was true, but the story gave movement to people he had known mostly through photographs and death. For a little while, he could picture them occupied with an ordinary afternoon.

"I wish I remembered," he said.

Sirius's expression softened. "We'll remember for you."

The words settled beside the stream. Harry looked at both their faces, allowing himself to consider what else they might be able to give him.

"Even the embarrassing things?"

"Especially those."

"Then I need stories about you."

Remus stood immediately. "I should check the kettle."

Sirius caught the hem of his robe.

"Sit down, Moony."

"The kettle may be dangerous."

"Harry deserves history."

Remus looked towards the cottage as though calculating whether dignity could survive the distance. Harry smiled, and after a moment Remus sat down again.

For the next hour, the old willow held every version of them.

Sirius told the story of Remus falling asleep in the library and waking with backwards notes copied across three feet of parchment. Remus described Sirius attempting to cut his own hair at fourteen after Walburga declared it too long; the result had required a hat until Christmas. They spoke of harmless disasters, confiscated sweets and a summer afternoon beside a river when James swore he could teach all four of them to dive.

They did not pretend their youth had been entirely happy. Some stories ended before the full moon; others curved around families, war or Peter's name. Yet for once, the past did not enter the conversation only as tragedy. Remus corrected Sirius, Sirius objected to the corrections, and Harry listened until the people in the photographs became untidy, loud and alive.

At some point, Sirius transformed again. Padfoot stole Remus's remaining cake and ran.

Remus shouted, and Harry gave chase. They tore through the orchard, ducking beneath branches and leaping over exposed roots. Padfoot slowed whenever Harry fell behind, then bounded away the moment he came close enough to reach him. Harry threw a windfallen apple; it missed the dog and struck Sirius's abandoned coffee cup, sending it into the grass.

Remus summoned the cake with his wand. Padfoot changed direction so quickly that his back legs slid beneath him. He rolled once, regained his feet and collided with Harry, sending them down together in a tangle of limbs and fur.

Harry landed in the grass with a black dog across his chest and Remus laughing hard enough to lower his wand. Padfoot licked the side of Harry's face.

"Disgusting," Harry said, pushing at the enormous head. "You eat floor eggs."

Padfoot sneezed into his hair. Harry laughed until he could not breathe, his hands caught in the dog's fur and grass tickling the back of his neck.

For a few seconds, he forgot to measure it. No part of him waited for the sound to end.

They returned to the cottage through the back door. Harry's trainers left mud across the kitchen floor, and he stopped as soon as he saw the marks. The apology reached his mouth before he had properly thought about the mess.

Sirius entered behind him in human form, equally muddy and still carrying the stolen napkin between his teeth. Harry stared. Sirius removed it.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Remus flicked his wand, and the mud vanished from the flagstones. No one shouted. Harry stepped fully inside.

An owl waited on the kitchen table, a large tawny bird with a narrow silver tube secured to one leg. The seal pressed into the wax showed a wand laid across three stars. Sirius's laughter disappeared.

"Dumbledore," Remus said.

Harry approached, and the owl extended its leg to him rather than either adult. He removed the message and unrolled a parchment containing only a few lines.

Dear Harry,

Sirius and Professor Lupin have informed me of your dreams and the pain in your scar. I believe you were right to report both. The possible presence of Nott is concerning, though the house and victim remain unidentified. Please continue recording anything you remember. I will visit tomorrow afternoon so that we may discuss measures which could make future dreams less distressing.

For today, I hope you permit yourself to be fourteen.

Albus Dumbledore

Harry read the letter again, searching for something more definite between its careful lines.

"He doesn't know," he said.

"Not yet," Remus replied.

"He always knows."

"No," Sirius said. "He often knows more than he shares. That is different."

The distinction did not reassure Harry. Before he could decide what to ask next, another sound came from the front of the house. This was no single clear note announcing someone at the boundary. The wards chimed repeatedly, their tones overlapping until the cottage seemed surrounded by distant bells.

Sirius looked at the clock. It was almost noon.

"That," he said, "is probably everyone."

Harry folded Dumbledore's letter.

"Everyone?"

Remus examined the ceiling. Sirius discovered an urgent interest in the ruined pastries. Harry stared at them both.

"What did you do?"

Beyond the garden, the front gate opened, and voices carried along the drive. Hermione's reached the house first.

"No, Mr Malfoy, the banner goes inside."

Lucius answered too quietly for Harry to hear. Draconia made no such effort to keep her voice down, and Hermione's next objection travelled perfectly well.

"It says Happy Birthday. Who would they be announcing it to outside?"

"The wards," Draconia said. "They have been more cooperative than most of the organisers."

Sirius edged towards the front hall. Harry caught the back of his jacket.

"You said we were taking a photograph."

"We are."

"You did not mention an audience."

"Technical omission."

"You invited the Malfoys?"

"Narcissa controlled the food."

"The Grangers?"

"Hermione controlled everything else."

"Who is everyone?"

Sirius considered the safest answer, but three precise knocks shook the front door before he could supply it. Someone tried the handle.

"Happy birthday!" a voice shouted through the wood.

Harry recognised Hagrid. Something enormous struck the porch beam, and dust fell from the ceiling. Sirius winced.

"Structurally adventurous," he reminded Harry.

Upstairs, the narrow fifth door burst open. Dozens of enchanted paper birds escaped onto the landing and flew down the staircase in a flood of red, gold, green and silver, each carrying a loop of ribbon in its beak. Three balloons rolled behind them, followed by a box of candles and a banner that had apparently ignored Hermione's instructions.

It unfurled across the sitting room window.

HAPPY FOURTEENTH, HARRY

One end immediately fell down.

Harry stood amid wings, ribbon and sunlight while his family argued on the other side of the door. For the second time that morning, he forgot to be afraid of wanting something. He looked at Sirius.

"You panicked."

Sirius smiled.

"Immensely."
