Prologue III

By the middle of November, Harry had begun to wish he could play his first Quidditch match before anybody heard about it.

A second-year Hufflepuff wished him luck on the stairs. Harry thanked him, then spent the next two flights wondering how someone he had never met knew which position he played. By lunchtime, he understood: apparently, everyone knew. A Ravenclaw he had never met asked whether it was true that he had caught a Remembrall after diving fifty feet. The distance increased to seventy by lunchtime. At dinner, Harry overheard two Gryffindors claiming he had stood upon his broom while doing it.

"I wouldn't try that," Hermione said.

Harry tore his bread in half. "I wasn't planning to."

"You looked interested."

"I was wondering how they think I did it."

"People prefer an impressive story to an accurate one."

Across the Hall, a small paper bird lifted from the Slytherin table. It circled a floating candle, narrowly avoided an owl and landed in Harry's bowl of potatoes. Hermione closed her eyes as gravy seeped into one wing. Harry rescued the bird and unfolded it over his plate.

Apparently you flew upside down.

He borrowed Hermione's quill.

I was waiting for you to notice. Try not to feel too outdone.

Draconia's reply arrived almost immediately.

I knew it. Arrogance.

Harry grinned. Hermione leaned across the table. "What did she say?"

He showed her.

"She started the rumour," Hermione said.

"What?"

A third note struck Harry's plate.

Greengrass asked whether it was true. I may have declined to correct her.

Harry looked across the Hall. Draconia raised her goblet in a solemn little toast, which made it much harder to convey how offended he intended to be.

Smiling came more easily to her since Halloween. She still tried to conceal it when other people were watching, but Harry caught it during arguments she enjoyed and whenever he made a mistake she considered particularly entertaining. Hermione could produce it too, usually by contradicting her so thoroughly that Draconia forgot to be offended and leaned closer to read the evidence. The three of them had stopped treating those moments as surprises.

Draconia no longer pretended their meetings occurred by chance. She sat with them in the library and began unpacking before checking who might see. Hermione carried spare green parchment because Draconia disliked beige, although she had explained at length that colour did nothing for the quality of an essay. Harry knew better than to ask either of them why they continued carrying it.

They were not left entirely alone. Older Slytherins still watched. Gryffindors whispered. Yet the questions had become less direct after three first-years defeated a troll together. It was difficult to claim their friendship was imaginary when everyone had heard how the three of them had been found together.

The story had changed in the telling, naturally. In one version, Draconia had transfigured the troll's club into a snake. In another, Hermione had discovered an obscure weakness in troll anatomy and instructed Harry where to strike. According to Fred and George Weasley, Harry had challenged it to a duel and won because the troll had forgotten to bring a second.

Harry preferred the version the three of them remembered: the club scraping stone, Hermione's sleeve pressed against his, Draconia trying to hold her bag shut around Trevor. It was frightening enough without turning any of them into somebody else.

His first Quidditch match arrived on a morning bright with winter sunlight. Frost silvered the lawns, and the air had the clean, sharp quality of glass. From the Great Hall windows, Harry could see students making their way towards the pitch in scarves and thick cloaks. He could also see that Hermione was not eating. She had spread a hand-drawn diagram beside her plate, complete with little figures charmed to move along pencilled routes. Her toast had gone cold while she followed one of them with her finger. Harry watched a tiny Chaser collide with a margin note and begin again.

"You're not playing," Harry said.

"I know."

"Then why do you look more worried than I am?"

"I don't."

Harry looked at her untouched toast. Hermione folded the diagram.

"You haven't eaten enough either."

"I have eggs."

"You moved them around your plate."

"I ate some."

"You ate half of one."

Harry glanced towards Slytherin. Draconia's usual place was empty. He looked back at his eggs before Hermione could follow his gaze.

He had seen her at one of his practices, despite her description of Quidditch as "a loud method of losing teeth." Afterwards, Snape had assigned her an evening of inventorying ingredients for missing a Slytherin study session. She insisted the punishment had nothing to do with watching Gryffindor.

Harry had not asked whether she would come today. He had assumed she would, and now regretted leaving himself no polite way to find out.

"Looking for someone?"

Hermione's tone was too casual.

"No."

A plate settled beside his with a firm click. Harry looked up. Draconia wore a dark green cloak fastened with silver at the throat, her scarf wrapped twice around her neck. Her cheeks were pink from the cold, and several pale strands had escaped her braid. He smiled before remembering that he had just told Hermione he was not looking for anybody.

"You're sitting at the Gryffindor table," Hermione said.

Draconia took the empty place beside him. "Very observant, Granger."

"You've made rather a point of doing it today."

"Then you should speak quietly."

Harry glanced along the table. Several Gryffindors were watching. Fred Weasley gave him an enthusiastic thumbs-up before George pulled him back into an argument about whether toast could be used to predict match scores.

Harry pushed the bowl of porridge towards Draconia. "You came."

"Clearly."

"I was worried you weren't going to come."

"I had to avoid three prefects and a particularly committed fifth-year."

"You could have used the paper birds."

"They are less effective against prefects."

Hermione reopened her diagram. "I've marked the standard movement patterns for both teams. Slytherin favours aggressive formations around the scoring area, but Gryffindor Chasers are faster along the outside."

Draconia examined it. "You have Bell positioned incorrectly."

Hermione frowned. "No, I don't."

"She flies farther forward when Johnson has the Quaffle."

"I watched the last three practices."

"So did I."

Harry looked between them. "You both watched three practices?"

Hermione adjusted the diagram. "It seemed sensible."

"I was determining whether Wood's strategy was as repetitive as it appeared."

"Was it?"

"Yes."

Hermione moved Bell's figure forward. It completed a neat little pass instead of colliding with the margin. Draconia pretended not to notice and took a piece of Harry's toast.

"You said I hadn't eaten enough."

"I'm helping you avoid excess."

Harry reached for it. Draconia held it out of reach. Hermione pushed another slice onto his plate without looking up.

"Thank you," Harry said.

"You're welcome."

Draconia bit into the stolen toast. Harry took a mouthful of eggs while the girls resumed arguing over the diagram. They were both here. For a few minutes, he could listen to them instead of thinking about all the people who expected him to catch something they would barely be able to see.

Outside, the stands were filling. Red-and-gold banners snapped in the wind above green scarves, and students hurried past with their hands buried in their pockets. Beneath the stands, the changing room smelled of damp timber and broom polish. Harry fastened his gloves while Oliver Wood delivered instructions as though the result might decide the future of the school. His Nimbus lay within reach. He had flown it often enough in practice to know what the handle felt like beneath his hands. Today he kept checking that it was still there.

Wood's instructions barely registered. He checked the fastening of his gloves twice. When they stepped onto the pitch, sound rose around him in a single enormous wave. For an instant, he was back in Diagon Alley with strangers reaching for his hand. There were too many faces, too many voices and too much expectation gathered around his name. His broom felt very small beneath his fingers.

Then he found Hermione and Draconia near the front of the stands, one wrapped in red and gold, the other in green. Between them stretched a banner.

DON'T CRASH, POTTER.

Hermione's face was bright red. Draconia held her end high, apparently delighted with the advice. Harry laughed, and the tightness across his chest eased enough for him to draw a proper breath.

Madam Hooch blew her whistle. He mounted his broom, kicked away from the ground and left the noise below. In the air, everything became simple. The pitch opened beneath him in lines and movement. Chasers passed the Quaffle through gaps that appeared and vanished in moments. Bludgers cut across the formation with murderous intent. Harry climbed above it all, turning slowly as he searched for gold. The cold bit through his gloves. Wind pressed tears from the corners of his eyes. He did not care. For the first time since learning that everybody expected something from Harry Potter, expectation felt light enough to carry.

Gryffindor scored. The stands erupted.

Harry heard Lee Jordan's commentary tumbling across the pitch, followed by Professor McGonagall rebuking him for describing a Slytherin Chaser as "a human battering ram with suspicious elbows."

A flicker of gold appeared near the goal hoops. Harry leaned forward, watching the gap between two Chasers. The Snitch vanished before he could turn towards it. He circled, scanning the same patch of air, and felt the broom jerk beneath him. At first, he thought he had met a gust. He tightened his knees and corrected, as Wood had taught him.

Suddenly the broom threw itself sideways. Harry nearly fell. A gasp moved through the stands. He flattened himself against the handle as it bucked again, twisting with enough force to wrench one hand free. The world tipped beneath him. Green grass became sky; the stands swung sickeningly across his vision. He pressed the handle down. The broom fought the movement and twisted harder, ignoring every instruction his body gave it. Harry locked both hands around the wood. There was no longer any question of finding the Snitch. He was trying to stay on.

Hermione was on her feet before she understood what she intended to do.

"That isn't possible."

Beside her, Draconia had gone completely still. Harry swung beneath the broom. From the stands, Hermione could see one hand gripping the handle and his legs kicking above empty air. Players climbed towards him, then swerved when the Nimbus lashed sideways. Their voices were lost beneath the crowd. She remembered how lightly his broom had risen into his hand at the flying lesson. It should obey him. It had obeyed him all through practice. Hermione pulled out her wand.

"What are you doing?" Draconia asked.

"Something is interfering with the broom."

"I can see that."

"Some interference spells have to be kept going. Whoever's doing this might be watching him and saying the incantation."

She had read about interference with enchanted objects after Harry's flying lesson; at the time, she had thought recognising it would be easy. Now hundreds of people were looking up, shouting, standing in front of one another. Her wand felt small and useless in her hand. Draconia caught her wrist. "There."

Professor Snape sat in the teachers' stand with his gaze fixed upon Harry. His lips moved continuously. Hermione's stomach dropped. "It's him."

Draconia did not answer.

"Draconia."

"I'm thinking."

"What is there to think about?"

"Snape has been watching Potter since the match began."

"That supports it."

"No. He watches Potter during breakfast."

Above them, the broom convulsed. Harry swung outward, one hand slipping down the polished wood. Hermione stopped listening. She pushed through the row, stepping on feet and ignoring angry cries. Draconia followed.

"What are you planning?"

"If I break his concentration, the jinx will stop."

"How?"

Hermione reached the end of the row and began climbing towards the teachers' stand. "I haven't decided."

"That is usually Potter's part of the process."

Hermione caught her shoe in a trailing scarf and nearly fell. Draconia hauled her upright without stopping. They reached the row behind the professors, where Snape was still muttering.

A few seats away, Quirrell leaned forward with both hands around the railing, his face pale beneath his turban. Draconia looked towards him. Hermione barely noticed; above the pitch, Harry's hand was slipping. She pointed her wand at the hem of Snape's robes.

"Lacarnum Inflamari."

Blue flame caught at the hem. Snape recoiled, striking Quirrell with his shoulder and knocking him sideways against the railing. Several professors rose at once. Draconia pulled Hermione behind a taller student before anyone turned towards their wands. Above the pitch, Harry's broom steadied. Hermione exhaled so sharply that her chest hurt. "It worked."

Draconia was staring at Quirrell.

"What?" Hermione asked.

Draconia did not answer. The roar of the crowd drew both their attention upwards. Harry had climbed back onto his broom. A glint of gold passed near his shoulder.

He dived.

The Nimbus obeyed him again. Harry hardly trusted the sudden steadiness, but the Snitch was there, dropping towards the grass. He followed, one shoulder aching and his fingers stiff from clinging to the handle.

Gold flashed in front of his face. He reached, missed, opened his mouth for breath—

Something struck his lips. Harry pulled up too late and hit the ground hard enough to drive the air from his lungs. For several seconds, he lay curled on the grass with both hands pressed to his mouth. Wood landed beside him, shouting his name. Something fluttered against Harry's tongue. He coughed. A small golden ball dropped into his palm. Its wings opened weakly between his fingers. The whistle shrieked.

"Gryffindor wins!"

His teammates descended around him. Harry was pulled upright, struck on the back and embraced by several people at once. Someone lifted him from his feet. Fred and George had landed beside Wood and were already singing a song that rhymed Potter with words he suspected they had invented.

Madam Hooch broke through the celebration and made everyone give him room. She checked his shoulder, told Wood to send him to Madam Pomfrey and took the Nimbus for examination. Professor Flitwick joined her, running his wand along the handle. Harry watched for a moment, then lost sight of the broom among the people pressing closer.

He searched through the bodies. Hermione reached him first. She pushed between two older Gryffindors and seized the front of his robes.

"Are you hurt?"

"I don't think so."

"You don't think so?"

"My shoulder hurts."

"Which one?"

"The one you're holding."

Draconia arrived half a second later. She did not ask whether he was all right. Her eyes moved over him instead—face, hands, shoulders—as though conducting an inventory. "You swallowed it," she said.

Harry looked at the Snitch. "Only a bit."

"That is revolting."

"It still counts."

"I wasn't questioning the score."

Hermione drew them away from the celebrating team.

"Your broom was jinxed."

Harry's remaining excitement vanished. "What?"

"It wasn't malfunctioning," she said. "Someone was maintaining a spell."

"Who?"

"Snape."

Draconia's jaw tightened.

Hermione turned to her. "We watched him."

"We watched him speaking."

"While staring at Harry."

"So was Quirrell."

Hermione blinked. "Quirrell?"

"He was muttering too."

"You didn't say that."

"I was occupied preventing you from falling into the teachers' stand."

Harry glanced towards the teachers' stand. Snape was checking the blackened hem of his robes while McGonagall spoke sharply to him. Quirrell had gone. From here, Harry could not hear McGonagall or Snape.

"You set Snape on fire?" Harry asked.

"Only his robes."

Draconia's mouth curved. "It was surprisingly effective."

Hermione looked almost proud before remembering the seriousness of the situation.

"We need to tell Professor McGonagall."

"With what evidence?" Draconia asked.

"We saw him."

"You saw his lips moving. You assumed the rest."

"The broom stopped when his concentration broke."

"So did Quirrell's."

Hermione opened her mouth, then closed it. Harry looked towards the stand again. Snape had disliked him from their first lesson. It was easy to fit that face to the memory of the broom twisting beneath him. Quirrell was much harder to picture doing it. Draconia kept glancing at the place Quirrell had occupied. For once, she did not seem to have an answer ready.

"You don't think it was Snape," he said.

"I don't know."

Hermione folded her arms. "You always defend him."

Draconia's head turned. "No, I don't."

"He favours Slytherin."

"That does not make him incapable of being unpleasant."

"He singled Harry out."

"Yes."

"He took points from us for no reason."

"I was there."

"Then why won't you admit it?"

"Because disliking someone is not proof that they attempted murder."

The word fell heavily among them. Around the trio, Gryffindor continued celebrating. Their banner had torn loose at one end and now flapped above the stands:

DON'T CRA—

Harry looked down at the Snitch in his hand. "I believe Hermione," he said.

Draconia became very still.

"But I believe you saw Quirrell too," he added. "So we find out which one it was."

Hermione's indignation eased. Draconia examined him for a moment. "That is irritatingly reasonable."

"I've been practising."

Hermione glanced towards the castle. "We still ought to tell someone."

"And explain that you set a professor on fire?" Draconia asked.

Hermione looked stricken. Harry closed his fingers around the Snitch.

"They've taken the broom. They'll look at it."

"That won't tell them who was casting the spell," Hermione said.

"No," Draconia agreed.

Harry looked from one girl to the other. "Then we find out more before we accuse anyone."

Hermione did not look satisfied. She walked with him to the hospital wing anyway and made certain he told Madam Pomfrey about the fall as well as the shoulder.

A few evenings later, the library had been quiet for so long that Harry could hear a photograph grumbling three tables away. A page turned somewhere beyond the shelves. Rain tapped against the tall windows in uneven bursts, driven against the glass whenever the wind strengthened around the castle. The lamps upon the long tables had burned lower as evening passed, their circles of amber light shrinking across abandoned books and empty chairs.

Almost everyone else had gone. Harry sat with his elbows on the table, surrounded by old copies of the Daily Prophet. Pages rustled without being touched, photographs muttered beneath folded corners and an advertisement for self-polishing cauldrons had been flashing at him for ten minutes. He pressed it beneath a book and enjoyed several seconds of peace.

Across from him, Hermione read with increasing speed. A small stack of rejected newspapers had formed beside her chair, arranged by date despite the fact that they were all going back into the same archive cabinet.

Draconia sat at the end of the table. Six articles mentioning Lucius Malfoy lay beside her elbow. Each time she found another, she read it in silence and moved it beyond Harry's reach, which made it considerably harder to concentrate on the subjects they had come to investigate.

"What does that one say?" he asked.

"Nothing relevant."

"You said that about the last five."

"They were also irrelevant."

Harry reached for the top newspaper. Draconia moved it away without looking up.

"That makes me think they're interesting."

"They concern Ministry committees."

"What sort of committees?"

"The tedious sort."

Hermione continued reading. "Lucius Malfoy is mentioned in the index under legislation, charitable donations and disciplinary inquiries."

Draconia looked at her sharply.

Hermione turned a page. "The archive has a subject index."

"You searched for my father?"

"I searched for Snape, Quirrell, Gringotts, broom interference, jinxes used in sporting events and every person connected to those subjects."

"Efficient," Harry said.

"Nosy," Draconia corrected.

Hermione finally lifted her gaze. "We are investigating attempted murder. Being selective about which names I search would be irresponsible."

"My father did not jinx Potter's broom."

"Then you have nothing to worry about."

Draconia's expression suggested she had several things to worry about, beginning with Hermione. Harry pulled another newspaper from the stack. They had been searching since dinner. Harry's broom had been returned after Flitwick's examination, and McGonagall had told him to report any further trouble immediately. There was no lasting fault in its enchantments. Nobody had yet told him why it had tried to throw him off. That left far too much room for thinking whenever he climbed onto it.

The first hour produced accounts of illegally enchanted racing brooms, a Keeper whose gloves had tried to strangle him and a correspondence course promising to identify curses through dreams. The second produced nothing useful. Harry unfolded a paper from the beginning of August and noticed the date mentioned beneath its main headline. The thirty-first of July. His birthday.

He had picked it up because the front page showed a photograph of several Ministry officials entering Gringotts. One goblin stood upon the bank's steps, blinking furiously as camera flashes burst around him. Harry began reading.

BREAK-IN AT GRINGOTTS

An attempted robbery took place at Gringotts Wizarding Bank on 31 July. The break-in occurred at an unspecified time and targeted a high-security vault believed to have been emptied earlier that day.

He sat straighter. "Hermione."

She did not immediately respond.

"Hermione."

Something in his voice made both girls look up. Harry turned the paper towards them. Hermione moved around the table rather than attempting to read it upside down. Draconia brought her chair closer. Their shoulders pressed against Harry's as the three bent over the article.

"It doesn't say what was inside," Hermione murmured.

"It was empty," Draconia said.

"It could be the one Hagrid emptied."

Hermione looked at Harry. "What?"

Harry read the line again, making sure he had not misunderstood.

"The day we went to Diagon Alley, Hagrid took me into Gringotts. We went to my vault first, and then another one. Seven hundred and thirteen."

"What did he take?" Draconia asked.

"I don't know. It was wrapped in paper."

"How large?"

Harry held his hands apart, trying to remember. "About this big. Maybe smaller."

Hermione pulled the paper closer. "The robbery happened on your birthday too."

"After Hagrid emptied the vault," Harry said.

The dates made the back of Harry's neck prickle. Hagrid had called the parcel Hogwarts business and tucked it away much more carefully than their purchases. If the thief had entered that vault, the thing they wanted might already have been here. Hermione touched the column with one finger. "It doesn't give the vault number."

"It was a high-security vault," Harry said.

"That doesn't mean it was the only one."

"Then we ask Hagrid."

Instead of arguing, Draconia read the article's final paragraph again. "It says no one knows how the thief entered or escaped."

"Gringotts is supposed to be impossible to rob," Harry said.

"No place is impossible to enter," Draconia replied. "Some are merely expensive to leave."

Hermione looked at her.

"What?"

"That sounded rehearsed."

"My father says it."

"Why?"

Draconia folded the article. "If I knew, I doubt I'd be permitted to tell you."

Hermione opened her mouth, but Madam Pince appeared at the end of the table before she could ask anything else. Harry caught the lamp as his elbow struck it. The librarian watched him steady it with an expression which suggested the lamp had better have survived.

"The library closed ten minutes ago."

Hermione looked towards the clock. "That can't be right."

The hands showed twenty minutes to curfew. Madam Pince's nostrils narrowed.

"Are you disputing the library's clock?"

"No."

"Are you intending to spend the night?"

"No, Madam Pince."

"Then leave."

They packed quickly. Hermione returned the newspapers to the archive cabinet while Madam Pince watched every movement. Draconia attempted to take the article about Gringotts with her. The librarian removed it from beneath her book without being asked.

When the heavy library doors closed behind them, the corridor was empty. Rain trembled against the nearby windows. The torches had been lowered for the night, leaving long stretches of stone in soft shadow. Hermione checked the time again. "We have eighteen minutes."

"That is more than enough," Harry said.

"For you to reach Gryffindor Tower, perhaps. Draconia still has to return to the dungeons."

"I know where my common room is," Draconia replied.

"That isn't the concern."

"You are concerned about me?"

Hermione began walking. "I'm concerned about losing House points."

"Of course."

They reached the first staircase just as it began to move. Hermione stopped on the bottom step. "No."

The stairs continued turning away from the second-floor landing, apparently unimpressed by her objection.

Harry stepped onto it. "We don't have time to wait."

"It's going the wrong way."

"It'll still go somewhere."

"That is precisely the problem."

Draconia followed Harry. Hermione looked between the moving stairs and the corridor behind them, muttered something about preventable disasters and stepped on just before the lower platform moved beyond reach. The staircase carried them upwards. It passed the second floor, turned again and joined a landing none of them recognised. Harry looked towards Hermione.

She opened Hogwarts: A History and consulted the map she had been adding to since September.

"Third floor," she said.

Draconia glanced along the corridor. "The forbidden one?"

"The third-floor corridor on the right-hand side is forbidden," Hermione replied.

All three slowly looked to the right. A long passage stretched away from them. Its torches had not been lit.

"No," Hermione said.

"We haven't moved," Harry pointed out.

"We are going left."

They turned left. A sharp cackle sounded ahead. Peeves emerged upside down through the ceiling, wearing three stolen neckties around his head. His eyes brightened when he saw them.

"Students out late," he sang. "Students lose points—"

Draconia raised her wand. Peeves blew a raspberry.

"Malfoy caught with Gryffindors! Wait till the snakes hear—"

"Quiet," Hermione hissed.

"FILCH!" Peeves shrieked. "STUDENTS ON THE THIRD FLOOR! NAUGHTY LITTLE FIRSTIES—"

Harry grabbed Hermione's hand.

"Run."

They sprinted back the way they had come. Peeves followed above them, shouting their location to the castle. At the staircase, they found only empty air. It had already moved to another floor. A door opened behind them. Lamplight spilled into the passage they had just left, and Mrs Norris appeared around the corner, her yellow eyes fixed on them. Filch's voice followed. The missing staircase lay at Harry's back; the unlit corridor stretched away to his right.

"What is it, my sweet? Who's there?"

Draconia seized the nearest handle in the right-hand corridor. It did not turn. Hermione drew her wand, looking once towards Filch's approaching light.

"Alohomora."

The lock clicked. Hermione had looked up opening and locking charms after Halloween, when the storeroom door had refused to move. Harry had practised them with her. Until now, he had thought the lessons were mostly an excuse to discuss the troll without admitting they wanted to.

"You said we shouldn't use that casually," he whispered.

"This is not casual."

Draconia opened the door and shoved them through. Harry pulled it closed just as Mrs Norris rounded the corner. They stood in darkness. Nobody breathed. Filch's uneven footsteps approached. Light passed beneath the door and stopped. Harry could hear Mrs Norris scratching at the other side.

"Students," Filch muttered. "I know you're here."

The door handle moved. Hermione pointed her wand at the lock and whispered the locking charm. The handle became still. Filch tried again. Harry's heart hammered painfully against his ribs. He became aware of Hermione's hand still trapped in his and released it. Draconia stood close enough that the edge of her sleeve brushed his other arm. Keys rattled on the other side. Harry pressed one hand against the door, as though that could help. Mrs Norris meowed.

"DOWN HERE, FILCH!" Peeves shrieked from farther along the corridor. "QUICK, QUICK—"

The keys stopped rattling.

"Come on, my sweet," Filch muttered. "We'll catch them below."

His footsteps followed Peeves's delighted cackling. Harry waited until the light beneath the door disappeared, then let out his breath. Hermione quietly undid the locking charm so they could leave.

"That," Hermione whispered, "was entirely avoidable."

"Potter chose the staircase," Draconia replied.

"It was the only available route."

"You also opened the door."

"The alternative was detention."

"You make an exciting accomplice, Granger."

"I am not your accomplice."

Something wet struck the floor behind them. The sound was thick and heavy. All three became still. A smell had begun to fill the darkness—hot animal breath, filthy fur and meat left too long in the sun. Harry felt Hermione's fingers close around his sleeve.

"Draconia," he whispered.

"I smell it."

Another wet slap landed behind them. Slowly, Harry raised his wand.

"Lumos."

Light bloomed at his wand tip. At first, he saw paws wider than his chest, their claws pressed into the cracks between flagstones. Thick black fur rose above them. Harry lifted the wand, reluctant to find out how far he would have to lift it. One enormous head emerged from the darkness. Then a second. Then a third.

Six eyes blinked against the light. The middle head lowered until its nose was level with Harry's face. A growl rolled through the room. Harry felt it in the floor, in the door at his back and in the bones of the hand holding his wand. Hermione's grip tightened. Draconia spoke without moving her mouth. "Do not run."

The head on the left sniffed. Its breath blew Harry's fringe from his forehead and coated his glasses in a fine mist. Harry wished she had suggested something besides standing still.

"Back towards the door," Draconia whispered.

They moved together. One step. The dog's centre head tracked them. Another. Its lips drew back. Teeth longer than Harry's hand gleamed in the wandlight. Hermione's heel struck the door. The dog let out a loud, deep bark. The sound hit them like a physical force. All three abandoned subtlety. Hermione dragged at the handle while Draconia fired a burst of red sparks towards the ceiling. The dog recoiled from the light, three heads snapping upwards. As the heads lifted, Harry saw a square of wood beneath their paws, with an iron ring set in its centre. A trapdoor.

Then the right-hand head lunged. Hermione wrenched the door open. Draconia caught Harry between the shoulders and shoved him through, teeth snapping shut in the space his robes had occupied. Harry slammed the iron-bound door behind them. Hermione jabbed her wand at the lock. It clicked just before the dog struck the other side. The timber jumped beneath Harry's hands. The handle rattled, and three barks crashed together behind it, loud enough for him to feel them through his chest. He had no intention of waiting to discover how long the door would hold.

"Move!" Hermione cried.

They ran. Filch no longer mattered. Neither did Peeves, the curfew or whatever punishment waited if they were caught. Harry followed the pale shapes of Hermione and Draconia through the darkness, one hand skimming the wall whenever the corridor turned. Their shoes struck the stone in a wild, uneven rhythm.

Behind them came another impact. For one horrible moment, Harry thought the door had broken. He looked back as his foot caught the edge of a rug. Draconia seized his sleeve before he fell and dragged him onwards without slowing.

"Don't look back."

"I thought it got out."

"If it gets out, looking won't improve matters."

They reached the narrow staircase at the end of the corridor and plunged down it. Hermione took the first several steps too quickly, slipped and caught the railing with both hands. Harry almost collided with her. Draconia ran into him from behind, and for a few confused seconds all three became trapped against one another upon the turning stairs.

"Keep going," Hermione gasped.

"I'm trying."

"Then move."

"That's my shoulder," Draconia said.

Harry could not tell whose foot was standing upon his robe. He pulled himself free and stumbled down the remaining steps.

They ran on past the second-floor landing, around two corners and behind a tapestry depicting an unsuccessful medieval hunt. A painted huntsman began to object. Hermione put a finger to her lips, and he fell silent at the sight of her face. Only when the barking could no longer be heard did Harry realise he had stopped looking for a familiar route. Hermione leaned against the wall and bent forward, one hand pressed beneath her ribs. Her hair had fallen across her face. Draconia stood beside the tapestry with her palm flat against the stone, drawing slow breaths through her nose as though determined to make her body obey through sheer irritation.

Harry's glasses were still coated in a fine mist from the creature's breath. He removed them. The corridor softened into indistinct torchlight. For several seconds, nobody spoke. Harry could still see the dog without needing his glasses: black fur, three sets of eyes and teeth closing in the space where his head had been. Hermione recovered first.

"What," she demanded between breaths, "is a three-headed dog doing inside a school?"

Harry wiped his lenses upon the hem of his robe. His hands were shaking badly enough to leave new smears across the glass. "Guarding something."

"That does not make it better."

"I didn't say it did."

He put his glasses back on. Draconia was looking at his neck and shoulders.

"Did it catch you?"

Harry shook his head. She folded her arms, tucking her hands out of sight. "You saw it too?"

Harry nodded. "The trapdoor beneath its paws."

Hermione straightened. "A trapdoor?"

"An old one," Draconia said. "Iron ring in the centre. The dog was standing directly over it."

Hermione stared in the direction they had come from, although several walls now stood between them and the third-floor corridor.

"Perhaps it leads to a storage chamber."

"Protected by a three-headed dog?" Harry asked.

"Hogwarts contains dangerous magical objects. It might be a security measure."

"It is a security measure," Draconia said. "The question is what requires it."

Harry thought of the newspaper article: a high-security vault found empty when somebody broke into it on his birthday. It might have been another vault. He knew that. But Hagrid's parcel had been small enough to disappear into a pocket, and something important enough to hide in a bank would surely need protecting here too. Hermione watched his face. "No."

Harry blinked. "I didn't say anything."

"You made the face."

"What face?"

"The one you make before deciding something dangerous has become your responsibility."

Draconia considered him. "She's right."

"I hate that you've both named a face."

"It is very recognisable," Draconia said.

"This isn't my responsibility."

Hermione looked unconvinced.

Harry lowered his voice. "But Hagrid took something from Gringotts, and somebody broke into an emptied vault that same day. Now there's a trapdoor inside Hogwarts guarded by that."

"We do not know that the two things are connected," Hermione said.

"You think they are."

"I think it is possible."

"That means yes."

"No, it means possible. They are different words for a reason."

Draconia glanced around the empty corridor. "We should continue this somewhere that does not involve hiding behind a tapestry."

As though summoned by the warning, a distant door creaked open. The three became silent. Footsteps crossed the floor above them. Harry looked towards one end of the corridor. Hermione checked the other. No lamplight appeared, but the castle no longer felt empty.

"We have to separate," Hermione whispered. "Draconia can't come into Gryffindor Tower, and we can't follow her into the Slytherin common room."

The practical truth of it landed badly. Harry had forgotten they would have to go in opposite directions. The dog was locked away above them, but leaving either girl to walk alone still felt wrong. He looked at the empty corridor beyond the tapestry and tried to make himself imagine it containing nothing more dangerous than portraits.

"I can reach the dungeon stairs from here," Draconia said.

Harry frowned. "Do you know where we are?"

"Yes."

"Why didn't you say that?"

"You were both occupied."

Hermione pulled her map from her bag. The parchment trembled faintly between her fingers. Draconia noticed. Without comment, she placed one finger upon the map and traced a route.

"We are here. That staircase will take you towards the Grand Staircase. Avoid the corridor beside the trophy room—Filch checks it before midnight."

"How do you know?" Hermione asked.

"I know useful things."

"Draconia," Harry said.

She looked at him.

"Are you all right?"

"Obviously."

"You nearly got eaten."

"So did you."

"That wasn't the question."

Her composure held for a moment longer. Then she glanced towards the stone floor.

"No," she admitted. "Not particularly."

Hermione folded the map.

"Neither am I."

Harry released a breath. "Good."

Both girls stared at him.

"I mean—not good. Just—"

"You wanted to know if we were scared too," Hermione said.

Harry rubbed the back of his neck. "Maybe."

"You aren't the only one," Draconia told him.

Another door closed somewhere in the castle. They needed to move. Hermione stepped forward and, after the smallest hesitation, took Draconia's hand. Draconia looked down at their joined fingers in surprise.

"We'll walk with you as far as the dungeon stairs," Hermione said. "Then Harry and I will go back together."

"That is unnecessary."

"Yes."

"It is also the longer route."

"I know."

Draconia looked towards Harry. He shrugged.

"You won't win."

She appeared to consider arguing anyway. Instead, her fingers closed around Hermione's. They left the shelter of the tapestry.

The walk towards the dungeons took nearly ten minutes. They listened at corners and stopped whenever a portrait stirred in its frame. Harry went slightly ahead, though Hermione caught his robe twice before he could turn without checking. By the time they reached the lower stairs, curfew had passed. None of them suggested leaving Draconia to save a few more minutes. At the staircase descending below the Entrance Hall, they stopped. Greenish torchlight glimmered upon the lower walls. Draconia released Hermione's hand.

"Go straight back," she said.

Hermione nodded. "So should you."

Draconia turned to Harry. "Don't investigate anything before breakfast."

"I wasn't planning to."

"You rarely are."

Harry almost smiled. Then the memory of three enormous heads returned.

"Draconia."

She paused on the first step.

"If you hear anything behind you—"

"I won't turn around," she said.

The corner of her mouth lifted.

"I was listening."

She descended. Harry and Hermione remained at the top until her footsteps faded and a door opened somewhere below. Only then did Hermione unfold her map.

"She was frightened."

"So were we."

"Yes."

Hermione studied the empty staircase. "She still noticed the trapdoor."

Harry looked towards her. "So did I."

"That is also worrying."

The Fat Lady was still in her portrait when they reached Gryffindor Tower, though she made it clear they had been fortunate.

"I was about to visit my friend on the fifth floor. I do have an evening of my own, you know."

Hermione apologised and gave the password. Harry followed her through before the portrait could change its mind. The common room was dark. Embers glowed beneath ash in the fireplace. Someone had abandoned a half-finished goblet of water beside an armchair, and the wind pressed softly against the tall windows. Hermione stopped near the staircase leading to the girls' dormitory. "We should tell a professor."

Harry had known she would say it.

"Which one?"

"Professor McGonagall."

"And tell her what? That we unlocked a forbidden door and missed curfew?"

"That isn't the important part."

"It'll be important to her."

Hermione folded her arms. "A dangerous animal is guarding something beneath the school."

"She already knows. All the teachers must."

"That doesn't mean they know somebody tried to steal it from Gringotts."

Harry looked towards the dying fire. "We don't know that's what it's guarding."

Hermione watched him.

"Now you care about certainty?"

"I care about asking Hagrid first."

"Because you think he'll tell you."

"He might."

"He might also refuse because whatever he collected was clearly secret."

"Then we ask why he won't tell us."

Hermione sat upon the arm of the nearest chair. The fear had begun to leave her, replaced by the need to arrange what had happened into something she could understand.

"The article said the vault was emptied earlier that day."

"Hagrid emptied it."

"You saw him remove one parcel," Hermione corrected. "We don't know whether anything else was stored there."

"It looked empty."

"You stood outside the door."

"I could see."

Hermione pressed her lips together. Harry waited. "Fine," she said at last. "We ask Hagrid. Then we tell Professor McGonagall."

"If there's something to tell."

"There is already something to tell."

"We'll argue about that tomorrow."

Hermione gave him a tired look. "That is not agreement."

"It's nearly agreement."

"It isn't."

Harry started towards the boys' staircase.

"Goodnight, Hermione."

"Harry."

He looked back. She stood beside the chair, smaller than usual in the dark common room. "We were lucky," she said.

The truth of it sobered him. The dog could have reached him. The door could have failed. Any one of them could have slipped upon the stairs while the others continued running.

"I know."

"Promise me you won't go back alone."

Harry answered without hesitation.

"I won't."

Hermione searched his face, making sure he understood that she meant it. "Goodnight, Harry."

He climbed to the dormitory. Sleep did not come easily. Every time Harry began to drift, the dog lunged again. He heard its teeth close, felt its breath upon his face and woke with his hands twisted in the blankets. Around him, the other boys slept undisturbed. Neville snored softly. Somewhere beneath his bed, Trevor croaked.

Harry stared into the darkness. He kept returning to the moment his wand illuminated the floor. The dog had not wandered accidentally into the room. Its paws had been arranged around the trapdoor. It had been placed there deliberately, a living lock protecting whatever waited below.

Harry wanted Hagrid to explain it. The vault, the parcel, the dog: he kept fitting them together whenever he closed his eyes. What frightened him most was the thought of asking and seeing Hagrid pretend not to understand. He had trusted Hagrid since the night he had arrived on the rock. He did not want to discover that being at Hogwarts made that trust foolish.

Morning arrived cold and colourless. Harry had slept perhaps two hours. He dressed without waking the others and found Hermione already waiting in the common room. Shadows darkened the skin beneath her eyes. She held Hogwarts: A History against her chest but had not opened it.

"You didn't sleep," Harry said.

"Neither did you."

They made their way down together. Neither mentioned Draconia, but upon entering the Great Hall they both looked immediately towards the Slytherin table. Her seat was empty. Harry's stomach tightened. Then Draconia entered through the side doors, composed, neatly dressed and very much alive. She saw them watching. One eyebrow rose. Harry released a breath.

Hermione turned towards the breakfast table as though she had not been worried. Draconia joined them several minutes later beneath the pretence of retrieving a book Hermione had borrowed.

"You both look dreadful," she said.

Hermione stared at the faint shadows beneath Draconia's eyes. "So do you."

"I always look like this."

"No, you don't."

Draconia sat beside Harry without further argument. For a little while, the three of them ate in silence. The ordinary sounds of breakfast felt strange after the night before. Cutlery struck plates. Students complained about lessons. Hundreds of people occupied the same castle as the three-headed dog without knowing it existed only a few floors above them.

Harry kept glancing towards the staff table. Snape was speaking quietly to Professor McGonagall. Quirrell ate toast with one trembling hand. Dumbledore's chair stood empty.

"You're doing it again," Hermione murmured.

"What?"

"The face."

"I'm eating."

"With your fork halfway to your mouth."

Harry lowered it. A barn owl swept beneath the enchanted ceiling. It circled once above the Gryffindor table and dropped a folded piece of parchment directly into Harry's porridge. Draconia looked at the soggy edge. "That bird dislikes you."

Harry rescued the note. The writing was large, slanted and familiar.

Harry,

Come down ter the hut after lessons. Been a while. Bring Hermione and Draconia if they're free.

Hagrid

Nobody spoke.

Harry read it a second time. Hermione leaned closer. "Did he know we were in the library?"

"No."

"Could Filch have told him someone was on the third floor?"

"Filch didn't see us."

"Peeves did," Draconia said.

Harry looked towards the staff table again.

Hagrid's enormous chair was empty. "He might just want to see us," Harry said.

Neither girl looked convinced. Less than twelve hours after they found the trapdoor, the person who knew what had left Gringotts had invited them to tea. Harry could not decide whether that made the visit easier or worse.

Draconia examined both sides of the note. "He doesn't mention last night."

"He wouldn't put it in writing if he knew," Hermione said.

Harry wiped porridge from the corner of the parchment. Hagrid had signed it exactly as he signed every note. There was no warning hidden between the words, no indication that anything was wrong.

Still, the invitation felt different now. Harry folded it. "We'll go after lessons."

Hermione nodded. "And we ask him directly."

"Not immediately," Draconia said.

"We need answers."

"We'll receive more if he doesn't realise he's being questioned."

Hermione looked disapproving. Draconia returned the note to Harry. "You may disapprove after it works."

Harry slipped the parchment into his pocket. Above them, owls continued crossing the artificial sky.

The rain had stopped, leaving the grounds washed clean beneath a low grey sky. Water ran from the sloping lawns in silver channels. Their shoes sank into soft earth as they crossed towards the edge of the Forbidden Forest.

Smoke rose from Hagrid's chimney.

Fang announced them before Harry could knock. The boarhound barked, something fell over inside and Hagrid told him to get down. Harry recognised the sequence from their earlier visits. Usually, it made him smile before the door opened.

"There yeh are!"

Hagrid filled the doorway in a moleskin coat and a red shirt that looked as though it had been repaired several times. Fang forced his head around Hagrid's leg and immediately began slobbering upon Hermione's sleeve. "Come in, come in. Ground's no good fer standin' about."

Heat struck Harry's face as he stepped inside. A fire roared beneath an iron kettle, and his glasses clouded before he could see the table. He wiped them while Hermione unwound her scarf. Bundles of herbs hung from the rafters, scenting the smoke with pine and something bitter. Hagrid's bed occupied one corner beneath a patchwork quilt. Four mugs stood on the table beside rock cakes and a slab of treacle tart large enough to feed the Quidditch team. Harry had been worrying all day about how to begin their questions. The sight of a place laid for each of them made him wish they had come only for tea. Draconia looked at the rock cakes. One had cracked the wooden plate beneath it.

"I made tart as well," Hagrid said quickly.

"Excellent," she replied.

Fang pushed against Harry's knees until Harry scratched behind his ears. The dog then moved to Draconia, rested his head in her lap and released a mournful sigh. She looked down at him. "He remembers you," Harry said.

"He has poor judgement."

Draconia scratched his head anyway. Hagrid poured tea. His mugs were almost the size of small buckets; Hermione needed both hands to lift hers.

"Thought we hadn't had a proper talk in a bit," Hagrid said. "Not since the match."

Harry glanced at the others. "Did you see it?"

"Course I did! Near pulled me beard out when yer broom started actin' up. Knew yeh'd get back on, though."

"You knew?"

"Well—not knew exactly. But yer dad was the same on a broom. Hard ter shake off once he'd decided where he was goin'."

Harry forgot the questions. He sat forward with both hands around the mug. "You saw my dad play?"

"Couple o' times. James was a Chaser at school. Flew like someone'd personally offended gravity."

Harry smiled, picturing his father leaning easily into a turn. He knew what that felt like. It was difficult to imagine him doing ordinary things until somebody supplied one.

"Was my mum there?"

"Oh, Lily wasn't much fer Quidditch. Came ter the important matches, though. Spent half of 'em tellin' James what he was doin' wrong."

Hermione smiled into her tea.

Draconia noticed. "What?"

"Nothing."

"You looked at me."

"I was thinking it sounds familiar."

Harry's smile widened. Draconia chose a piece of treacle tart with great care. "I have never attended one of Potter's matches to tell him what he is doing wrong."

"You made the banner."

"That was advice."

Hagrid laughed, a booming sound that made the mugs tremble.

For several minutes, they talked about lessons. Hermione described the essay Flitwick had set. Draconia complained that Snape praised her potion and then took points from Harry because his prepared ingredients were "unacceptably coarse." Harry told Hagrid how Neville had somehow melted a brass scale without using heat.

Fang rested his head more heavily on Draconia's knees, and she shifted her mug to keep it clear of his nose. It was easy to forget the reason they had come. That, Harry thought, might have been why Hagrid invited them. Not because he knew about the third floor, but because he had noticed the weeks passing and decided he wanted to see them. The realisation made the questions more difficult. Hermione set down her mug. "Hagrid, can I ask you something?"

"Course."

"What happened to the parcel you collected from Gringotts?"

Hagrid stopped chewing. Fang lifted his head from Draconia's lap. Harry watched Hagrid's expression pass through surprise, alarm and an unsuccessful attempt at innocence.

"What parcel?"

"The one from vault seven hundred and thirteen," Harry said.

"Dunno what yeh mean."

"You took me with you."

"Could've been anythin'."

"The newspaper said someone broke into an emptied vault later that day," Hermione said. "Was it the one you took Harry to?"

Hagrid reached for the teapot, though every mug remained nearly full. "Newspapers say all sorts."

Draconia watched him pour tea into Harry's mug until it overflowed onto the table. "Hagrid."

"Eh?"

"You're pouring it on the table."

Tea ran between the plates. Hagrid swore and grabbed a cloth. His ears had gone red beneath his hair. Harry watched him push the spilled tea away from Hermione's notebook and felt suddenly ashamed of how they had planned the visit. Hagrid had laid places for them, baked a tart and remembered his father's flying. Harry did not want him to think any of that had been a means of getting an answer.

"We don't want to get you in trouble," Harry said.

Hagrid stopped wiping.

"We found something last night."

Hermione gave Harry a warning look, but he continued.

"On the third floor."

The colour drained from Hagrid's face.

"Yeh didn't."

"We didn't mean to," Harry said quickly. "The staircase moved, and Filch was coming. We went through a locked door."

"You went in?"

"Only briefly," Draconia said.

Hagrid stared at her.

"One of its heads nearly ate Harry," Hermione added.

"Briefly," Draconia repeated.

Hagrid dropped the cloth.

"I told Dumbledore a locked door weren't enough."

"Dumbledore knows about it?" Harry asked.

Hagrid's mouth closed. Draconia placed the rock cake she had been examining back on the plate. "Is the dog yours?"

Hagrid sank into his chair. "'Course he's mine. Fluffy wouldn' eat anyone."

All three stared at him.

"He might chew a bit if startled," Hagrid amended.

"Fluffy?" Hermione said.

"He's a three-headed dog," Harry said.

"He's still got a name."

Draconia rubbed one temple. "Where did you acquire a three-headed dog?"

"Greek chappie I met in a pub."

"Of course."

Hagrid pointed a thick finger at them. "Never mind where I got him. None of yeh should've been there. That corridor's forbidden fer a reason."

"Because Fluffy is guarding the parcel," Harry said.

Hagrid's hand lowered.

"The same object you removed from Gringotts," Hermione added.

"I never said that."

"You didn't need to," Draconia said.

Hagrid looked towards Harry, perhaps hoping for help. Harry wanted to help him. Then he remembered the dog standing over the trapdoor. Hagrid had not denied what they thought it was guarding; he had begun explaining why they ought to leave it alone.

"Someone tried to steal it," he said. "What if they try again?"

"They won't."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's safe here."

"Behind one locked door?"

"Fluffy's not the only thing protectin' it."

Hermione leaned forward. "What else is there?"

Hagrid realised his mistake. His great hands closed around the edge of the table.

"Now, listen. What's down there was put there by Professor Dumbledore. He knows what he's doin'. There are enchantments from half the teachers in the school, and no student has any business knowin' more."

"Which teachers?" Harry asked.

"No."

"Is Snape one of them?" Hermione asked.

"I'm not tellin' yeh."

"Quirrell?" Draconia asked.

Hagrid pushed himself to his feet. "Enough."

The force of the word silenced them. Fang withdrew beneath the table. Hagrid looked from Hermione's determined face to Draconia's calculating one, then settled upon Harry. His anger faded almost immediately, leaving worry behind.

"Promise me yeh'll leave this alone."

Harry looked at the table top. Tea had soaked into the grain. One of the rock cakes sat near his hand, dense enough to be used as building material.

"Hagrid—"

"Promise."

Harry stared at the tea-darkened grain. He could promise not to go back to the dog. He could not promise to stop wondering about his broom, or about a thief who had found a way into Gringotts. Hagrid wanted all of it put aside at once, and Harry did not know how to do that. Hermione spoke carefully. "We won't go through the door again."

Hagrid's shoulders eased.

"Not unless we know someone else is trying to," Draconia added.

Hermione turned on her.

"That was not part of what I said."

"It was the missing qualification."

Hagrid groaned and covered his face. "Why'd the three of yeh have ter find each other?"

Harry smiled faintly. "You invited us."

"Worst decision I ever made."

Fang emerged from beneath the table and placed his head back in Draconia's lap. She resumed scratching behind his ears. Hagrid lowered his hands.

"Whatever it is," Hermione said, quieter now, "it must be important."

Hagrid returned to his chair. "It is."

"Valuable?" Draconia asked.

"Depends what yeh value."

Hermione and Draconia both became very attentive. Hagrid saw it happen.

"No."

"You've already said it belongs to Dumbledore," Hermione said.

"I said he put it there."

"So it belongs to somebody else," Draconia concluded.

Hagrid's face became desperate. Harry almost felt sorry for him.

"Who?" he asked.

"Not tellin'."

"Someone we'd know?"

"No."

"A teacher?"

"No."

"A Ministry official?" Hermione asked.

"No."

"Someone connected to Gringotts?"

"No, it's between Dumbledore and Nicolas Flamel, and that's all I'm—"

Hagrid stopped. Silence settled over the hut. The fire cracked behind him. Fang released a contented breath into Draconia's robes. Hermione slowly reached into her bag. Hagrid closed his eyes.

"Nicolas Flamel," she said, taking out her notebook.

"Forget that name."

Her quill touched the page. "I don't think I can."

"You've got an excellent memory," Harry said.

"Thank you."

"Don' encourage her," Hagrid muttered.

Draconia lifted the treacle tart to conceal her smile. Hagrid looked at the three of them and seemed to recognise, at last, the full size of his mistake.

"Yeh are goin' ter leave this alone," he said.

Harry glanced at Hermione's new line of notes. Then at Draconia, who had already begun considering the name. "We'll try," he said.

Hagrid pointed towards the door. "I think it's best yeh leave."

They gathered their things. Harry paused before leaving and looked back. "Thanks for telling me about my parents."

Hagrid's exasperation softened. "Any time, Harry."

"And for the name."

"Out!"

They spilled from the hut into the cold afternoon. The door closed behind them. Through the wood came Hagrid's muffled complaint to Fang about clever children and the dangers of conversation. Hermione opened her notebook before they had taken five steps. "Nicolas Flamel," she read.

Draconia adjusted her cloak against the wind. "French."

"How do you know?"

"The name."

"That doesn't prove anything."

"It is a beginning."

Harry looked back at the smoke rising from the chimney. The parcel had not been stolen; Hagrid had removed it before the thief arrived. Now they had a dog's name, another person's name and Hagrid's insistence that everything was safe. Harry wished the last part were enough to settle the others.

By the final week of term, the castle was emptying for Christmas. Trunks crowded the Entrance Hall. Owls carried last-minute letters in and out of breakfast, while students compared holiday plans and complained about the amount of work assigned for January.

Harry had never seen so many people delighted to go home. He had not expected the word to trouble him. Privet Drive was a place he endured; Hogwarts was the first place he had chosen. Staying behind should have felt like a victory. Yet every conversation about families reminded him that nobody expected Harry anywhere. Hermione discovered he was remaining at Hogwarts when she found his name upon the holiday list.

"You're not going back?"

Harry looked up from the chessboard in the common room. Neville had shown him how to play earlier in the week, before leaving to pack for the holidays. The black knight kept complaining that Harry had no plan. Harry was beginning to consider putting it back in the box.

"No."

"Why not?"

He moved a pawn. "My aunt and uncle don't want me there."

Hermione frowned. "They said that?"

"They don't have to."

The answer emerged more sharply than he intended. Hermione's expression changed.

Harry looked down at the board. "Sorry."

"You don't need to apologise."

"I snapped at you."

"Yes, but I was asking questions you didn't want to answer."

That distinction seemed obvious to her. Harry turned his pawn between two fingers. Hermione sat beside him rather than across from him. "You could come to my house."

He looked at her.

She tucked a piece of hair behind one ear. "My parents wouldn't mind. I've written about you. They know we're friends."

Harry looked at her for a moment, waiting for the part of the invitation he had misunderstood.

"Would they want someone they don't know staying for Christmas?"

"They'd know you once you arrived."

Hermione said it as if this solved the entire matter. For a few seconds, Harry let himself imagine it: a house he had never seen, Hermione's parents asking about school, a Christmas morning where nobody counted his presence as an inconvenience. He wanted it badly enough to become frightened of wanting it.

"I should stay," he said.

Hermione tried not to look hurt. "Why?"

"Because it's my first Christmas here."

That was true, if incomplete.

"And Hagrid's staying," Harry continued. "And I think the Weasleys are. It'll be all right."

Hermione studied him with the expression she wore when she knew an answer was missing but had decided not to force it.

"Next year then?" she said.

It sounded less like a suggestion than a reservation.

Harry smiled. "Maybe."

"Definitely."

She moved his knight into a better position and stood. "Come on. Draconia's waiting at the library."

Harry put the pieces away and followed her downstairs. Draconia stood outside the library studying a suit of armour missing one glove. He had learned that the less interested she was in what she was looking at, the longer she had probably been waiting. Hermione reached her first.

"I invited Harry to stay with my family next Christmas."

Draconia looked at her, then at Harry. He wished Hermione had allowed him more than three seconds before sharing that information.

"How generous," Draconia said.

Hermione's shoulders rose. "My parents would be happy to have him."

"I didn't say they wouldn't."

"You sounded as though you did."

"I said two words."

"Very pointedly."

Harry stepped between them. "I haven't agreed to anything."

"That's because it's next year," Hermione said.

Draconia folded her arms. "My mother would probably invite you too."

Harry blinked. "To Malfoy Manor?"

"She asked about you after Diagon Alley."

"What did you say?"

Draconia's gaze moved briefly towards the empty corridor. "That you were smaller than I expected."

Harry stared.

"And," she added, "that I liked you."

Hermione's irritation vanished. Draconia's ears turned pink. "What?"

"You told your mother that?" Harry asked.

"I was eleven. My judgement was impaired."

"You're still eleven."

"My judgement remains impaired."

Hermione smiled.

Draconia noticed. "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"I was thinking it."

"Loudly."

Harry laughed. The lightness lasted only a moment.

"I'm going home tomorrow," Draconia said.

Harry had known. He had seen her name upon the list, elegant and unmistakable beneath Hermione's. Still, hearing it made the coming holiday feel emptier.

"For the whole break?"

"That is generally how leaving works."

"You could write," Hermione said.

Draconia glanced at her. "So could you."

"I intend to."

"Then I suppose I'll have to answer."

They stood together beneath the watch of the one-gloved suit of armour. Harry wanted to ask them both to stay. He did not. Hermione's parents were waiting for her. Draconia's family expected her, and whatever complications existed at Malfoy Manor, she still spoke of her mother with warmth. He imagined asking, and then imagined the letters their parents must have been writing all term. Both girls were looking forward to going home. He could miss them without making them feel bad about it.

"When do you leave?" he asked.

"After breakfast," Hermione said.

Draconia examined the suit of armour again. "As do I."

Harry nodded. "Right."

Hermione looked at him. Before he could prepare, she put her arms around him. The hug was quick and awkward. One of the books trapped between them dug into Harry's ribs, and Hermione nearly struck his chin with her hair. Yet for those few seconds she held on firmly, as if making certain he understood something she had been unable to put into a timetable.

"Write to me," she said when she stepped back.

"I will."

"Not only if something happens."

"What would I write about otherwise?"

"What you ate. Which books you read. Whether you're sleeping properly."

Draconia watched this with a complicated expression. Hermione turned towards her. For once, Draconia failed to anticipate what was coming. Hermione hugged her too. Draconia's arms remained stiff at her sides. Her eyes widened over Hermione's shoulder and met Harry's in alarm. Harry covered his smile with one hand. After a moment, very carefully, Draconia returned the hug. Hermione released her.

"You have to write as well."

Draconia recovered her posture. "I gathered that."

"And answer the questions."

"Depending upon their quality."

"All of them."

"We'll negotiate."

The following morning, Harry stood upon the steps and watched them leave for the station. Hermione waved until the path curved away. Draconia did not wave. She looked back once, found Harry watching and touched two fingers to the edge of her scarf. Harry raised his hand. Then the path curved, and neither girl was visible any longer.

The castle grew quiet in the days that followed. Harry ate with the few Gryffindors who remained, visited Hagrid and discovered how far a dropped quill could be heard in the empty common room.

On Christmas morning, parcels waited at the foot of his bed. For several seconds, he only stared. He had received presents before, technically. The Dursleys usually gave him something so they could say they had: a coat hanger, old socks, a fifty-pence piece once, which Uncle Vernon reclaimed after breakfast because Harry had not thanked him enthusiastically enough. These parcels had his name on them. Different people had written it in different hands. Harry picked one up, then put it down again to look at the others. Nobody was watching to see whether he appreciated them properly.

Hagrid had sent a wooden flute. Mrs Weasley, whom Harry had met only briefly, had knitted him a thick emerald jumper and sent a box of homemade fudge. Hermione's parcel contained a book about famous magical mysteries. Between the pages, she had inserted notes wherever she considered the author's conclusions questionable. A letter lay inside the cover.

Harry,

Mum says the invitation stands for next year. Dad says you don't need to decide eleven months early, which is obviously true, but it would still be useful to know before December.

I found three references to Nicolas Flamel in the public library, but they were all about streets or French wine. I don't think they are relevant.

Please tell me you are eating something besides sweets.

Happy Christmas.

Love from Hermione.

Harry read the final line several times. Nobody had ever ended a letter to him with love before. He folded it carefully and placed it back inside the book. Draconia's gift was wrapped in dark green paper without ribbon. Inside was a small brass compass.

It had only one hand, no letters marking north and no glass over its face. The needle turned when Harry lifted it, spun twice and continued wandering. A note explained:

It does not point north. Picture a room or place you have actually visited, and it points towards it. It cannot find people or places you merely wish existed. It also cannot tell you whether a route is safe.

This seemed useful given your ongoing war with the staircases.

Do not take it apart. You will not be able to repair it.

My mother says Happy Christmas. My father asked why I was sending a gift to a Gryffindor and then spent twenty minutes recommending more expensive ones.

I chose this.

—Draconia

Below her name, in smaller writing, she had added:

Write back before Granger decides I have failed some friendship requirement.

Harry held the compass in his palm and pictured the Gryffindor common room. The needle turned towards the dormitory door. He tried the Great Hall, then Hagrid's hut; each time, it shifted. Then he pictured Malfoy Manor, or what he imagined it might look like. The needle wandered without settling. He had never been there. Draconia had included the warning for a reason. Harry set it beside Hermione's book, smiling.

At the bottom of the pile lay one final parcel. It contained a cloak unlike any fabric Harry had touched. Silver-grey folds slipped through his hands like water, light enough to feel insubstantial. There was no name upon the wrapping, only a note written in a narrow hand.

Your father left this in my possession before he died. It is time it was returned to you.

Use it well.

Harry drew the cloak around his shoulders and his body vanished. Only his head remained floating above the floor. He stared into the mirror beside his bed, then pulled the fabric higher and disappeared completely. For the first time in his life, being unseen felt like power rather than neglect.

That night, he waited until the common room was empty before taking the cloak into the castle. He kept checking his feet beneath its edge. Hiding was less useful if someone could see his slippers walking about on their own.

Hogwarts after midnight belonged to different creatures. Portraits slept in their frames, snoring against painted pillows. Suits of armour muttered in empty corridors. The staircases moved without carrying anyone, rearranging paths for reasons known only to themselves. Harry passed through it all unseen. It felt as though he had slipped out of the castle's attention. Portraits did not turn towards him and the suits of armour offered no directions. When Mrs Norris passed close by, however, Harry held his breath. She paused with one ear turned towards him, then walked on. He waited until her tail disappeared before moving again.

Harry used the compass to find the library, checking its direction at each landing. The doors were locked. He thought of the kitchens next, then remembered he had never actually reached them. The needle wandered, as it had when he tried to picture Malfoy Manor.

He put the compass away. Farther along the passage, a door stood slightly ajar with warm light showing through. Harry stopped beside it. He ought to head back to the tower; the cloak did not make unknown rooms sensible places to enter. But there was no barking, no unpleasant smell, only a quiet square of light on the floor. He eased the door open.

Old desks and chairs stood against the walls beneath dusty covers. At the far end of the room, a mirror caught the light. It stood taller than Harry, framed in gold darkened by age. Clawed feet supported it upon the stone floor. Words had been carved around the upper arch, their letters reversed or arranged in a language Harry did not recognise.

Harry approached and pushed back the cloak's hood. His head floated alone in the glass. He looked over his shoulder, found the room empty and slipped the rest of the cloak off, folding it over one arm. For a moment, the reflection was ordinary: striped pyjamas, untidy hair, a thin boy standing in somebody else's classroom.

Then a woman appeared behind him. Harry turned. Nobody stood there. In the mirror, she remained, her dark red hair falling over her shoulders and her green eyes shaped like his own. A black-haired man with glasses stood beside her, one hand resting on her shoulder.

Harry stopped breathing. He had no photograph to compare them with, no clear memory of either face. He looked at her eyes, at the man's hair, and could not think of anybody else they might be.

"Mum?"

The woman's smile deepened. Harry touched the glass, his fingers meeting cold resistance. His father smiled too. More people gathered behind them—men and women whose faces carried pieces of Harry's own. His hair. His eyes. The shape of his nose. They filled the mirror until Harry stood at the centre of generations.

His father looked pleased to see him. That was the part Harry could not stop looking at: the easy smile, the hand resting on his mother's shoulder, the space they had made for him without being asked. He lowered himself onto the floor, keeping one hand against the glass. He did not know how long he remained. Time became irrelevant in the room. His mother continued smiling. Harry imagined what their voices might sound like and whether his mother would have worried about him flying. He wondered whether his father would have laughed at the banner.

When the windows began to pale, the figures were still there. Harry stood reluctantly. He lifted one hand, and his mother raised hers in the glass. It took him several attempts to turn away.

He used the compass to find Gryffindor Tower and slept with it beneath his pillow. Later that morning, he wrote to Hermione and Draconia. He told neither of them about the mirror. Instead, he described Christmas dinner, the snow outside and Fred beating him at wizard's chess. He assured Hermione that vegetables had been present at several meals. He thanked Draconia for the compass and promised he had not dismantled it.

Hermione replied by return owl.

"Present" is not the same as "eaten."

Draconia's response arrived several hours later.

The fact that you specifically mentioned dismantling it has made me suspicious.

Over the next two days, more letters arrived. Hermione described her parents' confusion when an owl arrived during breakfast at their house in Oxford. Draconia wrote from Malfoy Manor upon thick cream parchment. She complained about formal dinners, visiting relatives and her father asking questions she suspected were intended to determine whether Harry had expressed political opinions. Harry wrote from the Great Hall, Hagrid's hut and once from beneath his blankets after midnight. Their letters accumulated in his trunk.

At night, Harry returned to the mirror. The second night, his parents appeared sooner. Harry sat closer. He tried speaking to them. He told them about Hogwarts, about being Sorted into Gryffindor and catching the Snitch in his mouth. He told his father about Quidditch and his mother about the troll. He explained who Hermione and Draconia were because it seemed important that they know.

The reflections never answered.

On the third night, Harry found Dumbledore waiting. The Headmaster sat upon one of the old desks, his long fingers folded over one knee. Harry froze. "I did not see you, sir."

"Strange how short-sighted being invisible can make you," Dumbledore said.

Harry pushed back the hood, drew the cloak off his shoulders and waited for punishment. Dumbledore looked towards the mirror instead.

"So. You, like hundreds before you, have discovered the delights of the Mirror of Erised."

Harry followed his gaze. His family remained in the glass.

"What does it do?"

"It shows us nothing more or less than the deepest and most desperate desire of our hearts."

Harry looked at his mother. "So they aren't really there."

"No."

The answer hurt despite being obvious. Dumbledore's voice gentled. "The mirror gives neither knowledge nor truth. People have wasted away before it, entranced by possibilities that cannot be reached."

Harry did not move from the glass. "I only wanted to see them."

"That is an understandable desire."

"Then why is it dangerous?"

"Because tomorrow night you will want to see them again. The night after that, you may wish to remain longer. Eventually, the life waiting outside this room may begin to feel less important than the one offered within it."

Harry thought of the letters in his trunk. "They're my family."

"Yes."

"I don't remember them."

"No."

"Everyone else does."

Dumbledore was silent. Anger tightened Harry's throat. "There are books about what happened to them. People know what House they were in and what they did at school. Hagrid knows what they were like. You knew them too."

"I did."

"But I don't."

Harry hated how young his voice sounded. The Headmaster did not look away. "That is a deeply unfair thing, Harry."

Harry looked at him. He had expected an explanation about how old he had been, or why he ought to be grateful to remember anything at all. Dumbledore had simply agreed that it was unfair. Harry swallowed, and some of the tightness left his throat.

He turned back to the mirror. His parents stood behind him, yet the reflection had changed. Hermione now appeared at Harry's right side, holding a book against her chest. Draconia stood upon his left, her pale hair bright against the crowd of Potters. Neither girl had been there the last few nights. Harry stared. Dumbledore rose and came to stand several feet behind him. "What do you see?"

Harry nearly answered, before he stopped. His parents were still there. His lost family filled the mirror. But among them stood two people who were alive—two people who would return to Hogwarts within days. Hermione would arrive with questions and books. Draconia would pretend she had not missed him. They existed beyond the glass.

"My parents," Harry said.

It was not a lie, but it was no longer the entire truth. Dumbledore's eyes rested upon him through the reflection.

"The mirror will be moved tomorrow night," he said. "I ask that you not search for it."

Harry touched the compass in his pocket. It could bring him back to this room. It could not promise that the mirror would still be here. "Where will you put it?"

"Somewhere I hope it will be useful."

"That still doesn't tell me where."

A faint smile entered Dumbledore's beard. "You have been spending time with Miss Granger."

"And Draconia."

"Yes," Dumbledore said. "That is increasingly difficult for the castle to overlook."

Harry looked again at the girls in the mirror. "Goodnight, Professor."

"Goodnight, Harry."

He covered himself with the cloak and left. In the corridor, he stood for a moment with the compass open in his palm. He pictured the Gryffindor common room: chairs by the fire, Hermione's books left beside them, the place where he would find her when term began. The needle settled. Harry followed it.

He never asked it to take him back to the mirror.

The first morning of the new term was loud enough to make Christmas feel like a dream. Students crowded into the Great Hall with holiday stories, late parcels and complaints about unpacking. They had returned the evening before, filling the corridors with voices Harry had missed more than he realised. Now benches scraped and owls crossed in confused flocks, while ghosts drifted through groups of students trying to welcome everyone at once.

Hagrid had kept him at the hut for supper when the train arrived. By the time Harry returned to the tower, Hermione had already gone upstairs, and he had not found a sensible excuse to search for Draconia in the dungeons.

Harry stood before he realised he was doing it. Hermione entered first. She saw him almost immediately and changed direction, abandoning the other Gryffindors she had arrived beside. Her winter scarf was tangled in her hair, and she had failed to notice.

"You look tired," she said by way of greeting.

"Good to see you too."

"Were you sleeping properly?"

"Yes."

"How properly?"

Harry smiled. "I missed you."

Hermione stopped, colour rising into her face. "Oh."

Then she hugged him. This time, no books came between them. Harry hugged her back before he could become uncertain about it. When they separated, Hermione looked pleased and embarrassed in equal measure. She began telling him about three Flamel references she had eliminated over the holidays.

Draconia arrived while Hermione was halfway through explaining why a nineteenth-century French merchant could not be the man Hagrid meant. She paused near the doors while two Slytherins went past her towards their table. Her eyes found Harry and Hermione, and the guarded expression she had brought into the Hall eased. Harry crossed the floor. Draconia waited, one hand still resting on her bag strap. "You didn't dismantle it?" she asked.

"No."

"Lose it?"

"No."

"Use it to enter anywhere forbidden?"

Harry hesitated. Draconia's eyes narrowed. "Potter."

"It worked perfectly."

"That was not my question."

Harry smiled. "I missed you too."

Her reprimand vanished. For a moment, she looked as surprised as she had in Madam Malkin's when he first made her smile.

"I gathered that from the letters," she said, rather quietly.

Then Hermione reached them.

"You failed to answer three of my questions."

"I answered the important ones."

"You don't get to decide which are important."

"I plainly did."

Hermione embraced her before the argument could develop. Draconia froze, then recovered more quickly than she had before Christmas. Her arms closed around Hermione, brief but certain. When they stepped apart, Hermione immediately resumed complaining about the unanswered questions. Draconia accused her of treating correspondence like an examination, which did nothing to discourage her. Harry listened, remembering the two quiet figures beside him in the glass.

He could still picture his parents' faces. They would not be waiting in the tower; no amount of looking into the mirror could change that, and he still wanted them badly enough for the thought to hurt. But Hermione and Draconia had come back. They were here, making plans and expecting him to join in. Harry moved closer as Hermione opened her bag to show them what she had found. He had missed this more than any reflection could show him.

"Harry," Hermione said.

He blinked. "What?"

"Draconia claims your handwriting became worse over Christmas."

"It did," Draconia said.

"You agreed to defend me," Harry told Hermione.

"I agreed to assess the evidence."

"Traitor."

Draconia smiled. "She's learning."

They found a place at the breakfast table together. That afternoon, they returned to the library.

Hermione had listed the Nicolas Flamels she could eliminate. Draconia brought names her relatives had mentioned in connection with Dumbledore, most of which Harry could not pronounce. He brought a Chocolate Frog left over from Christmas and set its card beside his elbow after eating it. For three hours, the card remained there while they searched books which were far less interesting than their titles promised. At last, Hermione shut another one.

"He could have used another name."

"Why would Dumbledore trust someone who concealed his identity?" Harry asked.

Draconia turned a page. "Because Dumbledore conceals almost everything else."

"That's unfair," Hermione said.

"Is it?"

"He told me what the mirror did," Harry said.

Both girls looked at him. Harry had not meant to mention it that way.

Draconia's quill stopped. "What mirror?"

Harry looked down. He had avoided telling them because speaking about the room would make it real in a different way. The memory belonged to a quiet part of him, one he did not know how to share without exposing everything around it.

Hermione's voice softened. "Harry?"

He drew the compass from his pocket and turned it once between his fingers. "Over Christmas, I found a mirror that showed me my parents."

Neither interrupted. Harry told them. He did not tell them how long he had sat there, or how hard it had been to walk away. He explained the cloak, the room and the people in the glass. When he reached Dumbledore's warning, Hermione's hand stopped moving along the edge of her notes.

"And then it showed you two," he finished.

Hermione's expression changed. Draconia became unreadable.

Harry hurried on. "Dumbledore said it shows what you want most. I think—I don't know. You were standing with them."

Silence gathered around the table. It was not hostile, but Harry wished one of them would break it. Hermione did. "Were we older?"

Harry blinked. "What?"

"In the mirror. Were we our current ages, or adults?"

"The same as now."

"What were we doing?"

"Standing there."

"With your family?"

"Yes."

Hermione looked down at her notes, although Harry had said nothing she could write there. Draconia watched the compass turn between his fingers. Neither hurried him towards the next answer.

"You didn't tell us in the letters."

"No."

"Why?"

"I thought it might disappear if I explained it."

The answer sounded foolish aloud. Neither girl treated it that way. Hermione reached across the table and placed her hand over his. "The mirror wasn't wrong," she said.

Harry looked at her fingers.

"No," Draconia agreed quietly. Her hand settled beside his other one—not covering it, only close enough that their knuckles touched.

Harry released a breath. They sat like that until Hermione glanced down and saw the name on the Chocolate Frog card. She drew it towards her with her free hand. "Wait."

Harry looked up. Hermione read the card once, then again, her fingers tightening around his hand. "Harry."

"What?"

She turned the card over. Dumbledore's portrait occupied the front; on the back, beneath a summary of his achievements, her thumb rested below one short line.

Professor Dumbledore is particularly famous for his defeat of Grindelwald in 1945, the discovery of the twelve uses of dragon's blood and his work on alchemy with his partner, Nicolas Flamel.

For several seconds, none of them spoke. Draconia leaned closer, her eyebrows rising as she reached the last name.

"We have spent two months searching for that name."

"It was in my Christmas sweets."

Hermione closed her eyes. "I told you to organise it."

"You told me to organise everything."

"Yes, and this demonstrates why."

Draconia took the card. "Flamel is an alchemist."

Hermione released Harry and began pulling books from the nearest stack. "A famous one. If he worked with Dumbledore, he may be in one of the older reference books."

Harry watched her check the index of a heavy volume on alchemy. Until now, they had been searching general histories and lists of names. Hermione found the page, and Draconia leaned over her shoulder.

"What does an alchemist make?" Harry asked.

Neither answered immediately. Their heads drew closer over the passage. Then Hermione looked up. "The Philosopher's Stone."

Harry frowned. "What does it do?"

"It transforms metal into gold," Draconia said.

"And produces the Elixir of Life," Hermione added. "It keeps the drinker alive—as long as they keep taking it. This says Flamel made the only known Stone."

Harry looked at the picture beside the passage, then thought of the parcel Hagrid had removed from Gringotts. Something small enough to carry in a pocket, important enough for a high-security vault and enchantments from half the school. Hagrid had named Flamel when they asked about its owner. The Stone fitted everything they had learned. It did not explain Snape's fixed gaze at the match, or Quirrell whispering beside him.

"We have to tell a professor," Hermione said.

"Which professor?" Draconia asked.

"McGonagall."

"And if Snape is involved?"

"Then she should know."

"What if Quirrell is involved?" Harry asked.

Hermione glanced between them. "Professor Dumbledore."

"He already knows the Stone is here," Draconia said. "He helped Flamel hide it."

Harry took the Chocolate Frog card back. A cold thought had begun to form. "What if whoever wants it tries when Dumbledore isn't here?"

Hermione frowned. "Why would he leave?"

"He can't stay in one place all the time."

Draconia's attention sharpened. "Then we need proof before that happens."

Hermione sat back. "No."

Harry knew that tone. "We have good reason to think the parcel was the Stone," she continued. "We know someone broke into an emptied vault that day. And we saw two professors muttering while Harry's broom tried to throw him off. That is enough to tell an adult, even if we can't explain all of it."

"It is enough to sound suspicious," Draconia said. "Not enough to accuse anyone."

"We don't have to accuse them."

"Then what exactly do you propose we say?"

"The truth."

Draconia gave a short, humourless laugh. "They aren't going to like us finding something they tried to keep secret."

Hermione's face tightened. Harry recognised the argument before either girl said anything else. Hermione wanted a teacher to take responsibility; Draconia wanted to know which teacher would listen before they handed over everything they knew. His shoulder no longer hurt, but he had not forgotten the broom wrenching his arm. "We tell McGonagall what we've seen," he said. "The newspaper, what Hagrid said, and both professors at the match. But we don't say either of them did it, because we don't know."

Draconia looked at him.

"She can decide what to do about the protection," Harry continued. "And if we see anything else, we tell her that too."

Hermione considered this. "And no going beneath the trapdoor."

Harry hesitated.

"Harry."

"We don't go beneath it unless someone else does first."

"That is not the same promise."

"It's the honest one."

Hermione frowned deeply.

Draconia said, "I agree."

"You would."

"Yes."

Hermione looked from one to the other. Then she sighed.

"Fine. But we speak to Professor McGonagall tomorrow."

Harry extended his hand across the table. Hermione eyed it. "What are you doing?"

"Making it official."

"That isn't necessary."

Draconia placed her hand over his. "She's frightened of commitment."

"I am not."

Hermione put her hand over theirs. Her fingers were ink-stained. Draconia pressed hers a little more firmly against his palm. Harry kept his hand beneath theirs and listened to the familiar scrape of chairs between the shelves. Tomorrow, they would have to explain all of this to Professor McGonagall. For now, nobody had let go.

"Tomorrow," Hermione said.

"Tomorrow," Harry agreed.
