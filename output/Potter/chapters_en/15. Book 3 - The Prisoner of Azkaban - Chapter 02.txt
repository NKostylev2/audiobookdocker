2

Aunt Marge arrived the following afternoon with three suitcases, two bulldogs and enough disapproval to require a separate vehicle.

Her car appeared at the end of Privet Drive shortly after four, moving with the laboured determination of a machine carrying more than its manufacturer had morally intended. The engine coughed whenever the driver changed gears. One headlamp had been repaired with tape, and the rear of the car sat noticeably lower than the front beneath the combined weight of Marge, her luggage and a pair of dogs panting against the windows.

Vernon had been waiting for nearly twenty minutes.

He stood at the bottom of the garden path in his best summer shirt, checking his watch whenever a vehicle entered the street and pretending not to. Petunia hovered several steps behind him wearing a floral dress and the strained smile she reserved for neighbours, school administrators and relatives whose approval she valued more than their company. Dudley occupied the open doorway, shifting excitedly from one foot to the other.

Harry stood behind him with an empty luggage trolley. The Dursleys did not own a luggage trolley.

Vernon had borrowed it from Mr Parsons at number seven after deciding that asking Marge to carry anything herself would create a poor first impression. Mr Parsons had offered to help. Vernon refused so quickly that Harry suspected Marge had already met him.

"There she is," Vernon announced.

The car mounted the kerb. It remained there for several alarming seconds before dropping back onto the road with a metallic complaint. A terracotta pot beside the pavement trembled.

Harry tightened his grip upon the trolley.

"One incident," Vernon murmured without looking at him.

"I remember."

"You will call her Aunt Marge."

"She isn't my aunt."

Vernon turned his head by less than an inch. "Do you want that form signed?"

Harry looked towards the sitting-room window.

The Hogsmeade form remained locked inside Vernon's desk. Harry had checked after breakfast. He had not attempted to retrieve it, though the silver potion case upon his desk included a vial labelled Burns, and setting fire to the drawer had begun to feel increasingly justifiable.

"Yes."

"Then show some gratitude."

Marge's car stopped outside Number Four. Harry stepped onto the pavement before gratitude could become visible on his face.

The driver's door opened. Marge emerged in stages, first one sensible brown shoe, then a broad leg enclosed in tweed, followed by the remainder of a body which appeared to have been constructed specifically for occupying narrow spaces. Her short grey hair had been set into stiff curls. A leather handbag hung from one shoulder and struck the car door when she turned.

"Marge!"

"Vernon!"

The larger-than-normal brother and sister approached each other with arms extended. Their embrace was loud, brief and strong enough to make Vernon stumble backwards when Marge struck him affectionately between the shoulders.

"Look at you," she declared. "Put on a bit around the middle."

Vernon laughed with genuine delight.

"Nothing compared with you."

"Good living!"

"Exactly!"

Petunia's smile tightened.

Dudley hurried forward. "Auntie Marge!"

"There's my boy."

Marge seized his face between both hands and kissed his cheek. Dudley accepted this indignity because her handbag had begun producing the unmistakable rustle of gift wrapping.

"You've grown."

"Mum says I'm nearly five foot."

"Strong too, I expect."

"I beat everyone in my year at wrestling."

Harry had seen Dudley wrestle. His technique consisted mainly of sitting upon smaller boys until they surrendered.

"Fine lad." Marge pinched one of his cheeks. "Good, solid build. That's what proper feeding does."

Her eyes moved past him. Harry stood beside the boot of the car. Recognition did not improve her expression.

"Oh," she said. "Still here, are you?"

Harry considered several answers. Vernon touched the breast pocket where he had placed the key to the desk drawer.

Harry smiled.

"Apparently."

Marge looked him up and down. Her gaze travelled from his untidy hair to Dudley's oversized shirt, then settled upon the trainers separating at the soles.

"Still scrawny."

"It's deliberate."

"What?"

"Cuts down on wind resistance."

Dudley made a choking sound.

Vernon cleared his throat loudly. "Harry has been looking forward to your visit."

Marge stared at Harry. Harry stared at Vernon.

"I've barely spoken about anything else," he said.

Petunia stepped between them before the silence could develop consequences.

"You must be tired after the drive."

"Roads were full of idiots," Marge declared. "People don't know how to drive anymore. No discipline. Give anyone a licence these days."

She had parked partly upon the pavement and left one wheel inside Petunia's flowerbed. Harry decided not to mention it. A bulldog shoved its face against the rear window.

Marge's entire manner softened.

"There's my precious Colonel."

The back door opened.

Colonel launched himself onto the pavement like a sack of wet cement. His front paws landed first, followed by a broad chest, wrinkled face and a length of saliva which remained attached to the car for several seconds. The second bulldog followed more cautiously. She was smaller, pale brown and wore a tartan bow around her neck.

Colonel saw Harry.

His body stiffened. A growl formed somewhere deep within his thick chest. Harry stopped breathing.

The dog's eyes were nothing like those of the animal which had watched Number Four during the night. Colonel's were small, watery and uncomplicated. He did not recognise Harry. He merely disliked him.

Even so, the first note of the growl travelled through Harry before he could separate one dog from another.

His hand closed around the trolley handle until the metal pressed into his palm.

"He remembers you," Marge said.

"Touching."

Colonel bared his teeth.

Marge patted his head. "Good instincts. Dogs know character."

"Does he tell you himself?"

Vernon coughed. Harry looked at him and saw the warning. The Hogsmeade form, one week. He stepped away from Colonel.

"Where are the bags?"

Marge smiled in satisfaction, having mistaken restraint for submission.

"Boot."

The boot contained enough luggage for an evacuation.

Harry loaded two suitcases onto the trolley. The third refused to fit and had to be balanced across the top. A canvas bag of dog food occupied the remaining space, accompanied by a polished wooden case which clicked ominously whenever tilted.

"What's in this?" Harry asked.

"Medicine."

"That's a rather large amount even for you?"

Marge did not appear to recognise the question as dangerous.

"It's for the dogs."

"Right."

Colonel followed Harry up the path, breathing heavily against the backs of his legs. The trolley's left wheel squeaked. One suitcase shifted whenever it struck a paving stone, forcing Harry to stop and rearrange the load beneath Vernon's impatient gaze.

He got everything inside without dropping it. Vernon did not thank him. Marge did not notice.

Petunia saw the mud Colonel tracked across the immaculate hall carpet and made a small sound through her nose.

"Should they be wiped?" she asked.

Marge removed her coat. "They're animals, Petunia."

"Yes. I know."

"They've been in the car for three hours."

"Of course."

Colonel shook himself; saliva fell from his mouth as he did, and he struck the wallpaper. Petunia closed her eyes. Harry pushed the trolley towards the stairs before the sight of her expression made him laugh.

The largest suitcase weighed more than his school trunk and possessed none of its useful handles. He dragged it step by step, the lower edge striking every stair. Colonel attempted to follow, blocking Harry's feet and growling whenever the suitcase approached him.

"Could someone hold the dog?" Harry called.

"He won't hurt you if you don't act frightened," Marge answered from the sitting room.

"That seems to be his entire ambition."

"What was that?"

"Nothing."

At the top of the stairs, Harry manoeuvred the luggage into the spare bedroom. Petunia had transformed the room over the previous two days. Fresh flowers stood upon the dresser. The sheets were new. A glass of water waited beside the bed beneath a lace cover designed to protect it from dangers Harry could not identify.

Marge's dogs had two padded blankets on the floor. Harry's birthday had passed without the Dursleys providing so much as a clean pillowcase. He placed the final bag near the wardrobe.

Colonel stood in the doorway, blocking the exit. Harry looked at him. The bulldog looked back.

"Move."

Colonel growled.

"You don't own the corridor."

The growl strengthened.

Harry took one step forward. Colonel crouched.

Every muscle in Harry's body tightened. His wand lay locked beneath the stairs. He had made the mistake of leaving Draconia's potion case in his room. The nearest possible weapon was a ceramic water jug upon the dresser, which Petunia would consider more valuable than his ankle.

"Colonel!"

Marge's voice rose from below.

The bulldog's ears shifted.

"Biscuits!"

He turned instantly and charged downstairs. Harry watched him go.

"Excellent character, bloody beast," he muttered.

He shut the bedroom door and leaned against it. His hands were shaking again. Harry curled them into fists.

It was only a dog. Not even an especially impressive dog. Its nose had been bred so flat it could barely breathe. Harry had always loved the pair of border collies that their neighbours on number 7 owned. Harry had faced Fluffy and a basilisk large enough to swallow Colonel without noticing. But these bulldogs weren't typical dogs, and none of those facts prevented his pulse from racing.

The reaction angered him. Fear should have followed rules. It should have measured present danger against previous survival and adjusted accordingly. Instead, it arrived whenever it pleased, mistaking slamming doors for collapsing stone and a bulldog's growl for something waiting beneath a streetlamp.

Harry pushed away from the door. The room remained quiet. Afternoon light sprang across the quilt. Roses stood in a vase upon the dresser, their petals opening towards the window.

He breathed in, held it and released it slowly. When his hands steadied, he went downstairs.

The first evening passed better than Harry expected.

Marge occupied Vernon's armchair while Colonel slept across her feet. The second bulldog, Matilda, lay beside the fireplace, snoring with a high, whistling sound. Dudley sat upon the carpet opening the presents Marge had brought him: a computer game, a box of chocolates and a twenty-pound note enclosed within a card declaring him A Credit to the Family.

Harry cleared the wrapping paper.

Marge spent dinner discussing dogs, local taxes and the deterioration of modern children. Harry listened to her describe a neighbour's seven-year-old as "soft in the head" for being frightened when Colonel chased him into a shed.

"That dog wouldn't hurt a fly," she said.

Colonel had attempted to bite Harry's hand before the soup.

"He prefers postmen," Vernon replied.

Marge laughed so hard she spilt gravy.

Petunia served roast beef, potatoes and Yorkshire puddings. Harry's plate contained a reasonable portion because Marge was present, though Petunia watched every bite with the tense attention of someone worried he might expose the family by enjoying himself.

Harry ate quietly.

The food did little to settle his stomach. He had spent the hour before dinner carrying Marge's belongings, filling water bowls and removing dog hair from the sofa. Exhaustion should have made him hungry.

Instead, each mouthful seemed to expand in his throat. Marge poured more brandy.

"And how's St Brutus's?" she asked at last.

The question arrived while Harry was cutting a potato. He had known it would. Vernon gave him a meaningful look. Harry placed the knife beside his plate.

"Still standing."

Marge snorted. "Good strict place, is it?"

"Uncle Vernon says I've improved since attending."

"Plenty of caning?"

"They say it's best not to count them."

Vernon smiled. Harry's fingernails pressed into his palm beneath the table.

Marge leaned closer. "What did they give you for that cheek?"

Harry looked at her flushed face. She wanted details. The Dursleys had told her he attended an institution for violent children, and she had apparently spent the intervening years hoping someone there hurt him correctly.

"Extra writing," he said.

Marge looked disappointed.

"Writing?"

"Lines."

"What sort?"

Harry chose the first sentence that came to mind.

Marge Dursley should be on a diet.

He nearly smiled.

"Mostly apologies."

"Not enough hard discipline nowadays," Marge said. "When I was young, boys like you knew the consequences of disobedience."

"Did girls not have consequences?"

Vernon lowered his glass.

Harry added quickly, "At your school."

Marge waved the distinction away. "We knew our place."

Harry looked towards Petunia. Her mouth formed the same thin line it wore whenever anyone claimed the past had been simpler.

"Do they lock you up?" Marge asked.

"When necessary."

"Good. Isolation teaches respect."

Harry's grip tightened upon the edge of his chair. A cupboard door closed in his memory. No light. Spiders moving near his feet. Dudley laughing beyond the wood while Petunia said he could come out when he learned gratitude.

Harry raised his water glass. The rim clicked against his teeth. Marge did not notice.

"I told Vernon years ago," she continued. "You cannot reason bad blood into behaving. Firm hand from the beginning. That's what was needed."

"More potatoes?" Petunia asked loudly.

Marge accepted. The conversation moved to Colonel's digestion. Harry finished dinner without speaking again. Afterwards, Vernon cornered him in the hall.

"That was acceptable."

Harry looked at him.

"High praise."

"You will maintain it."

"There are six days left."

"Correct."

"And then you sign."

Vernon's gaze flicked towards the sitting room. Marge was telling Dudley about a dog breeder in Yorkshire.

"If you continue behaving."

"I did everything you asked tonight."

"You answered back."

"I answered questions."

"Don't test me, boy."

Harry stepped closer. He kept his voice low.

"No new conditions; that's the rule, Big V."

Vernon's face reddened around the edges. Harry held his gaze. The cupboard key rested inside Vernon's trouser pocket. His wand remained behind the lock, and Harry knew he should not antagonise the man carrying it.

Knowing and caring had become separate processes.

"I will decide whether you have fulfilled the agreement," Vernon said.

"That wasn't the agreement."

"I am your guardian."

"Petunia's my guardian."

Vernon's mouth opened. Marge called from the sitting room.

"Vernon! Bring that photograph album."

He looked towards the sound, then back at Harry.

"Kitchen. Wash everything."

"There's a dishwasher now."

"Marge dislikes the noise."

"Colonel snores like a damaged engine."

"The kitchen."

Harry went. The sink filled with water hot enough to redden his hands. Petunia stacked dishes beside him without comment. Through the open doorway, Marge and Vernon laughed over photographs of Dudley as a baby.

Harry scrubbed gravy from a plate. Petunia passed him a saucepan.

"She'll settle after the first evening," she said.

Harry looked at his aunt. The statement might have been an apology if Petunia had possessed the courage to make one.

"Will she?"

"She gets excited."

"She asked whether strangers beat me."

Petunia picked up a cloth. "She doesn't understand."

"You told her I go there."

"Vernon decided—"

"And you agreed."

Petunia wiped the same section of counter twice.

"It was easier."

"For who?"

Her hand stopped. Harry waited.

From the sitting room came another burst of laughter. Marge shouted that Dudley had always possessed "a proper pair of lungs." Vernon agreed.

Petunia resumed wiping.

"Finish the dishes, and you can go."

Harry turned back to the sink. The water had grown grey with grease. His reflection moved faintly in the dark window above it, face pale beneath untidy hair.

He had his mother's eyes. Petunia never had the strength to look at them for long. Harry's first nightmare of Marge's visit began inside the cupboard under the stairs.

He was small enough that his knees touched the door when he lay upon the mattress. Darkness pressed close around him, warm and airless. Pipes ran above his head.

A voice hissed through them. Harry could not understand the words. He crawled towards the door and found no handle. The wood became stone beneath his fingers.

Behind him, something vast shifted its weight. Harry turned; green eyes opened in the dark. He woke on the floor beside his bed.

His shoulder had struck the bedside table during the fall. A glass lay broken near his hand, water spreading across the carpet in a dark pool.

Harry lay still. His bedroom door remained closed. The wardrobe stood where it belonged. Moonlight reached through the curtains, pale and harmless.

Then he heard breathing. Heavy, uneven and directly outside. Harry's fingers closed around a shard of glass.

The breathing continued. A claw scratched the door. Harry pushed himself upright.

"Colonel?"

A low growl answered. The bulldog had decided to wander. Harry pictured the animal sitting on the landing, broad head lowered, waiting for the door to open.

His pulse hammered.

"Go away."

Colonel scratched again. The noise travelled through the wood and became scales against stone. Harry's scar prickled. His arm ached, bringing back the shock of hitting the hospital-wing floor. He tightened his grip upon the glass until the edge cut his finger.

Warm blood ran across his knuckle. The sight dragged him fully awake. Harry released the shard and pressed his thumb against the cut.

Outside, Colonel lay down heavily. His breathing settled into the wet, obstructed rhythm of sleep. Harry remained seated beside the bed. He should clean the broken glass. He should stop the water soaking into the carpet. He should return to sleep before Petunia woke and discovered the damage.

For several minutes, he could not make his legs move.

Eventually he reached beneath the bed and pulled out Draconia's potion case. The vial labelled Cuts glowed faintly when he opened the lid. Harry uncorked it with his teeth and placed one drop across his finger.

The skin sealed.

He cleaned the glass without magic, wrapping every shard in an old shirt. The carpet retained a darker patch where the water had fallen, but it would dry before morning.

Colonel remained outside the entire time. Harry could not bring himself to open the door.

By the third day, Marge had settled into Number Four with the efficiency of an invading army.

Her coat occupied the hall stand. Her medicines filled half the bathroom shelf. Dog bowls appeared in the kitchen, sitting room and beside her bed. Colonel claimed the rug before the fireplace and growled whenever Harry passed. Matilda developed a fascination with the cupboard under the stairs and spent long periods sniffing beneath the door where Harry's trunk was kept.

Marge noticed everything Harry did.

She noticed that he read too much, though none of his magical books was available. She noticed he held cutlery incorrectly, sat too quietly and wore his hair too long. She regarded his glasses as evidence of weakness and his thinness as a personal moral failure.

"You need work," she announced over breakfast.

Harry looked at the bowl of cereal Petunia had placed before him.

"I have chores."

"Proper work. Outdoors. Builds shoulders."

"Vernon has me washing his car."

Marge glanced towards the drive. "Not properly, from the state of it."

The car had been washed the previous morning. Vernon looked through the window. A single leaf rested upon the bonnet. His face tightened.

"Do it again," he said.

Harry finished his cereal.

Marge watched.

"Slow eater too."

"I've never entered professionally."

"What?"

"He'll wash it after breakfast," Petunia said.

Harry carried his bowl to the sink. Marge's hand closed around his wrist as he passed. The touch was unexpected; his body reacted instantly.

The bowl fell. It struck the tiles and shattered. Harry twisted free before he knew what he was escaping, knocking his chair backwards. His opposite hand rose defensively.

Marge stared at him. So did everyone else. Harry's breath came too quickly. The kitchen had gone silent except for Colonel's panting beneath the table. Marge looked from Harry's raised hand to the broken bowl.

"What is wrong with you, boy?"

Harry lowered his arm.

His wrist carried four red marks where her fingers had gripped it.

"Don't grab me."

Vernon stood. "You dropped that deliberately."

"No."

"He nearly struck me!" Marge exclaimed.

"I didn't."

"I saw his hand."

"I was moving away."

"From your aunt?"

Harry looked at Vernon.

His heart had not slowed. The kitchen walls seemed closer, sunlight too bright against every polished surface.

"Don't grab me," he repeated.

Marge's face hardened.

"Skittish runt."

The word settled over him. She examined him with the same detached criticism she used when discussing dogs.

"Bad nerves," she continued. "That's breeding again. Weak dogs startle. Sometimes they bite."

Harry heard Draconia screaming his name in the Chamber. He heard Hermione say, That does not mean it wasn't real. His magic stirred. The light above the kitchen table flickered once.

Petunia saw it.

"Harry," she whispered.

The fear in her voice cut through the rising pressure. He bent and began collecting pieces of the bowl.

"Leave it," Petunia said.

"I can clean it."

"You'll cut yourself."

"I know what broken glass does."

Marge snorted. Harry placed the largest pieces together. A fine crack split the tile beneath them, travelling from the point of impact towards the table. Vernon had not noticed. Petunia's gaze remained fixed upon it.

"I'll get the dustpan," Harry said.

He left the room.

In the hall, he stopped beside the cupboard. Matilda had followed and stood sniffing along the lower edge of the door.

Harry crouched.

"Anything interesting?"

The bulldog glanced at him.

Unlike Colonel, she did not growl. She sat heavily upon the carpet and looked towards Vernon's pocket.

The cupboard key formed a clear outline beneath the fabric. Harry looked at the dog.

"You might be the clever one."

Matilda sneezed. Vernon appeared in the kitchen doorway.

"Why are you sitting there?"

"Discussing security."

"Get up."

Harry rose. Vernon held out the dustpan.

"One more display like that and the agreement is finished."

"She grabbed me."

"She touched your arm."

"I didn't know she was going to."

"You do not behave like a lunatic because someone touches you."

Harry stared at him.

Vernon had spent years hitting his shoulder, arm, grabbing his hair or the back of his neck whenever words proved insufficiently immediate. Harry used to endure it without thinking.

The Chamber had changed that. Now unexpected hands became vines around his ankles. Pressure upon his wrist became Riddle's fingers closing over Draconia's. Every touch carried the possibility of being restrained while someone he loved vanished beyond reach.

Harry knew Marge was not Tom Riddle. But his body did not seem to care.

"I didn't get a choice," he said.

Vernon frowned. "A choice on what?"

Harry almost answered. The words reached his throat and stopped there.

Explaining would require offering Vernon a piece of something real. It would require describing the Chamber, the nightmares and the moment Harry believed he was going to die. Vernon would either call him abnormal or look frightened.

Both possibilities felt unbearable.

"Nothing."

Vernon's expression grew satisfied. He understood retreat.

"Clean the mess."

Harry took the dustpan. Matilda followed him back into the kitchen.

On the fourth afternoon, Harry escaped to Mrs Figg's garden.

The word escaped was perhaps excessive. Petunia sent him there with a china bowl Mrs Figg had left after bringing over sponge cake. Marge was asleep in the sitting room after lunch, Vernon had gone to purchase more brandy, and Dudley was playing his computer game upstairs.

Harry took the longest possible route.

He walked around the block, through the narrow path behind Magnolia Crescent and along an alley where weeds grew freely between cracked paving stones. Clouds had gathered during the afternoon, softening the heat without producing rain. The air smelled of warm brick and honeysuckle.

Mrs Figg's gate stood open. Harry knocked anyway.

"Round the back," she called.

He followed a crooked stone path beside the house.

Her garden bore no resemblance to Petunia's. Plants grew wherever they had found space. Lavender crowded the path, rosemary leaned over a broken birdbath, and an apple tree occupied one corner with the untidy generosity of something never instructed to conform. A faded chair sat beneath its branches.

Mrs Figg knelt beside a flowerbed wearing gardening gloves. Four cats watched her from different heights.

Harry held up the bowl.

"Petunia sent this."

Mrs Figg looked at him. Her gaze stopped upon the red marks around his wrist.

"What happened?"

Harry lowered the bowl.

"Nothing."

"That word is becoming overworked."

"Marge grabbed me."

"Marge?"

"Vernon's sister."

Mrs Figg's mouth thinned. "The troll woman."

"You've met her?"

"I've smelled her."

Mr Tibbles jumped down from the wall and approached Harry. He rubbed against Harry's ankle once before inspecting the bowl.

"No cake," Harry told him.

Mrs Figg removed her gloves. "Come inside."

"I'm meant to go straight back."

"Then you had better come inside quickly."

Harry followed her into the kitchen.

The room smelled of tea, cat food and the faintly medicinal scent of boiled cabbage. Photographs covered one wall, each containing one or more cats in increasingly elaborate poses. Mr Paws appeared dressed as Father Christmas in three separate frames.

Mrs Figg filled the kettle.

"I'm not hungry," Harry said.

"I didn't ask."

"You're getting food."

"I'm getting tea."

She opened a tin. There were sandwiches inside. Harry sat at the small table.

Mrs Figg placed a plate in front of him without comment. Ham, cheese, thick bread, no crusts removed.

Harry looked at it.

"Petunia fed me lunch."

Mrs Figg took two cups from a shelf. "Then you can scandalise her by eating twice."

He picked up half a sandwich. The first bite awakened hunger so abruptly that it hurt. Harry had intended to eat slowly. He finished the half before the kettle boiled. Mrs Figg pretended not to notice.

She poured tea into chipped cups, added milk to Harry's without asking and sat opposite him.

"How long is Marge staying?"

"Three more days."

"You counted quickly."

"I'm talented."

"And after that?"

"Vernon signs my Hogsmeade form."

Mrs Figg's eyebrows rose. "Does he?"

"That's the agreement."

"Did you get it in writing?"

"He has the form."

"That is not what I asked."

Harry took another sandwich.

"You sound like Hermione."

"The clever girl?"

"Yes."

"You could do worse."

"Draconia would disagree."

"The blond one?"

"She is also clever."

"Then the clever girls."

"Hermione would want you to use her name."

"What would Draconia do?"

"Object more elegantly."

Mrs Figg smiled. The kettle ticked while it cooled. One cat jumped onto the empty chair beside Harry and began watching his plate.

"How is school?" Mrs Figg asked.

"Dangerous."

"I meant other than that chamber business."

"So did I."

Her smile faded. Harry looked down at the sandwich.

"Hogwarts is good," he said. "Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"The food's good."

"High praise."

"My friends are there."

Mrs Figg waited. Harry searched for another explanation.

Hogwarts was moving staircases, towers emerging from morning mist and corridors which changed character according to the hour. It was Hermione correcting his essays in the library and Draconia insulting his handwriting while quietly giving him her better quill. It was Hagrid's hut in winter, the Quidditch pitch beneath open sky, and the Great Hall filling with the sound of hundreds of people who knew magic was real.

It was also blood upon stone. A voice travelling through walls. Hermione's empty place at breakfast. Draconia's fingers trembling around his wrist.

Harry took another bite.

"I want to go back," he said.

Mrs Figg tilted her head. "But?"

He had not realised the word required one.

"But nothing."

She did not argue. Harry appreciated that enough to finish the second sandwich. A sound came from outside. Every cat in the kitchen turned towards the back door.

Their movement was simultaneous. Harry placed the food down.

"What was that?"

Mrs Figg rose. "Probably a fox."

Mr Tibbles's tail expanded. Harry followed her to the garden.

Nothing moved among the plants. The gate remained open, revealing a narrow slice of pavement beyond. Clouds passed overhead, turning the afternoon light grey.

One of the cats hissed towards the far hedge. Harry's eyes caught black fur between the leaves. The dog stood beyond Mrs Figg's garden, partially concealed by branches. Only its head and one broad shoulder were visible. It watched them without moving.

"There," Harry said.

Mrs Figg turned.

The dog stepped back, and the Leaves closed across it. Mrs Figg crossed the garden with surprising speed. She reached the hedge and pushed apart the branches.

The narrow service lane beyond was empty. Harry joined her.

"It was the same one."

"You're sure?"

"It's difficult to confuse with a normal dog."

Mrs Figg looked along the lane.

Her face had lost colour.

"You recognise it," Harry said.

"No."

"That was quick."

"I don't recognise it."

"But you know something."

Mrs Figg released the branches. "I know you should stay away from unfamiliar animals."

"Hermione said that."

"Then listen to her."

"Draconia said it might be magical."

Mrs Figg's gaze sharpened. "Did she?"

"She didn't say what sort."

"Probably because she doesn't know."

Harry folded his arms.

The cats gathered near their feet, all watching the empty lane.

"Mrs Figg."

"I have nothing useful to tell you."

"That isn't the same as nothing."

She looked at him.

Harry saw the decision pass across her face.

Whatever Mrs Figg knew, she chose silence.

"Go back to your aunt's house before they come looking," she said.

"You called it Petunia's before."

"And now I'm telling you to go."

Harry did not move.

Mrs Figg lowered her voice.

"If the dog appears again, do not approach it. Do not follow it. Go somewhere with other people."

"You think it's dangerous."

"I think you are thirteen and carrying no wand."

Harry looked towards Privet Drive.

"How do you know I haven't got it?"

"I know your relatives."

He remembered Hermione at King's Cross, holding the pouch with a silver disc set aside for Mrs Figg. She had told him Dumbledore had arranged for the neighbour to check on him. He had imagined something like this: sandwiches, questions about school, someone noticing if his letters stopped. Nobody had explained how much she already knew.

"Hermione said you'd be checking on me this summer," he said. "Did Dumbledore tell you about the wand?"

Mrs Figg glanced towards the neighbouring house.

"He doesn't have to tell me everything."

"You knew about Hermione and Draco. You asked how big the dog was before you saw it."

"I have eyes."

"And you knew about the Chamber."

Her face tightened. A breeze moved through the apple tree, turning the leaves silver at their edges. Somewhere beyond the fence, a lawnmower began.

"I've been watching for unusual things for a long time," she said quietly.

Harry stopped thinking about the dog.

"How long?"

She looked at him, then away.

"Since before you went to Hogwarts."

He thought of the visits, the stale cake and the photographs. They stretched back much further than two summers.

"Since I was little?"

Mrs Figg's silence answered him.

"Dumbledore asked you then?"

"Yes."

Heat climbed Harry's neck. He had thought she was helping because people finally knew what happened at number four. He had not understood that someone had been close enough to notice long before the rescue.

All those years.

All the afternoons he had spent in this house looking at photographs of cats while Dudley went to amusement parks and restaurants. All the times he returned to Privet Drive expecting nothing except another night beneath the stairs.

Dumbledore had known where he was. Dumbledore had placed someone close enough to watch.

"What did you tell him?"

Mrs Figg looked miserable.

"The truth."

"Which truth?"

"That your relatives were unpleasant. That you were underfed. That you were left alone too often."

"And?"

Allowed.

Draco,

I'm eating. I still have the potions. No one needs to come.

"Excuse me?"

"Harry, go upstairs."

"Running away. Like his father."

She lurched from her chair. Her body continued growing. Her shoulders rounded. Her cheeks expanded until her eyes became small points inside a reddening face. One shoe split at the side. The chair collapsed beneath her.

"I- I don't know how."

"You did this!"

"I didn't use my wand."

Marge rose from the floor, not by choice. Her expanding body had become lighter. Her feet left the tiles. The torn pieces of the Hogsmeade form spun beneath her as air rushed through the room.

Dudley crawled under the table. Colonel barked frantically, leaping at Marge's shoes as they lifted beyond reach. Matilda fled into the hall.

"VERNON!"

Vernon caught one ankle. Marge's other foot kicked him in the shoulder and knocked him across the table. Plates fell. The trifle overturned into Dudley's lap.

Harry stared at what he had done. Horror fought with a fierce, shameful satisfaction.

Marge had become a large brown balloon pressed against the ceiling, her arms drifting uselessly from her sides. Her brooch remained fixed to the front of her dress, the bulldog's silver face appearing even more disapproving than before.

"Put her down!" Petunia shrieked.

"I don't know how!"

"Use that thing!"

Harry pulled out his wand. Marge saw it.

"You freak!"

The word cracked across the room. Harry lowered the wand.

He could not remember any spell for deflating relatives. Hogwarts had failed to include it in the second-year curriculum. Vernon staggered upright.

"You've ruined everything."

Harry looked at him.

"You tore the form."

Vernon's face became almost colourless beneath the red.

"My sister is on the ceiling!"

"She insulted my parents."

"They are dead!"

The shout silenced even Marge. Harry felt the words strike every old wound at once.

Dead.

Therefore irrelevant. Dead, therefore defenceless. Dead, therefore available for the Dursleys to reshape into whatever explanation made their lives easier.

Petunia stood beside the ruined table, one hand pressed to her mouth.

Harry looked at her. She said nothing.

Marge had called Lily defective. She had blamed Lily's blood, laughed at James's death and thanked Vernon for raising the child they left behind.

Petunia had said nothing. Whatever grief Harry glimpsed earlier had not been enough.

It never was.

He put his wand away.

"I'm leaving."

Vernon stopped.

"What?"

Harry turned towards the hall.

"You will stay right where you are!"

"No."

Vernon followed. Marge shouted from the ceiling. Colonel ran in circles beneath her. Harry reached the cupboard under the stairs.

The door remained locked. He pulled his arm back and punched the shabby wooden door, causing its rusting hinges to buckle inward.

A sharp crack sounded outside—distant, perhaps only thunder, though the rain had stopped hours earlier.

Harry opened the cupboard.

His trunk sat inside beside the broomstick. School robes, books and cauldron filled the confined space. The sight of them loosened the tightness in his chest.

Vernon entered the hall.

"You cannot leave."

Harry pulled the trunk forward.

It caught on the frame.

"Oh yeah? Watch me."

"Where will you go?"

"Anywhere."

"You have no money."

"You sure?"

"No one will take you."

Harry looked over his shoulder.

Draconia had asked Lucius not to arrive personally, which meant Harry suspected the entire Malfoy household was one poorly worded letter from appearing upon the lawn.

Vernon was wrong. The certainty felt new.

"People will notice I'm gone," Harry said.

Vernon's mouth closed. It was the most important difference between this summer and every one before it.

People would notice. Harry hauled the trunk free.

"You are thirteen," Vernon said. "You cannot simply walk out."

"You told Marge I attend a secure centre for criminals. This feels like a failure of security."

"This is not a joke!"

"I know."

Harry straightened. Behind Vernon, Petunia stood in the dining-room doorway. Marge remained visible beyond her, floating near the ceiling while Dudley hid beneath the table.

Harry looked at his aunt. For one last foolish second, he hoped she might stop him for the right reason.

Not because the neighbours would talk. Not because Dumbledore expected Harry to call Number Four home. Not because his departure would require explanation.

Because he was her sister's son, Petunia gripped the doorframe.

"What will we tell people?"

Harry's hope disappeared so quietly it barely hurt.

"You'll think of something just like you did for Marge."

He carried the trunk upstairs. No one followed immediately.

Harry packed quickly. Hermione's broom kit fitted along one side. Draconia's potions went between two folded jumpers. He placed every letter into the inner compartment and secured them beneath his school robes.

The Monster Book fought the belts when he lifted it.

"You're coming too."

It snarled.

"That makes one of us excited."

He placed it inside and slammed the lid. Hedwig entered her cage without being asked. Her amber eyes remained fixed upon Harry.

"I know."

Harry did not know what he was answering. He dressed properly, pulled on his trainers and opened the bedroom door. Vernon waited upon the landing.

"You are not taking that trunk through the neighbourhood."

"I'll try to make it inconspicuous."

"It has an owl on top!"

"People keep birds."

"Not owls!"

"That sounds discriminatory."

Vernon blocked the stairs. Harry's hand settled upon his wand. Neither moved. Marge shouted again below. Her voice sounded farther away. Petunia ran into the sitting room.

"She's going out the window!"

Vernon turned instinctively.

Harry moved.

He dragged the trunk past before Vernon could recover, the wheels striking each step with heavy thuds. Hedwig gripped her perch and spread her wings for balance.

In the dining room, Marge had drifted through the broken upper section of the window. Her dress caught briefly upon the frame before tearing free. She floated above the back garden, revolving slowly while Colonel barked from beneath her.

"VERNON!"

Vernon ran past Harry. Petunia clutched Marge's shoe with both hands. It slipped from the swollen foot and sent Petunia falling into the roses.

Harry reached the front door. Dudley emerged from beneath the table covered in trifle.

"You can't go."

Harry stopped. Dudley stood in the hall, cream and custard dripping from his shirt. He did not sound angry. Only bewildered.

Harry looked at him.

"Why not?"

Dudley opened his mouth.

No answer came.

Perhaps he had never imagined Number Four without Harry inside it. Harry had occupied the edge of Dudley's life since infancy: target, servant, rival and proof that Dudley deserved whatever Harry did not receive.

Harry leaving would remove the comparison.

"You'll come back," Dudley said.

Harry knew that was true; he wouldn't have a choice.

"Probably."

The answer seemed to reassure neither of them. Harry opened the door. Warm night air entered the hall. Somewhere behind the houses, Marge continued shouting Vernon's name.

Harry dragged his trunk onto the path. At the gate, he looked back.

Petunia stood in the rear garden, reaching uselessly towards her floating sister. Vernon was attempting to untangle a washing line. Colonel and Matilda barked in different directions. Dudley remained framed by the open front door, sticky and pale.

No one called Harry's name. He turned away. The wheels of Harry's trunk were not designed for pavements.

They caught in every crack, struck kerbs with enough force to threaten the clasps and produced a steady grinding noise which followed him from street to street. Hedwig's cage rocked upon the lid. She dug her talons into the perch and glared whenever the trunk tilted.

"I'm doing my best."

She clicked her beak.

"You could carry it."

Hedwig turned her back. Harry continued walking. Privet Drive disappeared behind him.

The first corner produced relief. The second produced exhaustion. By the third, his anger had begun cooling enough for practical concerns to enter.

He had no destination.

The Grangers lived in Oxford. Harry knew their street name but not the number, and he possessed no idea which train to take or whether services ran after ten. Malfoy Manor existed somewhere in Wiltshire beneath enough concealment charms to confuse entire governments. Harry could send Hedwig, but a reply might take hours.

The Leaky Cauldron offered rooms. London lay more than twenty miles away. Harry checked his pockets.

Four pounds and seventy-three pence. His wizarding money remained in a pouch at the bottom of his trunk. Even if he opened it upon the pavement, no Muggle taxi driver would accept Galleons without asking difficult questions.

Mrs Figg's house was six streets behind him. Harry stopped beneath a streetlamp. Going to her would be sensible.

She knew magic. She had offered shelter. She could contact Dumbledore, the Malfoys or the Grangers. Harry would be safe inside her cluttered sitting room before midnight.

He pictured returning. Mrs Figg would send word to Dumbledore. Dumbledore would speak to the Ministry. Someone would restore Marge and escort Harry back to Number Four because ancient magic considered a locked cupboard preferable to Voldemort.

Vernon would be waiting.

Harry tightened his grip upon the trunk.

"No."

Hedwig shifted.

"I'm not going back."

The words sounded uncertain on the empty street. Harry repeated them.

"I'm not."

He walked onwards.

Magnolia Crescent gave way to unfamiliar roads. Houses became smaller and less uniform. Several front gardens contained bicycles, children's toys and flowers growing beyond approved boundaries. A pub near the corner released warm light and laughter whenever the door opened.

Harry passed without stopping.

Every few minutes, he looked behind him. Nothing followed. The absence should have been reassuring. Instead, the back of his neck prickled. The black dog had appeared every time Harry felt trapped. Now he was alone, carrying everything he owned through Little Whinging after dark, and the animal had vanished.

Harry reached a small public park. The gates were unlocked. A row of benches stood beside the path beneath chestnut trees. Beyond them, swings moved faintly in the breeze.

He dragged the trunk towards the nearest bench.

His palms were blistered. When he released the handle, both hands retained its shape. Harry flexed his fingers and sat heavily.

The night pressed close around the park.

A road remained visible through the railings, but traffic had thinned. Streetlamps cast pale circles over the pavement. Insects moved through the light. Farther away, a railway line hummed.

Harry put Hedwig's cage beside him.

"All right."

She watched.

"We need a plan."

Hedwig continued watching.

"Oxford is too far. Wiltshire is worse. Hagrid's house is in Scotland."

She ruffled her wings.

"Helpful."

Harry took out his wand. He could send sparks. Someone magical might see them. Unfortunately, Vernon had spent years insisting Little Whinging contained no strange people, and Harry suspected this was one subject upon which they largely agreed.

He considered sleeping in the park until morning. The bench was hard, but he had slept in a cupboard for years. August nights remained warm. He could take the first train to London once services started, then find the Leaky Cauldron.

It was not a good plan. But it was a plan. Harry opened his trunk and found the last shortbread biscuit.

It had broken into three pieces beneath the weight of his books. He ate each piece slowly, catching crumbs in his palm. Narcissa's baking tasted faintly of vanilla. He imagined her reaction when Draconia received his message.

He had not sent one. Harry looked at Hedwig.

"Should I tell them?"

The owl extended one leg. He found parchment inside the lid of his trunk and wrote upon his knee.

Hermione, Draco,

I've left Privet Drive.

He stopped; anything after that sounded alarming.

There was an argument- long story short, I accidentally inflated my 'Aunt' Marge.

Harry crossed out the line.

I'm all right. I have my wand, trunk and Hedwig. I'm going to try and make my way to the Leaky Cauldron.

That was evidence. It was also optimism.

Don't panic.

He looked at the sentence. Draconia would call it an instruction issued too late. Hermione would prove panic rational under the circumstances.

Harry signed his name and tied the note to Hedwig.

"Take it to Hermione first. Draco's owl is faster, so she can send word."

Hedwig did not leave.

"What?"

She turned her head towards the trees. Harry followed the movement.

Darkness gathered beyond the path. Tree trunks stood in irregular rows, their leaves blending into one canopy above. The swings moved once.

A shape emerged between them.

Harry rose. The dog entered the path. Moonlight caught the line of its back and the rough fur around its shoulders. It stood taller than Colonel by nearly two feet and carried none of the bulldog's clumsy aggression. Every part of it appeared built for speed.

Its eyes fixed upon Harry. Hedwig opened her wings.

The dog stopped. Harry's wand came up.

"S-Stay there."

The animal didn't move. Its obedience frightened him more than an attack might have. Harry stepped backwards. His heel struck the trunk.

"Who are you?"

The dog lowered its head. Not threateningly, it almost looked sad. Harry had never seen an animal give an expression like that before. He had seen frightened dogs, injured dogs and Colonel's permanent resentment towards quiet.

The dog took one step closer. Harry raised the wand higher.

"I said stay."

It froze. The distance between them was less than twenty feet. Harry could see how thin it was now. Ribs showed beneath tangled fur. One ear was torn. Mud darkened its legs, and an old wound had left a narrow bare patch near one shoulder. The dog looked half-starved.

Harry's fear shifted.

"You're hurt."

Its ears moved. Harry reached slowly towards the trunk.

The dog tensed.

"I, um, I have potions."

He stopped. What was he doing?

Hermione had specifically ordered him not to approach. Draconia had said magical creatures could resemble dogs and told him not to follow it. Mrs Figg had looked genuinely afraid.

Three intelligent people had formed an unusually united opinion. Harry continued reaching for the trunk.

"I'm only opening it."

The dog moved forward. Hedwig launched from her cage.

Her wings struck Harry's head as she flew between them. The dog recoiled. Harry lost his balance, tripped over the trunk handle and fell backwards.

His wand hand struck the air. A sudden deafening bang split the night. Harry rolled onto one elbow. A violently purple bus occupied the road beyond the park gates.

Three storeys rose above the pavement. Gold letters curved across the windscreen, shining beneath the streetlamps.

THE KNIGHT BUS

A narrow door folded open and a young man in a plum-coloured uniform jumped onto the pavement.

"Welcome!" he announced to the empty road. "To the Knight Bus, emergency transport for stranded witches and wizards! My name is stan shunpike, and I will be your conductor for the night."

He looked through the park railings. Harry remained on the ground.

The conductor frowned. "You stranded?"

Harry glanced towards the trees. The black dog had vanished, again. Hedwig circled above the bench.

"Yes," Harry said. "Very."

"Then you're in luck."

The conductor opened the gate and hurried down the path. He was perhaps nineteen, thin and covered in spots. A large purple cap sat crookedly over his ears.

He offered one hand. Harry allowed himself to be pulled upright.

"What happened to the dog?"

"What dog?"

Harry looked towards the path. There was nothing but an empty, damp street.

"Never mind."

Stan examined the trunk, owl cage and Harry's untidy appearance.

"Running away?"

Harry stiffened.

Stan shrugged. "We get loads."

"I'm not running away."

"You've got a trunk."

"I'm travelling unexpectedly."

"Right." Stan nodded gravely. "We get loads of that too."

He approached the trunk.

"What's your name?"

Harry hesitated.

The accidental magic would already have alerted the Ministry. Giving a false name might buy time, but it would also make him look guilty once someone identified him.

"Harry Potter."

Stan's hand slipped from the trunk.

"What?"

"Harry Potter?"

"No, it isn't."

Harry took off his glasses, moved his fringe and showed the scar.

Stan's mouth opened like he was about to say something, but nothing came out. Harry replaced his glasses.

"Does the bus go to London?"

Stan continued staring.

"Stan?"

"You're Harry Potter."

"We've established that."

"The Harry Potter."

"I don't know of another."

"Ernie!"

Stan shouted so loudly that Hedwig veered in the air. A voice answered from the bus.

"What?"

"It's Harry Potter!"

"So?"

"The Harry Potter!"

The driver leaned sideways until his head appeared through the open doorway. He was elderly, wore extremely thick glasses and had attached a stuffed vulture to the dashboard.

He squinted towards Harry.

"Can't see him."

"He's right here!"

"Then get him aboard. We've got Mrs Wickham waiting for Aberdeen."

Stan seized Harry's trunk with sudden enthusiasm.

"Where're you going?"

"The Leaky Cauldron."

"Popular tonight. Five Sickles."

"For the journey?"

"Eleven if you want chocolate, and fifteen gets you chocolate and a hot-water bottle."

"It's August."

"Ten, then. Seasonal discount."

Harry paid five. Stan looked disappointed but loaded the trunk.

The interior of the Knight Bus resembled a boarding house arranged during an earthquake. Brass beds lined both sides of a narrow aisle. Purple curtains hung from the windows, and candles floated beneath the ceiling, tilting whenever the vehicle moved. Several sleeping passengers occupied the beds, secured by straps across their waists.

A witch near the rear knitted through her sleep. Harry placed Hedwig's cage beneath the nearest bed.

Stan fastened a strap around his waist.

"What's this for?"

"Safety."

The driver shouted, "Moving!"

The bus accelerated before Harry could ask what safety meant. His bed shot backwards. The frame struck the wall, causing Harry's head to slam into one of the posts. The Knight Bus tore through Little Whinging.

Houses, cars and lampposts jumped out of its path. The bus squeezed between two vehicles with less than an inch to spare, then widened again upon reaching the next street. Harry gripped the bedframe while his stomach remained several roads behind.

Stan walked down the aisle without holding anything.

"How are you doing that?"

"Practice."

The bus turned ninety degrees at speed. Stan remained upright. Harry's pillow flew across the aisle.

"Mostly practice," Stan added.

The driver leaned upon the horn. The sound resembled an elephant being murdered. Streets and cars scattered. Harry looked through the window. Little Whinging disappeared in a blur of orange lights and dark roofs. No dog followed the bus. No figure stood beneath the streetlamps.

The relief surprised him. He settled back against the headboard. Stan dropped into the opposite bed.

"So why're you out this late?"

"Travelling unexpectedly."

"Right."

Stan grinned. Harry sighed.

"My aunt insulted my parents."

"You left over that?"

"She also floated away."

Stan let out something that was between a chuckle and a sigh.

"Fair."

"I didn't mean to do it."

"Most people don't. A lady in Croydon turned her husband into a wardrobe once. Said it was accidental."

"Was it?"

"Nah. He'd been sleeping with her sister."

Harry looked towards the front of the bus.

"Can I move beds?"

Stan laughed. The vehicle swerved around a church. The church leaned politely aside.

Harry's stomach turned.

"You get used to it," Stan said.

"I don't want to."

"Chocolate?"

"No."

"Hot-water bottle?"

"No."

"Postcard?"

"What would it say?"

Stan produced one from his pocket. The front displayed the Knight Bus travelling across a moonlit sky. Three passengers were visible screaming through the windows.

On the reverse, printed purple letters read:

WISH YOU WERE HERE!

Harry looked at Stan.

"Do people send these voluntarily?"

"Loads."

"To enemies?"

Stan returned it to his pocket.

"You're very difficult to sell things to."

"Being best friends with the brightest witches in my year has its perks."

The words escaped before Harry could stop them. Stan laughed and moved farther down the aisle to wake a passenger approaching her stop. The bus continued towards London.

Without Stan's questions, Harry became aware of his own body again. His shoulders hurt. The blisters upon his palms had broken, leaving raw circles beneath his fingers. His eyes burned with exhaustion.

He had inflated Marge. The fact hovered at the edge of thought, absurd and serious at once.

Ministry officials would arrive at Number Four. They would reverse the magic, modify Marge's memory and question the Dursleys. Then they would come for Harry. Last summer, the Ministry had blamed him for Dobby's Hover Charm and warned that another incident could result in expulsion. Tonight's magic involved an adult floating above Surrey.

Harry touched his wand. Hogwarts could be taken away. The possibility hollowed him. Leaving Privet Drive had felt like reclaiming himself. If the price was losing school, then Vernon had won without needing the form.

Harry bent forward, elbows against his knees. His breathing began to shorten. He focused on the bus, the flailing purple curtains. The forty-odd brass rails that adorned each bed. A candle passing above his head. Five beds on the left, six on the right.

He counted the windows. Named the colours. Felt the wood beneath his hands.

The panic receded before it became visible.

"You all right?"

Stan had returned. Harry looked up.

"Yeah, uh..Fine."

Stan frowned. The expression lacked Hermione's suspicion or Draconia's sharpness. It contained simple uncertainty.

"Want the chocolate?"

"You're a tough salesman stan, Stan. How much?"

"Six Sickles."

"You said eleven with the journey."

"That offer expired."

"When?"

"When you said no."

Harry stared. Stan grinned. Harry paid one Sickle for a small bar after a brief argument concerning fraud. The chocolate softened between his fingers. Warmth settled uneasily in his stomach. Stan sat opposite again, holding a folded newspaper.

"You hear about Sirius Black?"

Harry looked at him confusedly.

"Who?"

Stan unfolded the front page. A gaunt face stared from beneath the headline.

AZKABAN PRISONER REMAINS AT LARGE

The man in the photograph stood behind a numbered board. Long dark hair hung around a face stripped thin by hunger. His eyes burned from deep sockets, not wild exactly, but fixed upon something beyond the edge of the frame. Every few seconds, he turned towards whoever held the camera and laughed without sound.

Harry leaned closer. The expression disturbed him. So did the strange impression that he had seen those eyes before.

"Escaped from Azkaban," Stan said in a delighted whisper. "Only person ever managed it."

"What did he do?"

"Murdered thirteen people with one curse."

Harry looked at the photograph.

"Thirteen?"

"Broad daylight. Street full of witnesses. Blew a great hole through the road. Left nothing of one bloke but a finger."

Stan appeared both horrified and impressed.

"Why would he escape now?"

"No one knows. Ministry's gone spare. Dementors everywhere."

"What are Dementors?"

Stan's grin faded.

"Guards."

"Care to elaborate any more?"

"You don't want to meet one."

Harry looked again at Sirius Black. The man's photograph laughed.

"He looks like he's almost crying."

"Suppose."

Harry read the smaller print. Black had served Voldemort. Black had betrayed unnamed victims. Black was considered armed, exceptionally dangerous and capable of violence without warning.

The article did not explain where he might be going. A cold awareness crept across Harry's shoulders.

"Is he near London?"

"Could be anywhere."

"That's useful."

"Ministry thinks he might try leaving the country."

"Then why are they searching around here?"

Stan looked at him.

"Who said they were?"

Harry thought of Mrs Figg reporting a stray dog. Dumbledore saying precautions had already been taken. Lucius writing about Harry's wand with unusual urgency.

He looked towards the dark windows. The bus raced through the countryside now. Hedgerows folded aside before it. The moon appeared and vanished between clouds.

"Nobody."

Stan returned to his newspaper. Harry read the article again over his shoulder. Sirius Black's photograph turned. For one instant, his eyes met Harry's. It was only enchanted ink. Still, Harry looked away.

The Knight Bus entered London shortly before midnight. Buildings gathered around it in blurred rows. Shop signs flashed past. Traffic lights sprang sideways, and parked cars flattened briefly against walls before returning to shape behind them.

Harry saw the Leaky Cauldron only seconds before the bus stopped. The narrow inn stood between a closed bookshop and a music store, its dark bricks largely ignored by everyone passing. Warm light filled the downstairs windows.

The bus halted with a violent squeal. Harry's bed travelled three feet forwards and stopped against the next frame. Stan unfastened the strap.

"Leaky Cauldron!"

"I heard."

"Tell people you enjoyed the ride."

"Would you like me to lie?"

"Part of the service charge."

Stan carried the trunk onto the pavement. Harry followed with Hedwig, relieved when solid ground remained beneath him.

The night smelled of warm stone, rain and smoke from the inn's chimney. Stan held out one hand. Harry shook it. The conductor leaned closer.

"Be careful."

Harry glanced towards the newspaper beneath Stan's arm.

"Of Sirius Black?"

"Of London. Terrible for pickpockets."

The bus vanished with another cannon-like bang. Harry stood alone.

He had taken two steps towards the inn when the door opened, and he was greeted by Tom the barman. His bald head caught the light from behind him. He wore a stained apron and held a wooden spoon in one hand.

"Mr Potter?"

Harry stopped.

"That depends."

Tom looked past him towards the place the bus had occupied.

"We've been expecting you."

"That seems worrying."

"It needn't be."

Harry tightened his grip upon Hedwig's cage. Voices moved inside the pub. Glasses clinked. A fire crackled despite the warm night. Tom stepped aside.

Harry entered.

The Leaky Cauldron smelled of stew, old wood and smoke. A handful of late drinkers occupied the tables, though conversation faded when Harry passed. Faces turned. Recognition travelled across the room in whispers.

Harry's shoulders tightened. A door opened near the bar. A man emerged wearing a pinstriped cloak, polished shoes and a lime-green bowler hat held against his chest.

"Mr Potter," Tom said, "this is Cornelius Fudge, the Minister for Magic."

Harry looked from the man to Tom. The Minister himself had come to meet him. That did not seem like a promising sign.

Fudge smiled with visible relief.

"Harry."

Harry looked towards the door behind him. Tom had closed it.

"What are you doing here?"

Fudge laughed too loudly.

"Straight to business. Quite understandable after the evening you've had."

The Minister approached and extended one hand. Harry reluctantly shook it. Fudge lowered the hand and adjusted his hat.

"Perhaps we should speak privately."

"Am I expelled?"

"What? No."

The answer came immediately. Harry stared. Fudge's smile remained fixed.

"No disciplinary action?"

"Certainly not."

"Last summer, I received an official warning because a pudding floated."

"Yes, well—"

"Tonight my aunt floated."

"Different circumstances."

"She reached the roof."

"So I understand."

"Then what circumstances?"

Fudge glanced at the listening patrons.

"This way."

He led Harry into a small parlour beside the bar. A fire burned low in the grate. Two chairs waited beside a table set with tea, bread, cheese and a bowl of soup which still steamed.

Harry stopped at the sight of food. Fudge noticed.

"Sit. You must be exhausted."

"I'm not hungry."

His stomach cramped at the smell of soup. Fudge's eyes travelled briefly over Harry's face, clothes and thin wrists.

Concern flickered. It disappeared beneath official cheer.

"Nevertheless."

Harry sat. Tom brought the trunk inside and placed Hedwig's cage near the window.

"Room eleven is prepared," he said.

"Prepared when I just got here?" Harry asked.

Tom looked towards Fudge. The Minister answered.

"Shortly after the Accidental Magic Reversal Squad arrived at your relatives' home."

Harry looked at the clock. Less than two hours had passed.

"You found me quickly."

"The Trace registered the incident. Then the Knight Bus reported its distinguished passenger."

"Stan reported me?"

"Standard procedure when transporting an underage witch or wizard after midnight."

Stan had neglected to mention that. Harry picked up a piece of bread. It was warm.

"What happened to Marge?"

"Restored to her proper dimensions and safely returned to the ground."

"Her proper dimensions are debatable."

Fudge coughed into his hand. Harry ate the bread.

"Her memory has been modified," the Minister continued. "She will recall a reaction to spoiled brandy and an unfortunate fall into the garden."

"What about the Dursleys?"

"They have agreed to allow you home next summer."

Harry stopped chewing.

"I didn't ask."

"No, but these arrangements matter."

"To who?"

Fudge removed his hat and set it upon the table.

"Your protection at that address is unusually important."

"So everyone keeps saying."

Harry thought of Mrs Figg.

"Important enough to watch, but not important enough to change."

Fudge's fingers moved against the brim of his hat.

"I'm afraid I don't follow."

"Mrs Figg has been reporting to Dumbledore for years, as I'm aware."

The Minister's expression revealed genuine surprise. Harry filed that away.

"She knew the Dursleys didn't feed me properly," he continued. "She knew they left me alone. Dumbledore knew."

"I cannot speak for Professor Dumbledore."

"No one can, apparently."

Fudge leaned back.

"This conversation concerns tonight."

"Does it?"

Harry looked at the food. The soup smelled of onions and pepper. Cheese waited upon a wooden board. There was enough for three people, provided none was Dudley.

He had received more concern after breaking a law than during the years he obeyed every rule inside Number Four.

"Why aren't I being punished?" Harry asked.

Fudge spread both hands.

"Accidental magic occurs. Emotions were elevated. Your aunt was provocative."

"She insulted my parents."

"Yes. Deeply regrettable."

"The Ministry didn't care about provocation last summer."

"Last summer involved an enchanted object and a visiting family."

"Dobby."

Fudge blinked.

"A house-elf?"

Harry leaned forward.

"What's changed?"

The Minister's cheerful expression flickered for a second before recomposing. Perks of being a politician, Harry guessed.

"Nothing you need to concern yourself with."

"There's a killer on the loose."

Fudge's eyes sharpened.

"Who told you?"

"Stan had a newspaper."

"Ah."

"Is Sirius Black looking for something? Is he trying to find Voldemort?"

"You know who is dead, Harry."

The fire shifted inside the grate. Harry held Fudge's gaze.

The Minister looked towards the closed door, perhaps hoping someone older might enter and rescue him from direct questions.

"We don't know what his motivations may be."

"That's not an answer."

"Sirius Black is a dangerous man, Harry."

"That also isn't an answer."

Fudge pressed his hat flatter against the table.

"We have no firm evidence regarding his destination."

"But you were relieved when I arrived."

"Any responsible official would be relieved to find a thirteen-year-old safe after midnight."

"You found a room, contacted the Dursleys and came here yourself."

"Your circumstances are unusual."

"You keep saying that whenever you don't want to explain."

Fudge sighed. The sound briefly made him appear older.

"Harry, I cannot disclose details of an active Ministry investigation."

"Does the investigation concern me?"

Fudge took too long. Harry sat back. It did.

The realisation brought less fear than irritation. Adults had once again constructed a danger around him and decided ignorance would function as armour. Fudge pushed the soup closer.

"What I can tell you is that protections will be in place throughout the remainder of the summer. You are to stay here. You may enter Diagon Alley during business hours, but you must return before dark. No excursions into Muggle London. No Knockturn Alley. If you see anything unusual, you tell Tom immediately."

"Well, other than a stray dog trying to befriend me, the only unusual thing all summer has been the Dursleys' diet."

Fudge glanced towards the door.

"Then I'm sure you'll be fine here, Harry."

"You don't believe that."

Fudge stood.

"Remain in the inn tonight."

"I thought I was meant to stay here all summer."

"You are."

"But especially tonight?"

The Minister put on his hat.

"You have had a difficult evening."

"You've had a difficult conversation."

Fudge gave another brittle laugh. Tom opened the door at precisely the moment the Minister reached it.

"Everything settled?"

"Completely," Fudge said.

Harry looked at him. Fudge avoided his eyes as he left the parlour.

Tom turned his attention towards Harry fully.

"More soup?"

Harry glanced at the bowl. He had finished without noticing.

"Yes, please."

Tom smiled. That smile contained no pity, no questions and no expectation that Harry prove he deserved another portion.

Harry sat beside the fire.

His body had become unbearably heavy now that no immediate danger required movement. The day stretched behind him in scattered images: Marge against the ceiling, Vernon tearing the form, Dudley covered in trifle and a black dog watching from between the trees.

His wand rested inside his pocket. Harry was not going back to Privet Drive tonight, and that brought him a cautious relief.

Room eleven stood at the end of the first-floor corridor.

It contained a broad bed, a washstand and a wardrobe which opened without concealing anything except spare blankets. The window overlooked the small walled courtyard behind the inn. Above the rooftops, London spread into the night beneath a faint orange glow.

Tom placed Harry's trunk at the foot of the bed.

"Breakfast from seven."

"Thanks."

"If you wake earlier, knock in the kitchen."

Harry looked at him.

"How early?"

Tom's expression did not change as he shrugged. "Whenever."

He closed the door. Harry remained beside the bed. The room was quiet.

Not Privet Drive quiet, where silence meant listening for someone approaching. This quiet contained distant conversation below, floorboards creaking in other rooms and water moving through pipes without becoming a monster.

Harry unpacked only what he needed. Clean shirt. Toothbrush. Letters.

He placed Hermione's broom kit upon the dresser and Draconia's potions beside the bed. Hedwig drank from a bowl, then settled upon the wardrobe. Harry opened the window.

Night air entered.

"Can you still fly?"

Hedwig fixed him with an offended stare.

"Right. Stupid question."

He wrote two new notes. The first went to Hermione.

I've reached the Leaky Cauldron. Fudge was waiting-long conversation for another day. I still have my wand. Marge has been returned to normal, unfortunately. I'm safe.

I'm staying in room eleven, locked door, full dinner, no bars on the window.

Then he grabbed another small piece of blank parchment for Draconia.

Draco,

Your father's letter arrived at breakfast.

Vernon nearly died when the second page appeared. Please tell Lucius his sense of timing is terrifying but useful. I've left Privet Drive. Fudge says I'm staying at the Leaky Cauldron. He also says Sirius Black may not be looking for me in exactly the way someone says something while clearly refusing to say it.

Don't worry about me tonight. I'm safe

Can't wait to see you and Hermione.

He tied both messages to Hedwig and sent her on her way through the open window. Harry watched her cross the courtyard and disappear above the roofline.

A newspaper lay upon the bedside table; the same photograph occupied the front page.

Sirius Black stood behind the numbered board, thin fingers curled around its edges. His silent laughter repeated every few seconds. The image had been taken years ago, on the day he entered Azkaban. Harry could see fury beneath the madness now, buried so deeply the two had become difficult to distinguish.

The smaller article provided no explanation for why the Minister for Magic had waited personally for a runaway schoolboy. It did not mention Harry, the Potters or a large black dog.

Harry touched the moving photograph. Sirius turned towards him. The dark eyes fixed upon Harry with the same strange intensity he had seen beneath the streetlamp.

Harry lay back onto the bed, beneath clean blankets and listened to the inn settle around him. Every unfamiliar sound kept him awake: footsteps in the corridor, laughter below, a door closing two rooms away. Yet none carried the weight of Privet Drive. None belonged to Vernon or Marge.

He was finally free again.
