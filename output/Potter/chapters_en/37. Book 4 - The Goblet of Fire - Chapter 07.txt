7

The distant lights of Hogsmeade station slowly drew nearer, appearing and vanishing through curtains of rain, small golden squares suspended against the dark. Students crowded the corridor before the train had finished slowing. Doors opened, trunks struck knees, and an owl escaped near the rear carriage, flying over everyone's heads while its owner offered seed with increasing desperation.

Harry stayed seated a little longer. The window had grown cold against his temple, and each curve of the track carried the lights out of sight before returning them a little larger. Beyond his reflection, Scotland was wet black hills and occasional silver lines of water. Inside, warmth lingered in the upholstery alongside the remains of their afternoon: sweet wrappers, Hermione's open book, Narcissa's folded blue cloth, and one of Neville's gardening gloves beneath the opposite seat.

Draconia rested against his side. She had fallen asleep somewhere north of York, surrendering the rules she usually maintained about posture, dignity and personal space. Her cheek pressed into his shoulder, pale strands of hair scattered over his robes. He had scarcely moved for an hour. Waking her would have been unkind, and the trust in her unguarded weight felt too delicate to disturb.

She woke on his shoulder, remained still for one confused second, then sat up so quickly that her forehead almost struck his chin.

"How long?"

Harry considered.

"Three years."

"Potter."

"An hour."

Her hand went straight to her hair. Colour followed a moment later, gathering beneath the tips of her ears. Harry suspected that Draconia would consider dramatic embarrassment a moral failure, but she could do little about the quiet kind.

Hermione closed her book.

"It looks fine."

"You say that because you allowed it to happen."

"I did not place your head there."

Harry raised both hands.

"I was furniture."

"An improvement."

Draconia turned towards the window before either could see whether the insult had restored her composure.

The train stopped. Cold air entered through the nearest open door, and rain hammered the platform roof, spilling through gaps along its edge. Students emerged with cloaks over their heads and uniforms half fastened, their summer clothes suddenly unsuitable for Scotland.

Harry stepped down first. Lanternlight shone over the wet stones, where steam mingled with rain around his ankles. Beyond it, Hagrid's voice rose over the crowd.

"Firs' years! Firs' years over here!"

Harry found the top of his umbrella before the rest of him. Hagrid stood by the gate in a moleskin coat large enough to shelter several children, his beard divided into dripping ropes.

"Harry!"

He left the first-years long enough to sweep Harry into a one-armed hug that lifted his shoes from the platform. Pain pulled beneath Harry's healing ribs. He tried to breathe through it, but Hagrid felt the stiffness and lowered him immediately. The enormous hand became an almost sheepish pat between his shoulders.

"Good summer?"

Harry thought of the cottage, the Cup, and the Dark Mark reflected in the stream.

"Parts of it."

Hagrid looked towards Hermione and Draconia.

"All three of yeh back together. Good."

His gaze returned to the healing cut beneath Harry's eye.

"Heard about the Cup."

"Everyone did," Draconia said.

Several nearby students were already watching her. Hagrid noticed and straightened to his full height. Their interest became less obvious.

"Professor Dumbledore says there'll be people lookin' into it."

"There are panels," Hermione replied.

Hagrid's expression suggested that panels were less useful than large dogs.

"Come see me this week. Got summat new fer lessons."

"Does it possess teeth?" Draconia asked.

"Only little ones."

Harry looked at Hermione.

"That was not a no," she said.

Hagrid returned to his first-years before three could follow older siblings towards the carriages. Harry, Hermione and Draconia crossed the station yard together, heads lowered against the rain.

The horseless carriages waited in two rows, water running over their black roofs. Lamps illuminated empty shafts and buckles that hung around nothing. Harry had wondered about them every year; tonight, he lacked the energy to wonder properly.

They climbed into the fourth carriage. Neville sat with the Tunisian cactus secured on his lap and a bandage around one thumb. Opposite him were Ginny Weasley and Luna Lovegood, whose earrings resembled miniature rainclouds and shed droplets whenever she moved.

Draconia chose a seat beyond the cactus's reach.

"It remembers you," Neville said apologetically.

The plant leaned towards her. Draconia showed it one finger, and it retreated.

Luna watched with interest.

"It respects decisive boundaries."

"Most creatures do."

"People less reliably."

Draconia looked at her. Luna smiled.

The carriage moved without a driver's call, wheels turning along the muddy road. Rain blurred the grounds until Hogwarts appeared all at once: towers rising through mist, hundreds of windows burning above the cliffs, and the lake black beneath the storm.

Conversation faded. Even Neville's cactus withdrew its feelers. The castle filled the dark with the confidence of something too old to require the landscape's permission. Battlements vanished into cloud, windowlight broke over wet rock, and the highest towers appeared only when lightning revealed them.

Harry's chest tightened. Home. The word came automatically, but it no longer displaced the cottage. Somehow, the two could stand beside each other.

They passed between the stone boars, their backs silver with rain. Harry remembered Dementors stationed at those gates the previous year, and the cold they had brought into the train. Lupin had been seated across from him then, before either knew what the other would become. Tonight, only darkness and wind-tossed trees waited at the entrance.

The carriage stopped beneath the front steps, and students ran towards the oak doors. Harry reached the Entrance Hall soaked below the knees. Warmth touched his face, torches gleamed across the flagstones, and the marble staircase rose through familiar shadows.

Wet cloaks brushed past him, leaving dark streaks on the floor. Someone was complaining about a flooded trunk; somebody else had already lost the summer homework they had spent the journey copying. Harry listened to both with an odd sense of relief. These were complaints he understood, small enough to survive until morning without anybody drawing a wand. He looked up at the staircase, at the shifting torchlight along its carved edge, and loosened the grip he had kept on his bag since the station. For a moment, arriving was enough.

McGonagall stood beside the Great Hall doors, directing students inside. Her eyes found the cut beneath Harry's eye, lingered on his careful movements, then took in the two girls standing close enough to catch him.

"Mr Potter."

"Professor."

"I trust the remainder of your journey was uneventful."

"The cactus tried something."

Neville clutched the pot.

"It was misunderstood."

McGonagall closed her eyes briefly.

"Into the Hall."

Draconia began following Harry and Hermione towards Gryffindor, then stopped after a single step. Harry noticed. The train had allowed them to stay together; the four long tables restored the school's divisions with the ease of habit.

Beneath the green and silver banners, Pansy had claimed the centre of the fourth-year group. A place remained beside Daphne Greengrass, and another stood empty where Theodore Nott should have sat. Draconia adjusted her sleeve.

"Tomorrow," Hermione said.

"Breakfast," Harry added.

"The library," Draconia replied.

For a moment, her fingers caught Harry's sleeve. The crowd might have explained the touch, but the look she gave him was deliberate. Then she released him and crossed towards Slytherin under the gaze of students who had spent the end of summer reading her surname in hostile headlines.

Harry and Hermione continued to Gryffindor. Seamus made room, Dean raised two goblets in greeting, and Neville placed the cactus beneath the bench, where it immediately tried to eat a fallen fork. Harry chose a seat with a view of the Slytherin table.

The bench still had a groove worn beneath his usual place, and the table smelled faintly of polish under the stronger smell of wet wool. He had spent the summer remembering this room as a whole: candles, ceiling, four tables crowded with noise. He had forgotten the smaller difficulty of being able to see Draconia without being close enough to speak to her. Hermione set her bag beside his, the buckles knocking together beneath the table. Harry was grateful for that small sound of somebody remaining within reach.

Draconia reached Daphne's side. Pansy kept talking without greeting her, but Daphne moved her goblet closer and said something that lifted the corner of Draconia's mouth. Harry looked away before his attention became obvious.

An empty chair stood to Dumbledore's right, beside Snape, in the place traditionally reserved for whichever Defence professor had survived the summer.

"Who did they find this time?" Harry asked.

Seamus shrugged.

"Mum heard Dumbledore asked three people. One retired, one fled the country and one demanded danger pay."

"Which one accepted?"

"Possibly all of them."

Hermione leaned forwards.

"The position is not actually cursed."

Harry studied the chair.

"Evidence remains unfriendly."

The doors opened, and McGonagall led in the first-years. Wet robes clung to their ankles. Several stared at the enchanted ceiling, where storm clouds shifted beneath distant lightning, while candles swayed above the tables without any wind to move them.

The Sorting Hat sang about unity. Harry had begun distrusting such songs around the time the school's divisions became serious enough to require them.

Names followed one at a time, each answered by a shout from the Hat and applause from a table. Harry clapped with the others, watching children walk towards strangers who might become their closest friends, their worst enemies, or people whose names they forgot by Christmas. Emma Dobbs trembled so violently beneath the Hat that McGonagall steadied the stool.

"GRYFFINDOR!"

Hermione applauded until the girl smiled. When the final student joined Ravenclaw, Dumbledore rose.

"Welcome. Before we eat, I have only two words for you."

Harry waited.

"Tuck in."

Food appeared, and the Hall surrendered to noise. Harry filled his plate from three directions. Hermione added vegetables when she thought he was distracted; he transferred them to her plate, and she returned them. By the third exchange, Seamus was accepting bets.

At Slytherin, Draconia ate beneath observation. Theodore's place remained empty. Snape looked towards it once, then at her. She met his gaze, and whatever passed between them ended with a roll of thunder above the castle.

The Great Hall doors opened again.

A man stood beneath them in a travelling cloak the colour of old mud. Rain streamed from its hem. One hand rested on a long staff, the other near the wand strapped across his chest. His face seemed assembled from pieces that had survived separate wars: scars ran in every direction, and part of his nose was missing. One eye was small and dark, fixed on Dumbledore. The other was bright blue, perfectly round, and moving by itself.

It spun towards the students, and Harry felt it find him. The sensation was absurdly physical, as though it had passed through his robes to count the objects beneath them and examine the places recently covered by bandages. His hand began moving towards his pocket before he remembered that a retired Auror would notice him reaching for a wand.

The eye paused, then turned towards Draconia. Her fork stopped halfway to her mouth, and colour drained from her face. Harry watched her instead of the newcomer, seeing for an instant the girl from the Chamber: awake, but not entirely present, her body motionless while something else looked through her eyes.

Then she blinked and lowered her fork with perfect control. Only the hand beneath the table remained clenched.

The man crossed the Hall, each uneven step sounding differently against the stone: wood, boot, staff, boot. His magical eye inspected the ceiling, rolled towards the doors behind him, and completed another circle while the rest of his face remained still.

Dumbledore came down to meet him. They spoke too quietly for Harry to hear, then the man limped towards the empty chair and sat. Snape moved several inches away.

"That is Alastor Moody," Hermione whispered.

"You know him?"

"I have read about him."

"Of course."

"He was an Auror. Half the cells in Azkaban were filled because of his investigations."

Harry watched Moody pour water into a goblet, sniff it, and refuse to drink.

"Retired?"

"Several years ago."

"He looks relaxed."

The blue eye turned backwards into Moody's skull, apparently inspecting the wall behind him. Harry lowered his voice further.

"Very relaxed."

Dessert arrived. Moody ate none of it, drinking instead from a curved flask and watching the room in two directions at once. Harry tried to ignore him, but the sense of being observed stayed between his shoulder blades.

When the plates vanished, Dumbledore rose again, and conversation faded.

"Now that we have all been fed and watered, I have several notices for the beginning of term."

He introduced Moody first. The applause was uncertain; Moody neither stood nor acknowledged it, his ordinary eye on the table while the blue one travelled from exit to exit. Dumbledore then reminded them that the Forbidden Forest remained forbidden, that corridor magic would be punished, and that several of Fred and George's products had joined the caretaker's list of prohibited items. The twins applauded. Filch did not.

Dumbledore rested both hands on the lectern. Harry felt the Hall's attention gather even before he spoke.

"It is my great pleasure to announce that Hogwarts has been selected to host an event which has not taken place in Britain for more than a century."

Hermione sat straighter. Across the room, Draconia looked towards the staff table as though her father's former place on the board should have afforded her some warning.

"In October, we shall welcome delegations from Beauxbatons Academy of Magic and Durmstrang Institute. Together, our three schools will participate in the Triwizard Tournament."

The Hall erupted. Questions collided with shouts, older students climbed onto benches, and several people celebrated before discovering what they were celebrating. Goblets overturned. Nearly Headless Nick rose through the Gryffindor table to demand an explanation for all the yelling, which encouraged still more of it.

Draconia looked first at the staff table, then at the doors, measuring the announcement as though it might conceal another meaning. When she found Harry watching, an eyebrow lifted in silent inquiry. He shook his head. He remembered little more than a section of Hogwarts: A History Hermione had once tried to make him read during detention: three schools, three champions, three tasks, and enough deaths to end the competition.

He looked towards Hermione. She had gone pale.

Dumbledore raised a hand, and silence returned slowly.

"The Tournament was established nearly seven hundred years ago as a competition between Europe's three largest magical schools. A champion will be selected to represent each institution. Those champions will face three tasks designed to test courage, magical ability, reasoning and resourcefulness."

Several Hufflepuffs turned towards Cedric. He shook his head, ears reddening as they clapped his shoulders. Harry remembered Amos at the Cup, certain of our champion, and Cedric laughing at the words. Now the uncomfortable smile on his face made him look as though an expectation had been waiting for him all summer.

"The winning champion will receive the Triwizard Cup, one thousand Galleons and the honour of representing their school. However—"

Dumbledore's voice hardened.

"The Tournament has historically involved considerable danger. The Ministry and participating headmasters have spent the summer establishing protections intended to prevent the tragedies associated with previous competitions."

Harry watched the hands resting on the lectern. Dumbledore spoke calmly, with the assurance Harry had wanted from him since first year, but the Hall was already restless with things the announcement had offered. A thousand Galleons. A champion's name. Older students were smiling at one another as though the warnings had become part of the invitation. Harry understood the attraction well enough to distrust his own interest. Rules sounded reassuring from a table lit by candles, with dinner still warm inside him. They had sounded reassuring at the World Cup too.

"Protections established by the Ministry," Harry murmured.

Hermione heard, and her mouth tightened.

"For that reason, no student below the age of seventeen will be permitted to submit their name."

Outrage replaced excitement. Fred and George stood together.

"You don't know our birthdays!" Fred called.

"I attended your Sorting, Mr Weasley."

George sat first.

"An impartial judge will select the champions after the visiting schools arrive. Further instructions will be provided then."

Someone at Ravenclaw asked about Quidditch, and Dumbledore's expression grew regretful.

"Because the final Tournament task will require extensive preparation across the school grounds, there will be no inter-House Quidditch Cup this year."

Disappointment struck Harry before he could reason with it. Since first year, the pitch had been the place where Hogwarts became simple. A broom required balance, attention and the courage to move before fear finished its argument. Newspapers, House rivalries and the pain in his scar could do little to help or hinder a turn taken at speed.

There would be no dawn practices, no captain shouting himself hoarse from the ground, no clean exhaustion after a match. Harry stared at the lectern, trying to imagine a year without one of the few parts of school in which he knew exactly who he was supposed to be.

Across the Hall, Draconia understood his expression. Her hands made a small gesture beneath the table. You can still fly. He nodded.

Dumbledore finished the notices and dismissed them. Benches scraped, and students surged towards the doors, carrying a hundred versions of the Tournament into the corridors.

"Cedric!" someone shouted.

The Hufflepuffs closed around him, but Harry stayed seated. Three champions. Three tasks. Seventeen. The age limit should have ended his interest; instead, he wondered what the tasks would ask of them. Dumbledore had made their danger plain. Harry wondered whether volunteering changed how danger felt, whether preparation made fear easier to manage, and whether judges and rules could turn it into something unlike the threats that had found him without either.

Moody's magical eye turned towards him. This time, it stayed.

The Tournament reached Gryffindor Tower before Harry did. By the time he and Hermione entered, someone had drawn three columns on the notice board and begun taking nominations. Angelina Johnson led with seventeen votes. Fred and George shared twelve despite demanding separate consideration. Percy's name had been crossed out and replaced with TECHNICALLY GRADUATED, EMOTIONALLY PRESENT.

Conversation filled the room. Older students argued over likely spells while younger ones invented deaths with growing enthusiasm. Lee Jordan stood on a table conducting unofficial betting. The twins were debating whether an Ageing Potion constituted submitting under false pretences.

"Dumbledore said the judge would be impartial," Fred told Hermione as she passed.

"He also said students below seventeen would not be permitted to enter."

"Those statements are not contradictory."

"They will be when Professor McGonagall turns you into matching footstools."

George considered.

"Tournament-themed footstools?"

Hermione continued towards the stairs. Harry lingered by the fire until Dean waved him over.

"Would you enter if you could?"

Several conversations quietened. Harry looked at the board, grateful to whichever Gryffindor had possessed enough sense to leave his name off it. At fourteen, the suggestion would have been ridiculous.

"No," Hermione answered.

Harry turned towards her.

"I can speak."

"Then speak accurately."

Dean laughed. Seamus remained unconvinced.

"You went after a basilisk."

"It had Draconia."

"You chased a mass murderer last year."

"That situation was misunderstood."

"You fought people at the World Cup."

"They started it."

Neville lowered himself into a chair.

"That was almost an answer."

Harry looked at the board again.

"I wouldn't put my name in."

Hermione watched him.

"But?"

"There isn't a but."

"Your face has a but."

"I hate that everyone has named parts of my face."

"You were wondering about the tasks."

He sat on the arm of her chair.

"Weren't you?"

"I was wondering what safety measures could possibly make an event with a historical death toll appropriate for students."

"That sounds less fun."

"It is meant to."

The fire shifted. For an instant, green moved beneath its ordinary orange, and Harry reacted before it resolved into copper-treated wood. He gripped the chair until the carved edge bit his palm. The room vanished behind the Dark Mark over water: a serpent sliding from a skull, green light on the stream, masks turning through smoke.

Sound followed a moment later. A curse broke against Lucius's shield, Draconia shouted Harry's name, and Nott's mask struck hard beneath his knuckles. Then Hermione's fingers covered his, her thumb pressing once against them without asking the questions aloud.

Warm skin, worn upholstery, woodsmoke. Harry drew a breath and felt the chair beneath him again. The voices returned to their familiar places around the fire. Nobody else seemed to have noticed.

"I'm not entering," he said quietly.

Hermione's expression softened.

"I know."

The certainty unsettled him more than doubt would have. He had spoken honestly. The Tournament interested him because danger governed by rules seemed almost reasonable, with preparation, judges, a beginning and an end. Nothing in his life usually announced itself so politely. Curiosity was easier to admit than a wish to participate, and he repeated that distinction until he trusted it.

The room gradually emptied. Hermione confiscated two bottles of Ageing Potion by threatening to inform McGonagall before the twins perfected the dosage. Neville rescued his cactus from Crookshanks. Dean erased Harry's name after Seamus added it "hypothetically."

Near midnight, Harry and Hermione went to an unused classroom on the fifth floor. Six desks stood beside a wardrobe of cracked maps, and a wizard in a portrait slept beneath an enormous red hat. Hermione locked the door and drew the curtains while Harry took out his small mirror and tapped its corner three times.

The glass turned green. Draconia appeared with her face too close to the surface.

"You're late."

Harry leaned back.

"Good evening."

"We agreed upon half past eleven."

"Fred and George attempted to become seventeen through experimental poisoning."

"Did it work?"

"They became purple."

"Promising."

Hermione sat beside him.

"Where are you?"

Draconia moved the mirror, revealing green bed curtains and wandlight casting shadows over the canopy. The rest of the Slytherin dormitory lay in darkness.

"Pansy is asleep. Daphne is pretending to be."

From beyond the frame came Daphne's voice.

"Your whisper carries."

Draconia ignored her.

"Theodore still hasn't arrived."

Harry remembered the place at the Slytherin table.

"Did anyone say why?"

"Professor Snape announced that family circumstances delayed his return. Pansy claims his mother has taken him abroad."

"Do you believe her?" Hermione asked.

"Pansy believes being told something confidentially makes it true."

"His father nearly killed us," Harry said. "He might not know anything."

Draconia looked at him.

"I know."

The answer mattered. She could have held Theodore responsible through his blood alone; her family had spent generations allowing names to determine guilt. Saying otherwise was easy enough while his place stood empty. Harry thought she understood it would become harder if he returned.

"What did Snape say to you?"

"Nothing yet."

"He watched you throughout dinner."

"Professor Snape watches everyone as though anticipating a confession."

"Usually mine."

"You are unusually confessable."

Hermione shifted closer to the glass.

"What do Slytherins think about the Tournament?"

"That Durmstrang will win, Beauxbatons will complain and Hogwarts will embarrass itself by choosing someone sentimental."

"Cedric?"

"Nobody has said his name."

"They will," Harry replied.

Draconia studied him.

"You think he should enter."

"I think he'd be good."

"That was not the question."

"You sound like Hermione."

"Take it back."

Hermione folded her arms.

"Would you enter if you were seventeen?"

Harry looked between them.

"Why is everyone asking me?"

"Because you looked interested," Draconia said.

"It's interesting."

"So are venomous plants," Hermione replied.

"That does not mean I want to become one."

Neither looked reassured. Harry leaned towards the mirror.

"I cannot enter. I am not going to try. I am not drinking anything the twins give me, crossing an age line or persuading a house-elf to submit my name."

Draconia narrowed her eyes.

"You considered the house-elf."

"Only while listing things I won't do."

"That is how your face works," Hermione told her.

"I can hear you."

"Good."

The portrait wizard snored loudly. All three fell silent, looking at one another through the glass. The mirror brought Draconia's face close without bringing her out of the green curtains. Harry and Hermione remained beneath a faded Ravenclaw map, with two Houses and hundreds of feet of stone between them and their friend. Hermione's enchantment gave them a way to refuse the separation, however briefly.

"Breakfast," Harry said.

"The library," Draconia replied.

"And class," Hermione added. "We have Potions together before lunch."

Draconia's expression placed Potions on a separate emotional scale.

"Sleep," Hermione ordered.

"Your authority remains unrecognised."

"Good night, Draco."

"Good night."

Draconia looked back at Harry. Something from the conservatory passed between them without words.

"Good night, Harry."

"Night."

The glass cleared. Hermione stayed seated.

"She's frightened."

Harry looked at his reflection.

"I know."

"Did something happen at the cottage?"

Whatever he might have said, the truth belonged to Draconia.

"Ask her when she can answer."

Hermione watched him, then nodded.

"All right."

She opened the door and left. Harry remained for another minute with the empty mirror, seeing a tired face, thinner than it had been in June and older around the eyes than fourteen should look. Moody had studied it across the Hall. Half the school had done the same when the Tournament was mentioned, as though surviving danger made him hungry for more.

He had no easy way to explain how survival could make a person want rules badly enough to mistake them for safety.

He took out the second mirror. Sirius answered on the fifth tap, his face upside down.

"Harry?"

"Turn it around."

"I know how mirrors work."

The view rotated through a stretch of ceiling before settling on Sirius at the cottage kitchen table. Papers covered the surface, and Remus slept behind him with his head on his arms. Sirius looked alarmed.

"What happened?"

"Nothing."

"You called after midnight."

"You said I didn't have to wait for an emergency."

"I did." Concern gave way to confusion. "I was not prepared for you to listen."

Harry smiled.

"Hogwarts is hosting the Triwizard Tournament."

Sirius's expression changed.

"It is what?"

"Beauxbatons and Durmstrang arrive in October. Three champions. Three tasks. No one under seventeen can enter."

"Good."

"You didn't pause."

"I know you."

"That is becoming a popular excuse."

Sirius glanced towards Remus.

"Who is teaching Defence?"

"Alastor Moody."

This surprised him differently.

"Mad-Eye accepted?"

"He arrived late and stared at everyone."

"Then it is probably him."

"You know him?"

"By reputation. Met him twice during the first war. He searched me for concealed wands at my own parents' dining table."

"Did you have one?"

"Three."

"Reasonable."

"He won't agree." Sirius grew serious. "Moody sees traps inside teacups and occasionally finds them. Listen to what he teaches. Do not imitate his drinking habits, accept unlabelled objects or surprise him from behind."

"Specific."

"His dustbins once attacked a neighbour."

Harry glanced towards the classroom door.

"His eye watched Draco and me during dinner."

"It watches everything. That is the point."

"Right."

Sirius heard the uncertainty beneath it.

"If it becomes more than watching, tell me."

"I will."

"Before confronting him."

"That seems restrictive."

"Harry."

"Before."

His godfather relaxed a little.

"How was the feast?"

The ordinary question caught Harry unprepared.

"Good. Hermione tried to give me vegetables."

"Violence on the first night."

"I defended myself."

"Proud of you."

Harry told him about Hagrid's coat, Neville's cactus and the Hat's song. Sirius listened as though each detail deserved the attention he had given the Tournament. That made it easier to keep talking. Harry found himself remembering small things he would usually have left out, certain now that somebody wanted to hear them.

Sirius interrupted only to ask whether Draconia had really fallen asleep on his shoulder.

"Hermione told you that?" Harry demanded.

"Your expression told me."

"Nobody's face does that much talking."

"Yours has always lacked discretion."

Remus made a sleepy noise that might have been agreement. Harry felt his ears warm, and Sirius noticed. With restraint that deserved historical recognition, he changed the subject.

By the time Harry ended the call, the castle felt less far from home.

Morning restored Hogwarts through noise. Pipes complained in the dormitory walls, curtains opened, and boys searched for socks, timetables and homework they had yet to be assigned. The bell rang before Harry finished fastening his robes and continued while Neville retrieved the cactus from beneath Dean's bed.

Harry and Hermione reached the library seven minutes late. Draconia sat at their usual table with three newspapers open in front of her. The Prophet declared World Cup arrests "imminent." A French paper showed Lucius and Narcissa lowering the Roberts family, while a German one appeared to accuse the British Ministry of confusing optimism with policy.

"Anything?" Harry asked.

"Nothing about Nott. Nothing about the second set of footprints. Crouch has issued a statement confirming his wand was stolen but refuses to identify how."

Hermione set down her bag.

"His son."

Draconia looked up. Together, they returned to Winky's words, the question narrowing all three papers to the space between them.

"Barty Crouch Junior died in Azkaban," Draconia said.

"Hermione said."

"Mother mentioned the trial once. Father refused to discuss it."

Hermione took a folder from her bag.

"I asked Dad to visit the Oxford magical archive before we left. They have copies of historical editions the Prophet no longer keeps publicly indexed."

"You sent your father to investigate a dead Death Eater?" Harry asked.

"He visited a library."

"A dangerous family habit."

"He found three articles."

She spread them across the table. The first photograph showed a boy barely older than Cedric being led into a courtroom, dark hair over his eyes as he shouted towards someone outside the frame. Two Aurors held his arms. He fought with frantic, useless strength, searching the benches each time the image repeated until he found the same unseen person and began shouting again.

MINISTRY HERO'S SON AMONG FOUR SENTENCED

The next article recorded his death less than a year later in six paragraphs: three about prison policy, two praising his father's refusal to seek special treatment, and one about the dead boy. Even that contained no words from anybody who had known him. The third article praised Crouch Senior for continuing his work despite personal tragedy.

Draconia read each twice.

Around them, the library continued its ordinary morning. Chairs scraped softly between shelves, a quill scratched at the next table, and Madam Pince returned a stack of books to their places with small, disapproving taps. The reproduced photograph kept struggling beneath Harry's hand. He thought of the papers that had once called Sirius a murderer, then found space to celebrate his innocence without dwelling on the years between. The same tidy certainty lived in these older headlines. Harry wanted to know what had happened to the boy in them, however little he knew about the man that boy might have become.

"Where is his mother?" Harry asked.

Hermione turned the third page.

"Ill, according to this. She died shortly after him."

"Convenient," Draconia said.

Hermione looked at her.

"I mean for the story. A son dies in Azkaban. His mother follows from grief. His father returns to work. Every person who might dispute the Ministry's version disappears, and Mr Crouch becomes admirable for remaining."

Harry studied the boy's unheard plea in the photograph.

"You think the death was faked."

"I think wealthy families have faked cleaner things for worse reasons."

"Do wizarding prisons release bodies?" Hermione asked.

Neither knew. She wrote the question beneath her notes.

"Winky served the Crouch family," Draconia said. "She could refer to any younger male relative as young master."

"She could," Hermione agreed.

"But you don't believe that," Harry said.

"No."

Harry folded the first article, stopping before the crease reached the boy's face. If Crouch Junior lived, Winky had known, remaining frightened and loyal until dismissal prevented her explaining. "Some arrive in goblets."

It moved to Harry.

"Food."

Pansy shifted away from her breakfast crumbs.

Draconia's quill stopped. Harry remembered Quirrell drinking unicorn blood, Polyjuice changing faces, and a hooded stranger offering Hagrid a dragon egg after asking just the right question about Fluffy.

"I was."

"And powdered acromantula venom."

She held up a colourless vial.

"Unsupervised."

Harry faced forwards.

"Yes, Professor."

Neither moved.

She kept her expression neutral.

"He saved four people."

The question escaped before caution could catch it. He became perfectly still.

"Professor."

Hermione began walking.

"No."

"No. He isn't."

They reached Charms twelve minutes late. Flitwick removed three points from each House and asked no questions.

Their first Defence lesson came after lunch. Moody's classroom had changed overnight: cheerful diagrams were gone, replaced by maps of Britain dotted with red pins, sketches of wand grips and photographs of dark objects in evidence boxes. Three cracked Sneakoscopes stood on the windowsill. Beside the blackboard, a Secrecy Sensor's gold antenna trembled whenever somebody whispered.

Dust, metal and the bitter medicinal smell from Moody's flask hung in the room. The chairs faced forwards, but little else suggested comfort. Maps had been pinned at standing height, and a duelling dummy waited in the back corner with a curse-blackened chest. Three words scored the slate in heavy chalk.

RECOGNISE. SURVIVE. REPORT.

Harry disliked the order. Whoever had written it seemed to think the first two might fail before anybody could attempt the third.

The curtains were closed. Moody entered from his office when the last student sat, his staff striking the floor and ending conversation. The blue eye moved over robes, bags and desks, then turned downwards to stare through the wood in front of Dean.

Harry settled his bag beneath his chair without taking his attention from the front. This was still a classroom. He recognised the marks ink bottles had left on the desks and the draft that found its way beneath the door whenever the corridor emptied. Yet all those familiar things seemed to have changed their purpose around Moody's equipment. The windows were covered, the dummy watched from its corner, and an instrument reacted to every whisper. Harry could not decide whether the room had been made safer or simply better prepared to announce that it was unsafe.

"Hand it over."

Dean froze.

"Professor?"

"Left pocket. Joke wand."

Dean produced it. Moody caught it without looking and snapped it in half; the wand became a rubber haddock. One corner of his mouth shifted.

"Weak concealment."

He threw it into the bin and fixed his ordinary eye on the class.

"Alastor Moody. I have agreed to teach for one year because Dumbledore asked, because retirement is tedious and because most of you would not recognise a curse until it finished introducing itself."

Nobody wrote until Hermione recovered and began moving her quill. The blue eye spun towards her parchment.

"Fourth year. Ministry guidelines recommend counter-curses, defensive theory and an introduction to hostile enchantment."

He limped towards the desk.

"Guidelines are written by people who arrive after attacks and describe what survivors should have done."

At another time, Harry might have admired the cynicism. After the Cup, it sounded like a fact several officials had demonstrated personally. Hermione's quill paused over guidelines. Draconia had not begun writing; she watched Moody's wand hand and the eye that had already inspected every pocket in the room.

He opened a drawer containing three jars, each with a large black spider inside. Neville's breathing changed as the first jar reached the desk.

"Who can name the Unforgivable Curses?"

Hermione's hand rose, but Moody ignored it. His eye settled on Draconia.

"Malfoy."

Heads turned, and her spine straightened.

"The Imperius Curse. The Cruciatus Curse. The Killing Curse."

"Penalties?"

"A life sentence in Azkaban for using any of them upon another human being."

"If convicted."

The addition entered the room like cold air. Quills resumed moving, but Harry kept his hands flat on the desk. The lesson had yet to become dangerous, and his body seemed unable to accept that distinction. His shoulders held themselves ready for impact. Each click of the Secrecy Sensor sounded like a wand drawn behind him.

Moody opened the jar as the spider tried to climb its walls.

"Imperio."

It stopped, then leapt onto his palm. Several students recoiled. He placed it on the desk, where it stood on two legs, turned slowly, and tapped the remaining six in a rhythm against the wood. Laughter travelled through the room. Harry stayed silent, and so did Draconia.

"Looks harmless," Moody said. "That is its advantage."

He made the spider jump through a ring of his fingers.

"No pain. No struggle. The victim may feel happier obeying than they have felt making a decision in years."

Draconia gripped the edge of her desk. From the row behind, Harry moved his shoe until it touched her heel. She pressed back without turning, giving the smallest answer she could offer without the class seeing. He kept his foot there while the spider bowed and spun through movements it had never chosen.

Moody's blue eye dropped towards their shoes. The ordinary one stayed on the desk.

"Some victims understand control only after it ends. Some remember every command. Some spend the rest of their lives wondering which thoughts belonged to them."

Draconia's heel grew rigid. Harry knew which words had hurt. She had described the months after the diary in much the same way, speaking into the dark because looking at him would have made honesty impossible. Some replies remained clear in her memory. Other hours survived only in things people told her she had done.

He pressed his shoe more firmly against hers. Here. Now.

"Professor," Hermione said.

Moody looked towards her.

"Is that necessary?"

"Knowing?"

"Making it perform."

He released the curse. The spider collapsed onto all eight legs and fled towards the edge, where he caught it beneath the jar.

"You object to discomfort, Granger?"

"I object to treating suffering as entertainment."

The Secrecy Sensor trembled violently in the silence. Moody studied her.

"Good."

Hermione blinked.

"People who enjoy these lessons learn the wrong part."

The laughter was gone. Students who had smiled now examined their notes. Moody replaced the jar, his magical eye turning towards Draconia.

"And people who already understand control should remember that knowledge makes them harder to catch twice."

Her face emptied. Harry felt the change through her heel and wondered how Moody knew.

There were reasonable explanations. Professors had witnessed the basilisk and the diary's destruction, the governors had received a report, and Draconia had spent weeks in the hospital wing. A new teacher might have been briefed. Harry could arrange the facts neatly enough, but they did little to ease the feeling in his stomach.

"Longbottom."

Neville stopped. Recognition moved across Moody's scarred face, carrying something older than a teacher learning a student's name. It belonged to a history Neville had not offered.

"Name the second curse."

Neville's lips parted without sound. Hermione raised her hand again, but Moody continued watching him.

"The Cruciatus Curse," Neville whispered.

"Effect?"

"Pain."

Moody took out the spider.

"Crucio."

Its legs folded inwards so violently that the joints seemed ready to split. It rolled across the desk, struck an ink bottle and twisted again. There was no sound, and somehow that made the movement worse. Pain filled its whole body without leaving any place to escape.

Neville made a small, broken noise through his nose. He looked past the spider and the classroom towards something Harry could not see. Sweat gathered at his temples, and his mouth formed a word that never emerged. His knuckles whitened against the desk.

He had measured the distance before realising he was doing it: seven feet, three desks, Moody's bad leg beside the aisle and his staff compensating. Harry might reach his wand before Moody turned, if the magical eye did not count.

It always counted.

"Stop," Hermione said.

She spoke quietly. Moody released the curse, leaving the spider curled on the wood, but Neville continued staring.

"Mr Longbottom."

He did not answer. Hermione crossed to him despite Moody standing at the desk and took his wrist.

"Look at me."

Eventually, his eyes moved.

"Breathe."

Moody watched without giving anything away.

"Five points to Gryffindor."

Hermione's head snapped towards him.

"For what?"

"Recognising when the person matters more than the lesson."

The answer checked her anger without resolving it. She returned to her seat only once Neville nodded. Moody covered the second spider.

"The Cruciatus Curse requires intent. Anger may produce pain. Hatred may produce more. Neither guarantees control. The caster must value suffering as an outcome rather than an instrument."

His eye passed over them.

"Remember that when someone claims they lost their temper."

Harry looked at Neville. The warning might prove useful, but its cost remained written on his face.

He thought of the boy who had guarded a biting cactus all the way from London, apologising whenever it threatened someone, and who had found a place for it beneath the dinner bench. Neville had seemed himself through all of that, worried and patient and ready to rescue the plant from the consequences of its own behaviour. Now he sat so carefully that Harry wondered whether any movement might undo him. Harry wanted to say something across the desks, but the lesson continued before he could find words that would not draw every eye to Neville again.

"Avada Kedavra."

Green light filled the room, and Harry heard his mother scream.

It rose inside him from somewhere scar and memory had never separated. The light swallowed desks, stone and afternoon. He smelled cold dust and smoke in a room he could not consciously remember. Lily begged Voldemort to take her instead. A high voice laughed, a body fell, and an infant cried without understanding what had been removed from the world.

The spider struck the desk almost silently. Harry could not reconcile that small sound with death; something so final should have announced what it took.

Hunger.

Hermione touched his sleeve.

"Why were you waiting?"

Harry told them about Theodore. Neither interrupted, and by the end Remus looked thoughtful.

"Theodore may know where his father is. He may also be a fourteen-year-old removed from his school because adults made him dangerous to be near."

"That's what Harry said," Sirius replied.

Harry stared.

"You sound surprised."

"Proud. Deeply confused, but proud."

The dinner bell rang below, and Harry stood.

"I have to go."

"Call tomorrow," Sirius said.

"If nothing happens?"

"Especially then."

Harry ended the connection and tucked the mirror beneath his robes. At the dormitory door, he realised Sirius had never asked whether he could handle the Tournament. He had simply reminded him he need not face school alone.

The Slytherin common room quietened differently from the rest of Hogwarts. Noise withdrew behind curtains, into lowered voices and corners chosen for privacy. By midnight, it looked empty while remaining full of people aware of one another.

Draconia entered alone. Green light moved over the ceiling, and the lake pressed black against the windows until silver fish turned together and flashed like coins falling through deep water. Usually, the room soothed her. Water softened the noise from above, making even storms seem distant. Tonight, the shadows kept resembling a round blue eye before dissolving into fish.

Snape stood by the cold fireplace, having finished with the prefects. Two seventh-years left through the boys' passage looking as though their privileges had become responsibilities without warning. His gaze found Draconia, briefly touching the hand Harry and Hermione had held outside Defence. No mark remained there, but she could still feel Harry's fingers closing first, then Hermione's covering both.

"Curfew is in three minutes."

"I am inside the common room."

"A rare triumph. Preserve it."

He swept towards the exit.

"Professor."

Snape stopped. She considered asking what Moody had read, whether Dumbledore distributed the Chamber reports, and whether a man familiar with spying found it strange that the Auror watched Harry as though everybody else existed for comparison.

He waited. She could not summon enough trust to ask.

"Nothing."

His face suggested he recognised the lie and expected it to be better constructed next time.

"Three minutes, Miss Malfoy."

He left, and she descended to the dormitory. Pansy's curtains were closed. Millicent snored strongly enough to disturb her canopy. Daphne sat brushing her hair before the window, green lines from the lake crossing her face.

White paper had been pinned to Draconia's curtain.

She stopped. For an ugly instant, another object waited beside her bed: dark green leather, silver initials, pages blank enough to seem harmless. Her wand reached her hand beneath her sleeve before thought caught up, its point directed at the paper from a distance.

There was no name. A serpent had been drawn in dark ink, its head several inches from its body. The warning sent to the cottage had been copied crudely by someone who understood its meaning without possessing the seal.

Daphne saw it in her mirror.

"That wasn't there before dinner."

Draconia removed it and checked the ink for curses twice. The second inspection was unnecessary, but her hand performed it anyway.

"Did you see anyone near my bed?"

"Millicent came back for a book. Two third-years were showing Pansy their new dress robes."

Behind the curtains, Pansy's breathing changed. Draconia looked towards her.

"You are awake."

Silence answered.

"Your false sleeping lacks conviction."

The curtains opened. Pansy sat against her pillows, knees beneath the blanket.

"I didn't put it there."

"I did not say you did."

"You thought it."

"I thought you knew something."

Pansy looked at the drawing.

"It was one of the third-years. I don't know which. They were laughing when they left."

Daphne set down her brush.

"You could have removed it."

"And have them tell everyone I'm defending her?"

"That would be terrible," Draconia said.

Pansy flushed.

"My mother says I'm not to associate with your family until your father remembers himself."

"How fortunate that I am not my family."

"That isn't what she means."

"I know precisely what she means."

Draconia crossed to the iron stove beside the washroom. Its fire had gone out. She touched her wand to the page, and blue flame consumed the serpent from its severed neck outwards. Her hand shook once. Both girls noticed.

She expected the old instinct to conceal the movement before they could assign it meaning. Instead, she remembered Harry sitting among broken glass with his wand out of reach. You don't have to be brave with me.

Pansy had earned no such trust. Daphne had earned some without asking for it. This room was far from the conservatory, but Draconia let the tremor exist.

"Are you frightened?" Pansy asked.

There was no mockery in it, which made the question harder to answer. A month earlier, Draconia would have lied automatically. Fear became information in Slytherin, and information became currency. She knew how to end the conversation: Of them? Hardly. I have survived worse. Concern yourself with your own curtains. Each answer held enough truth to convince.

"Yes."

Pansy stared. Daphne rose, crossed the room and closed the gap in Draconia's bed curtains, restoring a little privacy without pretending nothing had happened. Pansy looked at her blanket.

The fabric settled with a faint rustle. Such a small alteration should have mattered less, but Draconia no longer felt displayed against the white paper's empty place. Daphne had neither asked for an explanation nor announced her loyalty for the room to judge. She had noticed something that needed doing and done it. Draconia looked at the curtain while the last of the blue flame faded from her wand, allowing herself a breath she had been holding since she entered.

"Father says Malfoy will lose his influence with the board."

"Possibly."

"And the Ministry may search the Manor."

"They already have."

"What will you do?"

Draconia remembered the newspaper photograph, her father bleeding beneath the Mark, and Harry joining her on the platform while everybody watched.

"Attend class."

Pansy frowned.

"That cannot be all."

"Tomorrow I intend to eat breakfast."

"Draconia."

"Then I will go to the library, where Harry will pretend he completed the assigned reading and Hermione will expose him before he finishes the first lie."

The picture steadied her. Harry would arrive with his tie crooked and his hair defying every civilised arrangement. Hermione would have marked the pages already. Tea would appear beside Draconia without a question, because they had begun learning which questions gave her room to refuse things she needed.

Daphne returned to her bed, the corner of her mouth lifting. Draconia folded her robes over the chair.

"After that, I will decide what the next hour requires."

Pansy looked uncertain.

"You make everything difficult."

"Only the things people wish were simple."

She closed her curtains. This time, Draconia felt less rejected by it.

On her pillow lay the letter Lucius had sent that afternoon. She had read it three times, but its final line remained visible beneath her wandlight.

Choosing correctly does not make the consequences less real. It makes them worth bearing.

Below, in smaller writing:

Eat more.

Despite herself, she smiled and placed it beneath her pillow. Outside, something enormous passed between the dormitory and the distant surface, swallowing the silver fish in its shadow. Then it moved on, and the water grew green again.

September settled around the castle. The World Cup left the front page before it left conversation. No arrests were announced, and the Ministry panel scheduled its first meeting for November. Lucius wrote that the Manor's wards were almost complete, ending each letter to Draconia with the same two words. She complained about eat more, and obeyed.

Theodore stayed away. His empty chair grew ordinary enough that Harry disliked himself whenever he stopped noticing it.

Defence remained the school's most discussed subject. Moody produced written consent forms as promised. Most students signed; some wanted to discover whether they could resist an Unforgivable, while others seemed afraid refusal would make them look frightened.

Draconia returned hers blank. Harry placed his beside it, equally empty.

"You could learn whether you can resist it," she said.

"I can learn with you."

The guardedness around her eyes eased.

"Sentimental."

"Contagious."

Hermione redesigned the partnered exercises, using layered suggestion charms, moving targets and enchanted instructions that contradicted themselves. Moody reviewed her plan, removed two safeguards and awarded fifteen points to Gryffindor. Harry restored one safeguard when his back was turned. Moody knew, but said nothing.

Outside lessons, Harry flew. With no Cup to prepare for, the pitch remained open at weekends, and he took the Firebolt above the empty stands until the castle dwindled behind rain and the world reduced itself to movement.

Clear afternoons were rare. He usually flew under low cloud, damp hair blown into his eyes and rain needling his face whichever way he turned. He went anyway. At fifty feet, he had to attend to his breathing; at a hundred, the next turn needed all of him. Above the western hoops, the cold and wind left little room for green fire, Ministry reports or an eye that could follow him through wood.

The Firebolt answered the smallest adjustment of his hands. Harry learned the wind by the way it caught his sleeves, easing one shoulder into a turn before accelerating out of it. Water gathered on the rims of his glasses and broke away when he dived. Below, the rows of seats gleamed empty and the white lines blurred into grass. He liked having no crowd to interpret the flight, no score to protect. For these afternoons, moving well could be its own reason to move.

Hermione called it avoidance. Harry called it exercise. Draconia considered it safer than his usual methods and therefore worthy of encouragement.

Cedric joined him near the end of September, emerging from beneath the Hufflepuff stand on a battered Cleansweep. A yellow scarf was wound twice around his throat, and his expression asked permission before he spoke.

"Pitch occupied?"

Harry glanced at the thousands of empty seats.

"Terribly. You may have to book ahead."

Cedric kicked off. For ten minutes, they flew separately: Harry climbing and diving, Cedric circling the hoops through turns tight enough to snap his robes behind him. Then he cut across Harry's path, glanced back, and leaned forwards.

They began racing without agreeing to it. The Firebolt won the first straight so easily that Harry laughed into the wind, but Cedric made no attempt to match its speed. He dropped beneath the lowest hoop, hid his turn behind a post and emerged heading across the pitch before Harry realised the course had changed. Harry rolled, lost several seconds, and chased.

They swept through the outer hoops, around the towers and between two sets of goalposts from opposite directions. Cedric made the air smaller, choosing corners that punished Harry's speed and approaching turns from angles he had not expected. He reached the centre hoop first by less than a broom's length.

Harry followed half a second later and almost collided with him. Both swerved. Cedric shouted something the wind stole, and Harry's shoulder clipped the hoop, spinning him through a full turn before he recovered. For a breath, sky and pitch traded places. He hauled the Firebolt level, his startled laugh breaking into the rain.

They landed laughing too hard to speak.

Hermione and Draconia waited in the lowest stand beneath a shared umbrella. Charmed against the wind, it had responded by rotating slowly above them. Hermione held a book while steadying the handle; Draconia watched with a wand across her knees and the controlled neutrality she adopted when Harry did something idiotic in the air. Cho had arrived during the race and sat a row behind them, her broom against the railing. She applauded as Cedric approached.

"That final turn was cheating," Harry said.

Cedric wiped his face.

"There were no rules."

"There was a course."

"There was a pitch."

"You sound like him," Hermione told Harry without looking up.

"Whose side are you on?"

"The side which recognises that neither of you established a finish line."

Draconia looked at his muddy shoulder.

"Or a survival requirement."

"I survived."

"Again, an observation after the event."

Cedric leaned his broom against the railing.

"Do they attend every practice?"

"It wasn't practice," Harry said.

"Then I find their presence more concerning."

Hermione turned a page.

"We were reading elsewhere until Draconia decided elsewhere lacked an adequate view of Harry falling."

"I said he would fly better if observed."

"You said someone ought to be present to identify the body."

"Motivation takes many forms."

Harry climbed into the stand and sat between them before the argument became historical. Draconia gave him a dry cloth. Hermione moved her bag out of reach of his wet robes, then warmed his sleeves with a charm. Cedric watched with an expression Harry distrusted.

Rain drummed against the turning umbrella, occasionally shaking a spill of water from its edge. Harry rubbed his hair with the cloth and left it worse than before. Warmth spread through his cuffs while his boots remained thoroughly muddy, and the satisfaction of the race settled into something quieter. The five of them had gathered for no better reason than flying and the likelihood of somebody needing an umbrella. After the first weeks of term, Harry found he liked that reason very much.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"That was very clearly something."

"I was thinking your life appears thoroughly supervised."

"Against my wishes."

Draconia took back the cloth and wiped rain from Harry's glasses with one corner.

"His wishes have repeatedly proved an inadequate basis for policy."

Her fingers were suddenly close to his face, and Harry went still. She seemed to notice a fraction later. The cloth paused against a lens as her eyes met his; then Hermione shut her book with a decisive thump.

"You missed a spot."

Draconia removed the glasses, cleaned both lenses with meticulous hostility, and returned them. Cedric studied the pitch. Cho became very interested in the rain.

"You should enter," Harry told him.

Cedric looked towards the castle.

"The Tournament?"

"Unless there's another competition everyone keeps asking you about."

"Dad wants me to."

"What do you want?"

Cedric rubbed water from his broom handle.

"To know whether I'd be chosen."

Harry understood that too well.

"Different question."

Cedric glanced at him.

"Who told you that?"

"Moody."

"Disturbing."

"Usually."

Behind them, Cho's umbrella had turned inside out, and she struggled to restore it with Hermione's help. Draconia declined on the grounds that a properly made umbrella should possess standards.

"I want to enter," Cedric admitted. "Not because Dad expects it. Not only because of that."

"Then you should."

The amusement left Cedric's face as he rested both hands on his broom.

"What if the judge chooses me because of my father?"

"Can it?"

"No idea. Everyone says it's impartial, which usually means nobody knows what it wants."

Harry thought of the Sorting Hat, the only impartial magical judge he knew. It had considered Slytherin while he sat beneath it, frightened and uncertain, before choosing Gryffindor for him.

"Then it might choose you because you're good."

"That answer lacks your usual suspicion."

"Hermione has enough for both of us."

She looked up from beneath the umbrella.

"I heard that."

"See?"

"Hermione will provide the historical death statistics," Cedric said.

"She has charts."

"Of course she does."

They carried their brooms from the pitch together. At the changing-room doors, Cedric caught Harry's arm.

"The Cup. I never thanked you properly."

"You did."

"I thanked you for helping Eleanor. I didn't thank you for coming back when you could have run."

Harry glanced towards Hermione and Draconia. They had gone a few steps ahead, but both slowed in the unmistakable manner of people trying not to listen.

"You came back too."

"She was one of our first-years."

"Exactly."

Cedric studied him, then nodded as though Harry had answered more than he intended.

"If I enter, you'll help me train?"

"I'm fourteen."

"You've survived more forms of magical violence than most of the seventh years."

"That is a terrible qualification."

"Still counts."

Harry looked back at the empty pitch, where rain silvered the hoops they had raced through.

"All right."

Cedric offered his hand, and Harry took it. The promise seemed easy then: a few afternoons on the pitch, useful spells, and Hermione supplying more research than either of them had requested.

By October, Tournament rumours filled every corridor. Durmstrang students had become seven feet tall and travelled with trained wolves. Beauxbatons taught wandless magic before breakfast and refused anybody who could not descend a staircase elegantly. Hermione attempted corrections until she realised accuracy diminished nobody's enthusiasm.

A notice appeared announcing the delegations' arrival.

30 OCTOBER — LESSONS END AT THREE-THIRTY. STUDENTS WILL ASSEMBLE BEFORE THE CASTLE AT FIVE.

Another confirmed that the champions would be selected the following evening. Harry read both between Hermione and Draconia.

"Cedric will enter."

"So will half the seventh years," Hermione said.

"Only one will be chosen."

Draconia watched him instead of the notice.

"You remain fourteen."

"I checked this morning."

"And?"

"Still not entering."

"Good."

They went to dinner. Moody stood on the landing above them, his back to the corridor. The magical eye followed Harry until the Great Hall doors closed.
