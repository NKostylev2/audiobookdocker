The shortbread had been taken into a confidential meeting. Harry discovered this when he opened the pantry, found Kingsley and Lucius studying a folded map, and was politely directed back into the kitchen without so much as a biscuit.

A week earlier, he could have crossed the ground floor without asking anyone to move a ward. Now even the places where Hawthorn kept its food seemed to have acquired secrets. There had been fewer closed doors when he returned at the end of July. Hawthorn preferred gaps. Its oldest doors had swollen in their frames and required a shoulder to close; its newest opened whenever the pipes knocked, as though startled by the noise. The kitchen door had never possessed a latch. Sirius's bedroom stood open unless he was changing, Remus's unless the moon was near, and Harry's because after Privet Drive he found he slept better when the corridor remained visible.

The Order had already been gathering at Hawthorn before Harry came back. Now the meetings grew longer, the visitors more frequent, and the rooms required for them multiplied.

The dining room had been the first room placed under a privacy ward. Harry discovered it while carrying a stack of plates back from the kitchen and had to leave them on the hall cabinet until the meeting ended.

Wards settled over its frame in thin blue lines. A brass phoenix appeared above the handle, no larger than a thumbnail, and turned its head towards anyone who approached. When a meeting began, the bird folded its wings and every sound inside disappeared.

The library followed. Then Sirius's study, though Sirius argued that a room containing three broken broomsticks, a dismantled wireless and no desk did not qualify as a study. By Wednesday, even the pantry had hosted a private conversation between Kingsley Shacklebolt and Lucius Malfoy. Harry found that one especially offensive.

"It has biscuits," he told Hermione.

"I did tell you to get them before he arrived," Hermione said, pushing her own plate towards him. "Here. I kept two before Sirius found the tin."

"Then they shouldn't put intelligence beside the shortbread."

They sat at the kitchen table beneath the remains of his birthday banner. Nobody had removed it because Sirius claimed taking decorations down was how people encouraged another birthday to arrive. Two letters addressed in Ministry green lay between them. A third had been opened and pinned to the wall beneath Fudge's statement. Draconia stood before it with her arms folded.

"Read the final paragraph again," she said.

"I've read it four times," Harry replied.

"I know. Read the part about communication this time. It's slipped between the harmless-looking bits."

"Has that happened with any Ministry document?"

"Not so far. I'd still like to know how much they're trying to hide in one paragraph."

Harry lifted the parchment. The ink re-formed into the large, careful script used for announcements intended to sound less threatening than their contents.

In light of recent disruption, unverified claims and concerns raised regarding student safety, the Ministry for Magic is pleased to announce the appointment of Senior Undersecretary Dolores Jane Umbridge as Special Commissioner for Educational Standards at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. The Commissioner will observe instruction, review curriculum, assess safeguarding procedures and advise upon student welfare, communication and organised extracurricular activity.

Harry lowered it.

"Still awful."

Hermione had copied the paragraph into a notebook and divided it into separate powers. "Observe instruction means she can sit in classes. Review curriculum means she can control what teachers are allowed to teach. Student welfare is broad enough to justify almost anything."

"Communication," Draconia said. "And organised extracurricular activity."

"Letters and clubs," Harry translated.

"Witnesses and meetings," Draconia corrected.

The two unopened envelopes bore Harry's name and Cedric's. Kingsley had brought them from Amelia's office, where correspondence connected to the inquiry was being collected without giving the Ministry Hawthorn's address. Cedric had asked for his to be left here until his next visit. Amos, apparently, was composing another reply several feet longer than the last. Harry broke the green seal upon his.

"You should let Draconia check it," Hermione said.

"If Umbridge has cursed me through official stationery, at least the paperwork will be simple."

The parchment unrolled.

Dear Mr Potter,

I trust that you are recovering comfortably under the supervision now considered most appropriate to your circumstances.

Harry stopped.

"She made guardianship sound like a medical restraint."

"Keep reading. I want to hear whether she actually asks for anything, or only implies we've already agreed."

In advance of the coming school year, I wish to assure you that the Ministry's review has been designed with the protection of all students in mind. You should not feel burdened by the need to repeat distressing or disputed accounts to your peers. Questions concerning the events of 24 June should be directed to the Commissioner's office, where qualified adults may ensure that no student is unnecessarily alarmed.

You will be pleased to know that reasonable measures will be introduced to protect your privacy and support your continued adjustment.

Yours sincerely,

Dolores Jane UmbridgeSenior Undersecretary to the MinisterSpecial Commissioner for Educational Standards

Harry let the letter settle upon the table. The porridge spoon clicked against the side of Hermione's bowl, then stopped; Draconia was still staring at the second paragraph, her lips moving as she read it again. Harry read the second paragraph again.

"She's banning me from talking about it."

"She hasn't said ban," Hermione replied.

"Reasonable measures," Draconia said. "She has left herself a great deal of room to decide what those are."

Harry turned the parchment over. The back was blank.

"Can I write back?"

"Not while you're angry," Hermione said.

"Draft it, if you must," Draconia added. "We can decide whether it's worth sending when we've heard the briefing."

"I haven't told you what I'd write."

"You smiled," Hermione said.

"That is not evidence."

"It is when you're holding a quill near authority."

The kitchen door swung open. Sirius entered carrying a length of blackened chain and smelling strongly of smoke. A tear crossed one sleeve of his shirt, and soot darkened the side of his face. Harry rose before he meant to. Sirius saw it; his expression altered, the pleased energy of arrival quietening at once.

"Workshop," he said. "Not Death Eaters."

Harry sat again.

"What exploded?"

"An inaccurate assumption."

Remus followed him into the room. He was clean, which established that the inaccurate assumption had not been his.

"Sirius attempted to repair the motorbike's exhaust with a Heating Charm," he said.

"It was already hot."

"A useful observation which arrived after the explosion."

Sirius dropped the chain into the sink. "It works now."

"It is embedded in the garden wall."

"Parts of it work now."

Harry looked at the torn sleeve. No blood. The knowledge released his shoulders by degrees. Sirius washed soot from his hands, glanced at Umbridge's letter and swore with enough creativity that Hermione looked towards the ceiling.

"May I use that in my reply?" Harry asked.

"Quote me accurately."

"He's not sending that reply," Hermione said. "They can quote every word, Sirius. You know they will."

"Why not?"

"Because encouragement is irresponsible."

"At this stage, repression may be more dangerous."

Harry reached for a quill. Hermione moved the ink away from her notes, then stopped with her hand around the bottle. Draconia turned the letter over, leaving a clean page beside it for a draft.

"Authoritarian," Harry said.

"I was trying to stop you giving her a sentence she can put in the Prophet," Hermione said. "But you're right. It's your letter. I can ask you to wait; I can't take it away."

"The words are becoming difficult to distinguish."

Something changed in Sirius's face. It vanished quickly, but Harry saw it. Sirius had spent twelve years with other people calling confinement necessary. Since taking guardianship, he treated every locked cupboard as a moral question. He never stood between Harry and an exit. He explained wards before placing them and asked before touching Harry in sleep. Yet Hawthorn's doors kept closing, each one carrying the same assurance: danger existed, adults knew more, and exclusion was proof they cared.

"You'll receive the full briefing this afternoon," Sirius said.

"The full one?"

"The part concerning you."

"That isn't what full means."

"It's what relevant means."

"I'm relevant to Voldemort. Apparently that covers quite a lot."

Remus leaned against the dresser. "There are operational details which would endanger sources if anyone outside the meeting knew them."

"I know."

Harry hated how young the answer sounded. He pushed back his chair.

"Where are you going?" Sirius asked.

"Out."

"Harry—"

"The door's open. I assumed I was allowed."

He crossed the kitchen before anger could become anything less controlled. The corridor beyond was crowded with proof that the house no longer belonged only to the people living in it. Cloaks hung from every hook. A stack of coded reports occupied the umbrella stand. Someone had left three Sneakoscopes upon the stairs, all spinning faintly and pointing towards Lucius's study. The Marauder's Map lay in a warded drawer of the hall cabinet, where the trio could reach it without leaving it on display for visitors. Harry glanced towards the drawer as he passed, then took his jacket from the peg. Sirius called his name again. He did not stop. Draconia did.

She caught his wrist before he reached the front door. Her grip was not hard, but her thumb rested directly above his pulse.

"We have training," she said.

"With Remus. Who just explained why I shouldn't know things."

"Remus explained why a room of spies will not provide an alphabetical list of spies."

"Thank you. I was struggling with the concept."

"You're being tiresome."

"You usually tell me to speak up."

"Not continuously."

Hermione had followed them into the hall. She held Umbridge's letter in one hand and Harry's abandoned birthday journal in the other.

"You said you wanted adults to tell you when a boundary exists," she said.

"I want them to stop deciding the boundary always ends directly in front of me."

"Those are different problems."

Harry looked at her. Hermione stood upon the runner in stockinged feet, her curls still untidy from breakfast. Concern had drawn a small line between her brows. She was not defending the adults. Harry recognised the deliberate care with which she refused to agree merely because he was hurt.

"Come to training," she said. "Then hear the briefing. If they keep back something you have a right to know, we'll argue about the right thing."

"Let Hermione get them to state the rule first," Draconia said. "She's much harder to get rid of when she knows exactly what she's arguing about."

"It is more effective than storming into the lane."

"I wasn't storming."

"You put on leather in August."

Harry glanced at the jacket. The day beyond the glass was bright and breathless. Heat shimmered above the lane.

"It has a wand holster."

"Then unfasten the holster and wear it with your shirt," Hermione said. "Bill said the straps could be adjusted for that. You needn't roast just to carry your wand."

Draconia's fingers remained around his wrist. Harry became aware of them in a way which had little to do with anger.

"Come with us," she said. "I want to see whether I can beat you at something that doesn't involve reaching the top shelf."

Harry looked past them. Sirius still stood in the kitchen doorway, giving Harry the space to choose whether to return. Remus waited beside him. The front door remained unlocked. Harry could see the lane beyond the glass and knew that nobody was going to plant themselves before the handle. He was still angry, but the choice to stay did not have to be another thing taken from him. That mattered, even while Harry resented everything else. He removed the jacket.

"If I die of heatstroke, this will be entered into the record."

Hermione took it from him. "I'll risk the paperwork."

Remus had transformed the orchard into an argument about Harry's worst habits. Wooden targets waited among the old apple trees, six visible from the starting line and others folded flat in the grass. Red cloth marked the centres of the hostile figures; the figures they were meant to protect wore blue. Between them, ropes hung at shoulder height, enchanted branches shifted without wind, and clusters of silver bells waited beneath the grass for an incautious foot.

At the far end, Sirius sat upon the low stone wall with one boot braced against the trunk of a tree. He had changed his damaged shirt but failed to remove the soot from his ear.

"This looks personal," Harry said.

"It is educational," Remus replied.

"That's what Fred and George call explosives."

"Their educational standards remain under review."

Hermione stood beside the first target and tied her hair away from her face. Draconia removed her summer robe, revealing a white shirt and dark trousers beneath. She folded the robe upon Harry's jacket with the precise calm of someone who expected to win. Harry watched her roll both sleeves to the elbow.

"Concentrate," Hermione said.

He turned. "I am."

"On the lesson."

Draconia glanced between them. "Was he not?"

"He hasn't looked at a target yet."

"I hate you both," Harry said.

"No, you don't," Hermione replied.

That silenced him more effectively than a spell. Remus hid a smile by examining the targets.

"Today's exercise is simple," he said.

"That means it isn't," Harry muttered.

"You begin here. You reach the wall. Every target with a red centre is hostile. Blue targets are civilians. The bells identify your position. The branches resist movement. Sirius and I will interfere."

He tapped a bell with his wand. "The targets' spells will knock you off balance, not injure you. Sirius and I will use the same restraint. Anyone says 'stop', the whole course stops. You are practising decisions, not proving how much punishment you can take."

Harry stared at him. "How?"

"By casting from cover. You'll need to watch more than the targets."

Sirius lifted a hand from the wall.

"Can we attack back?" Draconia asked.

"You may respond to any spell you recognise."

"And the ones we don't?"

"I recommend avoiding those."

Hermione raised her wand. "What is the actual objective?"

"All three of you reach the wall."

"Together?"

"Yes. Nobody is left behind. If one of you is caught, the others have to work out how to help without getting caught too."

The trio exchanged a look. Harry moved automatically towards the centre. Hermione took his left, Draconia his right. Remus's gaze followed each of them before returning to Harry.

"Ready?"

"Not particularly," Harry said.

Remus kept his wand still. "Would you like another minute?"

Harry checked the girls, then the nearest target. "No. I'm ready to try. I reserve the right to complain afterwards."

"I assumed you would." Remus flicked his wand.

The orchard attacked. The first red target pivoted between the trees and released a jet of orange light. Harry blocked it. The spell struck his shield and burst into sparks bright enough to obscure the second target rising behind it.

"Left!" Hermione shouted.

Harry turned too late. Draconia's curse crossed his shoulder and struck the wooden figure through its red centre. The target shattered. A blue figure sprang upright beside it. Harry had already cast. His Stunning Spell hit the blue cloth. The figure fell. A bell rang somewhere behind them.

"Civilian," Hermione said.

"It appeared beside an attacker."

"Civilians often do."

"Unhelpful."

"Civilian hit," Remus called from among the trees. "That counts against the whole team. Keep moving."

Harry glanced towards the fallen target. Silver ropes erupted from the grass. Hermione cut the first. Draconia vanished the second. The third wrapped around Harry's ankle and dragged. He fell backwards, caught himself with one hand and fired blindly towards the movement between two trunks. Sirius stepped from cover. Harry's curse passed an inch from his face. Sirius grinned.

"Better."

"You said targets were hostile!"

"I said red centres were hostile. My centre is devastatingly black."

"Your shirt is grey."

"Emotionally black."

Sirius sent a Trip Jinx at Hermione. She blocked and returned it. He jumped over the spell, landed upon the low branch of an apple tree and disappeared behind the leaves.

"He's enjoying this," Hermione said.

"He's been waiting fourteen years," Harry replied.

He sliced the rope from his ankle and rose. The next sequence came faster. Targets rotated, branches lowered and bells moved beneath the earth. Harry stopped attempting to identify every danger alone. Hermione read patterns quickly, calling positions before figures appeared. Draconia attacked with precise economy, rarely casting twice when one spell would do. Harry held the centre, shielding whichever side broke first and advancing whenever space opened. It worked for almost a minute. Then Remus attacked Hermione. The curse was unfamiliar, pale and soundless. Harry saw it cross between two trees towards the back of her shoulder. He moved without thinking.

His Shield Charm turned the spell. The impact drove him sideways. A red target rose behind Draconia. Harry struck it. Another appeared to Hermione's left. He struck that too.

"Harry," Hermione warned.

Three more targets appeared. He stepped in front of both girls.

"Stay behind me."

"Absolutely not," Draconia said.

An orange spell struck Harry's shield. Then another. His arm shook. He held. The targets divided, two moving wide. Harry followed the left and left his right shoulder exposed. Remus's Disarming Charm took his wand. It flew into the branches. At the same instant, Sirius emerged behind Hermione and tapped two fingers between her shoulder blades.

"Caught," he said. "You lost track of what was behind you."

Draconia spun. The ground beneath her opened into a shallow pit. She dropped with an expression of incandescent offence.

"Caught as well," Remus said, stopping the targets. The pit's padded floor rose beneath Draconia's feet, bringing her safely level with the grass.

The branches lifted clear of the path, and the targets folded back into the grass. A bell gave one last note beneath Harry's shoe. With the movement stopped, he could hear how hard all three of them were breathing. Harry stood unarmed between two fallen blue targets.

"I hate this lesson," he said.

"That's promising," Sirius replied.

Draconia climbed from the pit with earth upon her shirt. "If that was your idea of teaching me to watch my footing, I intend to return the favour," Draconia said, brushing earth from her sleeve.

Hermione retrieved Harry's wand from the tree. "You told us to reach the wall."

"You did not," Remus said.

"Because you attacked us."

"The enemy was inconsiderate."

Harry accepted his wand. "I blocked the curse."

"You did."

"Then why did we fail?"

Remus approached the fallen blue target Harry had struck first. He lifted it by one wooden arm.

"You hit a civilian within six seconds. A Stunner could still hurt someone badly if they fell. You need to identify what you're casting at before you cast."

"It was a target."

"You saw movement beside an attacker and treated both as threats. I want you to notice that before we make the course any faster."

Harry looked at the fallen blue figure and remembered how quickly he had cast. He had scarcely registered the cloth before deciding that the movement beside the attacker needed stopping. Harry looked away.

Remus continued without sharpening his voice. "Then you attempted to shield Hermione and Draconia from every direction at once."

"They were being attacked."

"We were also armed," Draconia said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

Harry looked at her. Soil streaked one cheek. Her hair had escaped its tie, pale strands clinging to her neck in the heat. She was irritated, but the question beneath her irritation was real.

"I know you can fight."

"Then why order us behind you?"

"Because—"

The graveyard answered first. Cedric hidden beneath roots at the foot of the headstone. The protection concealing his breathing while men stood close enough to kill him. Then the images Voldemort had forced into Harry's mind: Hermione and Draconia bloodied, dying, things which had never happened and felt real enough to scar. Harry's magic weakening, and Cedric's protection weakening with it. His breath shortened.

Remus's voice changed. "Harry. Name the place."

He looked down. Grass. Fallen apples. A silver bell beside his shoe.

"Orchard. Hawthorn."

"The year."

"Nineteen ninety-five."

"What happened?"

"I lost."

"In the present."

Harry forced himself to look at the girls. Hermione was alive. Draconia was dirty and furious and alive. Sirius stood behind them, wand lowered.

"Training," Harry said. "Draconia fell into a hole."

"I was tactically lowered."

His lungs released. Remus waited.

"I tried to protect them," Harry said.

"You stopped letting us help," Hermione said. "I was calling the left side, and you moved across me before I could cast."

The words were not cruel. That made it harder to dismiss them.

"I thought the shield would hold."

"But it didn't. And we were still behind you, unable to see what you'd left open."

Harry looked at his wand.

Sirius came down from the wall. "Protection isn't standing in front until everyone behind you forgets how to move."

"You'd do the same for me."

"Yes."

"You would've done it in the graveyard."

"Yes."

"Then don't tell me it's wrong."

Sirius stopped an arm's length away. For a moment, he appeared younger and much older at once. Recklessness lived visibly in him; Harry recognised it because he had inherited none of Sirius's blood and too much of his instinct.

"It isn't wrong to take a blow," Sirius said. "It is wrong to decide you're the only person permitted to choose who takes it."

Harry had no immediate answer. Sirius took Harry's wand hand and turned it palm-up. A scrape crossed the skin where Harry had caught himself against the ground.

"You leave your back open whenever one of them is threatened," he said. "James did it. I did it. We called it loyalty because that sounded better than panic."

"Did it work?"

The question came out sharper than Harry intended. Silence moved through the orchard. James had died in front of Lily. Lily in front of Harry. Sirius had chased Peter instead of protecting what remained. Harry was alive because Lily had stood there. That knowledge did not make it easier to imagine either of his parents falling, or Sirius discovering too late what had been left behind. Sirius released his hand.

"Not often enough," he said.

Harry regretted the question at once. Sirius did not make him apologise.

Remus reset the nearest target. "Again."

Hermione looked at him. "Now?"

"If all three of you want to. We can lower the pace. Stopping now would be perfectly reasonable too."

Draconia wiped the dirt from her cheek and raised her wand.

"No one orders anyone behind them," she said.

"All right. I can try it differently," Harry said. "A little slower this time."

"Good. And if I say I've got a target, let me have it."

"I won't order either of you behind me."

Hermione lifted her wand. "And?"

"There are additional terms?"

"You listen when we call a threat."

"You do not attack every object which surprises you," Draconia added.

"What if it looks hostile?"

"Ask whether it has a red centre," Sirius said.

Harry gave him a look.

"Too soon?"

"A little."

They returned to the starting line. On the second attempt, Harry struck only one civilian. On the third, Hermione reached the wall while Harry and Draconia were caught in an enchanted net.

On the fourth, Draconia reached the wall first, then realised that doing so had left Hermione and Harry without anyone watching their rear. She turned back at once and was caught trying to free the net. Afterwards she was quieter while Remus explained how they might have moved together. On the fifth, they stopped trying to place Harry in the centre.

Hermione led. Draconia covered the rear. Harry moved between them instead of ahead, taking instructions he would once have mistaken for restrictions. When Sirius attacked from the trees, Hermione forced him left. Draconia anticipated the opening and bound his wand arm. Harry saw Remus behind a blue target and waited half a second before casting. The half-second was enough to recognise the cloth. He changed the Stunning Spell into a shield. Remus's curse struck it. All three reached the wall. Sirius remained tied to the apple tree.

"Do I count as a casualty?" he asked.

"Emotionally," Harry said.

"Untie me."

"We're protecting you from further bad decisions."

Hermione laughed. Draconia inspected the knot, announced that Sirius might at least thank them for not dropping him into a hole, and released it with a flick of her wand. Remus looked at Harry across the orchard.

Approval was there, but so was calculation. The lesson had not been only about the three of them reaching a wall. It was about what Harry did when fear arrived before thought. Harry looked at the blue target he had chosen not to attack. Half a second. It was not much. It might be enough to change who remained standing.

At three o'clock, the house filled with people who checked the windows before sitting down. Kingsley returned first in plum-coloured Ministry robes, carrying an ordinary leather briefcase which made every Sneakoscope in the hall scream. Tonks followed through the Floo and tripped over the fender, turning her hair bright orange in irritation. Arthur Weasley brought a folded rail timetable and two rolls of parchment. Professor McGonagall appeared at the front door precisely as the clock struck three. Alastor Moody came through the kitchen window.

"Door's watched," he said when Narcissa objected.

"So was the window."

"Watched wrong."

He stamped mud across the clean floor, then reached for a small brass detector on his belt. Its needle swung towards the cupboard where Winky kept her cleaning charms. Moody raised his chin to inspect it; his replacement magical eye turned independently towards the doorway while the natural one remained upon the detector. Winky slapped a tea towel into his hand.

"Boots," she said. "Before another step."

Moody looked down at the muddy prints. "Good rule."

Harry stood beside the table with Hermione and Draconia. The birthday journal had been replaced by route maps, lists of student names and a diagram of the Hogwarts Express drawn from above. Several red crosses marked the track north of York. Nobody had yet told Harry what they meant. Dumbledore arrived last.

He did not enter with a flash or dramatic silence. The front bell rang, Sirius opened it and the headmaster stepped inside wearing deep blue robes speckled with rain which had not fallen anywhere near Hawthorn. He greeted Narcissa, thanked Dobby for the tea and looked directly at Harry before speaking to anyone else.

"Good afternoon, Harry."

"Professor."

"Before we begin, I would like to establish the structure of this meeting."

Harry glanced at Sirius. Dumbledore noticed.

"The first part concerns threats to you, your friends and the students travelling to Hogwarts. You will remain for it and may ask questions. The second concerns identities and methods whose exposure could endanger people not present. I will explain when that boundary is reached and why. The final decisions which affect your movement or education will be given to you before they are put into effect."

Nobody reached for the maps while Dumbledore spoke. Harry watched Sirius keep his hands upon the back of the chair, waiting rather than adding another reassurance to the explanation. Harry had not expected the explanation to soothe him. It did not. A boundary remained a boundary even when named politely. But it was not a locked cupboard beneath the stairs. It had reasons, edges and a promise to reopen.

"All right," he said.

Dumbledore inclined his head. Arthur gathered the timetable while Kingsley carried the maps into the dining room. Sirius held the connecting door open, then pulled out the chair beside his own. Harry sat with Hermione on his left and Draconia on his right. The adults arranged themselves around them. The kitchen door remained open behind Moody, who had wiped his boots without admitting the towel was useful.

The meeting began with the railway. Arthur unfolded the timetable across the table and placed four salt cellars upon its corners. It was an ordinary Muggle booklet, printed in small black type and creased along the route between London and Inverness. Someone had drawn the Hogwarts line in red ink where no public track existed.

"The Express leaves King's Cross at eleven on the first of September," Arthur said. "That has not changed."

"Comforting," Harry murmured. "I was worried they might move the school."

Moody turned towards him. "If they could, they should. Castle's got too many entrances."

"Most of them are walls," Hermione said.

"That's what they want you to think."

McGonagall's lips pressed together.

Arthur traced the printed line north. "What has changed is the number of people who know the journey may be vulnerable. The route cannot be altered substantially without rebuilding the concealment charms. The departure point is public. The date is public. And the train carries most of Britain's school-age witches and wizards at once."

Harry examined the red crosses.

"Those?"

Kingsley opened his briefcase. The Sneakoscopes in the hall shrieked again. He removed a folded map, closed the case and waited until they quietened.

"Unconfirmed sightings," he said. "Broom purchases under false names. Protective clothing, modified masks, cursed iron and components associated with track disruption. None of it proves a plan against the Express."

"But you think there is one," Draconia said.

Kingsley looked at Lucius. Lucius sat at the opposite end of the table beside Narcissa. He wore grey rather than black, as though every morning now involved a calculation about how threatening a man could afford to appear. The alteration fooled no one. With his cane resting against the chair and neither Manor nor Ministry allies to support him, Lucius occupied a chair like disputed territory.

"I think," he said, "that the elder Nott enjoyed symbolic violence before he discovered true allegiance. He prefers targets which humiliate institutions and implicate their enemies. An attack upon the school train would frighten every family at once, weaken Dumbledore and demonstrate that the Ministry cannot protect children upon its own infrastructure."

"And kill us," Harry said.

Lucius's gaze moved to him. "That too."

Narcissa placed one hand flat upon the table.

"Nott knows Draconia travels with Harry and Hermione."

Her voice was composed, but Draconia straightened beside Harry and put down her quill.

"He knew enough about your friendships to exploit them during the Tournament," Lucius said. "We must assume he knows they continue."

"Then split us up," Harry said.

Three adults answered at once.

"No," Sirius said.

"Absolutely not," Narcissa said.

"Counterproductive," Moody said.

Harry looked around the table. "If he wants all three of us in one place—"

"We cannot know precisely what he intends," Dumbledore said. "But removing the people you trust would cost you support without making you invisible to him."

"And make each of you harder to protect," Kingsley added. "One compartment, one guard position."

"We're having a guard?" Hermione asked.

"Several," Arthur replied. "Discreetly."

"How discreetly can several Aurors board a school train?"

Tonks raised her hand. Her hair had faded from orange to a soft violet. "You'd be surprised. I'm an excellent fifth-year."

"You look twenty-two," Harry said.

"Not from the back."

"You walked into a grandfather clock yesterday."

"Authentic adolescence."

Kingsley continued as though this exchange had not occurred. "Aurors will be distributed through the carriages. Additional riders will shadow the route where concealment allows. No public announcement will be made. Panic upon a moving train would create its own danger."

"Does the Ministry know?" Draconia asked.

"The Minister approved an increased escort," Kingsley said.

"That wasn't the question."

Kingsley regarded her for a moment.

"The Minister knows there is a general threat to transport. He does not know the sources behind our concern."

Lucius leaned back. "Because one of those sources believes the Minister's office has been compromised."

"Do you?" Harry asked.

"I believe Cornelius Fudge is compromised by Cornelius Fudge. Anyone else would be redundant."

Sirius made a sound suspiciously like a laugh. Green light spilled from the kitchen Floo through the open doorway. Every wand at the table rose. Severus Snape stepped out of the fireplace and stopped at the dining-room threshold. He took in the circle of aimed weapons, brushed ash from one shoulder and looked particularly unimpressed by the welcome.

"If this is the Order's present standard of discipline," he said, "the Dark Lord may safely take the remainder of the summer off."

Sirius did not lower his wand.

"You're late."

"I was occupied preserving the illusion that I do not spend my afternoons in your kitchen."

"Could've preserved it permanently."

"Sirius," Remus said.

"Severus," Dumbledore said at the same time.

Both men stopped without appearing obedient. Snape moved to the only empty chair. It stood between Moody and Lucius, a placement so hostile Harry suspected Draconia had arranged it. Snape noticed the suspicion upon his face.

"Something to contribute, Potter?"

"I was admiring the seating plan, sir."

The final word was faultlessly polite. Snape's eyes narrowed. Sirius looked delighted.

"Harry," Hermione whispered.

"Manners," he whispered back.

Dumbledore folded his hands. "Severus has been assessing whether Voldemort's supporters believe Harry's location to be known."

The headmaster used the name without hesitation. Several people around the table did not. Snape's attention shifted to Harry with none of the softness the others attempted to conceal.

"They know you have left the Muggle residence. They do not know where you are. Some believe Black has taken you abroad. Others believe Dumbledore has hidden you at Hogwarts. The absence of certainty is not safety."

"Do they know about the train?" Harry asked.

"Everyone knows about the train."

"Do they intend to attack it?"

"If I knew that, I would have said so."

"You say plenty without knowing."

Hermione stepped upon Harry's foot beneath the table. Snape's mouth became a thin line.

"Your confidence survives, at least."

"Unfortunately, I'm told it suits me."

Draconia stared determinedly at the timetable. Colour rose along the side of her neck. Snape saw that too. His gaze travelled once between them, gathered more information than Harry wanted him to possess and returned to Dumbledore.

"There is another matter."

He placed a small glass phial upon the table. Inside, a thread of black smoke twisted against the stopper.

"This was taken from a mask recovered near an abandoned property in Wiltshire. The charm woven through the fabric is intended to obscure identity from human sight, portraits and common Revealing Spells. It was produced recently."

Hermione leaned closer. "Would it confuse the Marauder's Map?"

Snape glanced at Dumbledore. Harry's hand moved instinctively towards the hall where the Map remained locked. Until this meeting, knowledge of the Map had stayed with the household and the few people involved in Peter's exposure. Most Order members had been told only that specialised detection magic had helped to identify him.

"We don't know," Remus said.

"Can we test it before September?" Draconia asked. "I'd rather find out here than when we need it."

Moody's natural eye narrowed. "With the map?"

"With the mask," Harry said before Draconia could answer. "Use another tracking charm against it. If it defeats those too, we learn something without showing anyone how the original works."

Moody looked dissatisfied by an answer which gave him no new secret.

Hermione nodded. "And we can test the Map privately afterward."

"We?" Sirius asked.

"It belongs to us," Harry said.

"I know who it belongs to."

"Then that was an easy question."

Remus covered his mouth.

Dumbledore's eyes brightened. "A sensible division."

Sirius looked at the headmaster. "Stop encouraging him."

"I fear that opportunity passed some years ago."

Arthur drew a fresh sheet towards him, and Kingsley began listing the decisions they could make without knowing whether Nott had chosen the train. Harry found those easier to listen to than the imagined attack. They gave him questions he could ask and answers that might actually change something. Harry learnt that the train's emergency brakes could be controlled from three separate carriages; that the driver's compartment possessed a ward which had not been examined since 1967; that broom riders could not safely Apparate onto the moving roof but could Apparate ahead of the route and intercept it; and that no one had ever developed an evacuation plan for eight hundred students travelling through open countryside at speed.

Hermione began one before Arthur finished speaking.

"Prefects need assigned sections," she said. "Not Houses. If everyone tries to reach their own carriage, the corridors will block."

"Windows open outward," Harry said. "Most older students carry brooms in the luggage vans, but getting to them would be impossible during an attack."

"Unless some were distributed beforehand," Draconia said.

"Absolutely not," McGonagall replied. "I will not have unsupervised broomsticks stored throughout passenger compartments."

"Then supervised ones."

"Miss Malfoy—"

"If evacuation becomes necessary, Professor, rules concerning corridor storage will be of limited comfort."

McGonagall inhaled through her nose.

"Six brooms," she said at last. "Secured in designated carriages. Accessible only to prefects and escort personnel."

Draconia wrote the number down.

Harry looked at her. "Do you negotiate with every teacher like this?"

"Only when I think the rule leaves something important out."

"I heard that," McGonagall said.

"I assumed you would."

By the time Arthur rolled up the timetable, the plan remained incomplete but no longer imaginary. There would be Aurors, hidden riders, reinforced carriage wards and emergency brooms. Prefects would receive instructions without being told the precise intelligence. Harry, Hermione and Draconia would travel together. They would not be placed in an isolated guarded compartment, because Lucius argued that isolation identified the target more clearly than ordinary company. Sirius agreed on the condition that Harry carry his mirror, Remus's compass, the brass key and two emergency Portkeys.

"The key won't get me off a train," Harry said.

"I know. Carry it."

"If the Portkeys work, why do I need the other things?"

"Because spells fail."

Arthur touched the railway diagram. "And the Portkeys will have to work with the carriage wards. We're checking the release points. Don't use either object aboard the train until the escort tells you it's safe."

Moody struck the table with one scarred finger. "Correct. Never trust a single exit."

"You've made him happy," Harry told Sirius. "That feels irresponsible."

Moody gave him a thoroughly unimpressed look. Dumbledore waited until the laughter faded.

"There is one final matter concerning Hogwarts. Dolores Umbridge's formal powers extend farther than the public announcement suggests. She may observe all lessons, inspect staff qualifications, review student correspondence where a threat to safety is alleged and require approval for organisations not directly connected to an existing subject."

Hermione's quill stopped.

"Can she read our letters?"

"Not lawfully without cause," Kingsley said.

"She writes the cause," Lucius replied.

"Can she stop us discussing the graveyard?" Harry asked.

"The hearing's findings remain public," Dumbledore said.

"That wasn't what I asked."

The headmaster's expression grew grave.

"She may attempt to treat public discussion as harmful disruption, particularly if it occurs in class. She cannot legally forbid a private conversation between students. Whether legality prevents her from punishing one is another question."

Harry thought of the letter's pleasant phrasing. Support your continued adjustment.

"She'll make me choose," he said.

"Between what?" Sirius asked.

"Silence and behaving badly enough that everyone thinks she's right about me."

No one answered at once. Umbridge did not need to prove the graveyard false. She needed Harry angry, disobedient and visibly unstable in front of students who had not heard the hearing. Every punishment could become evidence that he required control. Every refusal could be reported as distress. Draconia turned the Ministry letter face-down.

"Then we do not give her the choice she prepared," she said.

"I stay polite?"

"Within reason."

"That sounds terrible."

"You make her state every restriction precisely," Hermione said, thinking aloud now. "You ask for it in writing. You keep copies. You don't argue about what she means; you record what she does."

"And when she lies?"

"Then we have something besides your word against hers," Hermione said. "Someone else in the room, a copy of the order, a parent who knows what happened. I don't think it will make her fair. It might make it harder for her to hide."

The phrase had changed since the hearing. It no longer meant merely sending transcripts. It meant witnesses, records, students from four Houses and adults beyond the walls of one office. Harry looked towards Dumbledore.

"Will you stop her?"

The question emptied the humour from the room.

Dumbledore met his eyes. "Where I possess the authority, yes. Where the Ministry's law limits mine, I will challenge it openly. I will not ask you to endure harm in silence so that I may preserve a strategic advantage."

Harry heard the care in the promise. He also heard its boundaries.

"All right."

It was not trust restored completely. Trust did not return because someone stated the correct sentence once. But it was a place from which trust might move. Dumbledore nodded. Then he looked at Hermione and Draconia.

"You will be watched as closely as Harry. Perhaps more subtly."

"We assumed so," Hermione said.

"Good. Do not mistake preparation for invulnerability."

"We don't," Draconia replied.

Harry thought of standing before them in the orchard.

Stay behind me.

He did not say it.

Dumbledore turned to the adults. "We have reached the operational portion of the meeting. It concerns sources whose identities Harry has no need to carry and movements he cannot act upon."

The wording was deliberate. No need to carry, rather than no right to know. Harry did not like it, but he understood.

"How long?" he asked.

"Approximately one hour. Afterward, Sirius and I will explain any decision which alters your protection."

"Before it's done?"

"Before."

Harry rose. Hermione gathered her notes. Draconia folded the railway diagram she had copied and placed it inside her sleeve. At the door, Harry looked back. Sirius sat among maps, weapons and people preparing to fight a war. He seemed more alive there than he did in any quiet room. His shoulders had straightened. One hand rested near his wand, and his attention followed three conversations at once. Harry wondered what being his guardian had cost him before either of them had learnt how to live together. Sirius caught him watching.

"One hour," he said.

Harry nodded. The dining-room door closed. The brass phoenix above the handle folded its wings.

"We could listen through the chimney," Harry said.

Hermione did not look up from the railway plan. "You promised to wait for the briefing," Hermione said. "And I don't intend to explain soot in your hair when they open the door."

"It connects to the kitchen fireplace."

"The wards will include the chimney."

"We don't know that."

Draconia sat cross-legged upon the library rug with an open warding manual beside her. "They do. The charm seals any continuous space crossing the room's boundary, including pipes, vents and structural damage wider than two inches."

Harry stood near the window. "Convenient expertise."

"My parents held private meetings throughout my childhood."

"Did you listen?"

"Of course. They improved the wards when I was nine."

Hermione glanced up. "How did you manage it before then?"

"The west drawing room at the Manor had a portrait of my great-great-grandmother on both sides of the wall. She disliked Father."

"You bribed a portrait?"

"I appealed to inherited resentment."

Harry left the window and dropped into the armchair opposite them. Hermione gathered her railway plan and moved to the other chair, drawing her feet beneath her. The library door stood open. Beyond it, Hawthorn remained busy in muffled fragments. Dobby and Winky argued in the kitchen. Someone crossed the upper corridor. Cloud had gathered over the orchard while they were at the meeting, and rain began softly against the windows. The dining room remained silent.

"They'll tell us what affects us," Hermione said.

"You don't know that."

"Dumbledore promised."

"Dumbledore has promised things before."

She closed the railway plan.

"Then we judge what he does today."

Harry leaned his head against the chair.

"You always have to be reasonable?"

"No. Sometimes I choose it to irritate you."

"It's working."

Hermione's mouth curved.

Draconia turned a page. "The meeting does not concern only you."

"I know."

"Do you?"

It was the second time she had asked that question today. Harry looked at her. Draconia's attention remained upon the book, but she had not read a line in several minutes.

"My father is inside that room," she said. "So is Professor Snape. Professor Snape goes back to Voldemort. Father cannot do that now, so he follows people who would be delighted to deliver him instead. Mother calls it necessary. Father calls it useful. Professor Snape calls it nothing at all."

Her voice stayed even through effort.

"Every closed door in this house may contain the decision which kills one of them. I dislike it too."

Harry's anger changed shape. Draconia did not often offer fear directly. She placed it inside analysis, polished the edges and expected everyone to admire the argument instead of asking what it protected. He left the chair and sat upon the rug beside her, their shoulders touching.

"Lucius doesn't have to go back," he said.

"You did not have to enter the maze."

"I did, actually. Magical contract."

"Then choose a different example."

"I don't have many safe decisions available."

"Then you know why I'm not reassured merely because he calls it a choice."

She turned another unread page. Harry rested his hand beside hers upon the carpet, close enough that their smallest fingers touched.

"If he leaves," he said, "we'll make him tell us when he's meant to return."

Draconia's gaze moved to him.

"And if he doesn't?"

The graveyard offered answers. A body. Blood. Silence where a person had been. Harry pressed his finger more firmly against hers.

"Then we notice."

The sentence was inadequate. It was also the promise upon which every rescue began. Draconia turned her hand palm-up. Harry's fingers settled into it. Hermione watched them from the armchair.

Something crossed her face—brief enough to deny, real enough that Harry felt it even before looking. Then she moved from the chair to the rug and placed the railway plan between all three of them.

"If we are establishing return procedures," she said, "we should not wait until someone leaves."

Draconia did not release Harry's hand. Hermione took out a fresh sheet of parchment.

"Every Order member needs an expected time, a delayed time and a point at which delay becomes emergency."

"Passwords," Harry said. "Not just names. Voldemort can show someone who looks right."

"And voices can be imitated," Draconia added.

"Questions based on shared memories?"

Harry shook his head. "He can enter memories."

Hermione's quill paused. None of them spoke for a moment. The graveyard returned in fragments: Voldemort's voice inside Harry's head, images of Hermione and Draconia suffering, false memories wearing the physical weight of truth. A question about the past could be stolen. A face could be copied. A voice could lie.

"New answers," Harry said slowly.

"What?" Hermione asked.

"Something we agree that day. Not where we went on holiday when we were nine."

Draconia's thumb moved once against his knuckle. "He could take today's memory as easily as an old one."

"I know. But someone who stole the old answers wouldn't have the new ones."

Hermione wrote it down, then drew a line beneath the need for a second channel. "It helps. It doesn't prove everything."

"No," Harry said. "Nothing does."

They built the procedure upon the library floor while adults planned a war beyond a sealed door. First: every person leaving Hawthorn stated a return window and an earlier check-in time. A missed check meant the duty adult contacted the mission coordinator at once; it did not authorise the children to search. Second: if delayed, they contacted the house through mirror, Patronus or a prearranged messenger. No single method counted as certain. Third: any request to meet, rescue or change location required verification through two channels. Fourth: images, dreams and visions were warnings to report, not proof that an event had happened or that a person was in a particular place.

Fifth: if a message involved immediate harm to someone they loved, the person receiving it did not act alone. Harry disliked the fifth rule. That was how he knew it belonged.

"What if there isn't time?" he asked.

Hermione looked at him. "Then whoever is with you helps decide that."

"What if I'm alone?"

"Use the mirror first. Send a Patronus if you can. The compass can point you towards a place you've marked for help, but it can't tell you who is there. Find an adult, or get a message to one."

"And if all of those fail?"

Draconia's fingers tightened around his.

"Then you do what you always do," she said. "But you are not permitted to begin by assuming they have failed."

Harry read the rule again. He could agree to it in a warm library. He did not know whether the agreement would survive someone screaming inside his head.

"Write that part down," he said.

Hermione did. The floor around them filled with arrows, contingencies and objections. Harry argued that a Patronus could reveal their position. Draconia pointed out that mirrors might be confiscated at Hogwarts. Hermione proposed paired phrases for each method and immediately rejected phrases based on birthdays, parents or Houses. They settled upon changing questions recorded in two separate books—one at Hawthorn, one carried by the trio. Answers would be agreed shortly before each departure. Any replacement sent afterwards had to come through a second channel; no message could supply its own proof that it was genuine.

By the time Hermione reached the bottom of the page, two rules had been crossed out and another required a separate note. Harry read the revisions while Draconia checked how a missed message would be reported. The difficulty was frustrating, but it was better to find the gaps here than while someone was waiting at a gate. After nearly an hour, Hermione sat back upon her heels.

"This needs testing."

"Everything needs testing with you," Harry said.

"That is how we discover whether it works."

"Sometimes things can simply be trusted."

"Name one."

Harry looked at Draconia. She raised an eyebrow. He looked at Hermione. The answer became embarrassingly obvious.

"This room," he said.

Hermione's expression softened.

"I hope you don't mean the ceiling. It dropped plaster in my tea yesterday."

"I meant the people in it."

Draconia's composure failed for half a second. Hermione bent over the parchment, though she had stopped writing. Harry became fascinated by the diagrams. The dining-room wards released with a distant chime. All three stood.

Snape left first. He emerged from the dining room with his robes moving around him like black water and stopped when he found the trio waiting in the hall. His gaze fell upon their parchment, travelled across the branching diagrams and rested on the line Hermione had underlined twice.

Images, dreams and visions are warnings to report, not proof of an event or location.

Something unreadable passed through his face.

"A rare improvement upon instinct," he said.

"We thought so," Harry replied.

"Then see whether you remember it when you are frightened, Potter."

"That's why there are three of us checking it, sir. I don't think I'll always manage it alone."

Snape looked at Hermione. "Miss Granger, your procedure assumes that the subject recognises an intrusion before acting upon it."

"That's why there are external checks."

"Checks which require time."

"Then tell us what to practise while Harry works with Remus. Something we can do before we have to make those decisions."

Snape's attention moved to her. He had always treated Draconia differently from Harry, though not gently. In Potions, his approval arrived as precision: fewer corrections, harder questions, no indulgence when her surname might have purchased it from someone else. Since the graveyard, a further tension had entered. Her father had defected. Snape had returned. Each represented a choice the other might have made.

"That," he said, "is the purpose of Occlumency."

Harry folded his arms. "Remus said it isn't only walls."

"Lupin has the luxury of teaching philosophy. The Dark Lord will not wait while you identify five objects and admire the upholstery."

The old reflex rose: anger before thought, the need to strike wherever Snape had exposed something vulnerable. Harry felt it gather and remembered the orchard. Half a second.

"Then it's fortunate Remus started before you," he said. "I might know where I am when the harder part begins."

Snape's expression did not soften. It shifted by a degree from contempt towards assessment.

"We shall see."

"We?"

Dumbledore appeared in the doorway behind him.

"That is among the matters we must discuss with you, Harry."

Harry looked from him to Snape.

"I'm not agreeing to lessons with him," Harry said.

Snape gave a quiet, humourless laugh. "A promising demonstration of discipline."

"You haven't asked the question," Hermione said.

"I know the question."

"You know the teacher," Dumbledore replied. "The structure has not been decided."

Harry's refusal remained ready upon his tongue. He remembered Voldemort inside his head. The invasion had not felt like a door opening. It had felt like his thoughts changing ownership while he remained awake to watch. Snape knew how to prevent that. Snape also knew how to find every bruise Harry carried and press until it looked like instruction.

"What structure?" he asked.

Dumbledore stepped aside so the others could enter the hall. Moody limped towards the front room. Arthur and Kingsley remained by the dining table, speaking in low voices. Lucius emerged with Narcissa, who found Draconia immediately and placed a hand against her shoulder. Snape did not leave.

"Professor Snape is the Order's most accomplished Occlumens," Dumbledore said. "There may come a point at which experience resisting Voldemort directly is necessary. That point has not arrived. Remus will continue your grounding and recognition work. If direct Legilimency becomes useful, it will occur only after you agree, with an observer you choose, a stated purpose and a means to end the session which does not depend upon the instructor."

Harry looked sharply at him.

"How?"

"A warded object held in your hand. It would activate a barrier between teacher and pupil when released. Professor Flitwick must test that it interrupts the lesson safely before anyone uses it with you. I will not call it a guarantee merely because we have designed it."

Hermione considered. "Even if Harry freezes?"

"The observer ends the session," Dumbledore said. "We cannot rely only upon Harry being able to move. The object will also respond if his grip tightens beyond the pressure we agree."

"And if the object is knocked away?" Draconia asked.

"That must trigger the same barrier. If testing shows it does not, the device is not ready for a lesson."

"Who makes it?"

"Professor Flitwick, with your participation if you wish."

The girls continued questioning the device: whether it could be tampered with, whether Snape could prevent Harry releasing it, whether the observer could break the link independently. Dumbledore answered every one. Snape endured the discussion with the expression of a surgeon watching a patient interrogate the knife. Harry listened.

Nobody called his fear childish. Nobody told him to submit because the threat was greater than his comfort. The option remained horrible, but it had shape now. It could be entered, observed and stopped.

"Not yet," he said. "I want the device explained before I decide anything."

Dumbledore inclined his head. "Not yet."

Snape's eyes narrowed. "The Dark Lord is unlikely to respect your timetable."

"Then help Remus make me ready faster."

"I have students capable of following instruction to prepare for September."

"I must've missed them in Potions."

Sirius barked a laugh from inside the dining room. Snape turned his head.

"Something amusing, Black?"

"Deeply."

"Your standards remain low."

"Keeps conversations with you accessible."

Remus came between them without moving, a feat he had practised for years. "Severus, the Floo is clear."

Snape looked at Harry once more.

"Learn the delay," he said. "Whatever else you think of me."

Then he crossed the kitchen and vanished into green flame. Harry watched the fire settle. It would have been easier if every useful warning came from someone he loved.

Dumbledore kept his promise. He explained that no change would be made to Harry's location. Hawthorn's wards would be strengthened, but every addition involving Harry's room, mirror or movement required explanation before activation. Two Order members would remain within reach of the property while Sirius, Remus or the Malfoys were present; one when all three households were home. The Ministry would not be told the address.

He also explained that the Order intended to investigate an abandoned safe house once used by supporters of Voldemort during the first war. Recent protective activity suggested someone had returned. Lucius recognised two of the ward signatures. Remus would approach through the woodland and locate the outer detection charms before Tonks crossed them. Tonks would enter with them. Moody would coordinate from the boundary. Sirius had not been selected.

"Why?" Harry asked.

He stood beside the dining table while the others prepared to leave. Dumbledore had gone to speak with McGonagall. Arthur and Kingsley were rolling the route maps into separate cases. Nobody answered quickly enough. Sirius leaned both hands upon the back of his chair.

"Because Moody thinks three people are sufficient."

"Four," Moody said from the doorway. "Tonks, Lupin, Malfoy and me."

"You said boundary."

"Boundary's a place."

"A safe one."

"No such thing."

Sirius's jaw tightened. "Then you need another wand."

"Need one here."

"Hawthorn has protections, house-elves, Narcissa, Dumbledore's alarm wards and half the Order passing through every day."

"It also has Harry."

The name landed between them. Harry felt every face turn towards him and hated that more than the closed door. Sirius straightened.

"Harry is not a reason to treat me like glass."

"Didn't say glass."

"You didn't have to."

Remus moved closer. "Padfoot."

"Don't."

The old name cracked through the formal room.

Moody glanced at Harry before returning his attention to Sirius. "You're his guardian. You're also recognisable, impulsive and twelve years behind on field work. Pick the reason that offends you least."

"I've fought more Death Eaters than Tonks has met."

Tonks, halfway into her cloak, stopped. "I'm still in the room."

"It wasn't an insult."

"Had the shape of one."

"Sirius," Remus said again. "The site is confined. Animagus movement offers little advantage. Your face compromises any cover the moment something goes wrong."

"Lucius's face is hardly obscure."

Lucius fastened his cuffs. "Unlike you, Black, I possess the useful reputation of a coward."

"That isn't why you're staying," Moody said.

Sirius looked at Harry. Only then did Harry understand what made the decision unbearable. It was not merely that Sirius wanted to fight. Being told to remain because he was Harry's guardian made Harry the lock upon the door.

"Go," Harry said.

The room stilled.

Sirius's expression sharpened. "What?"

"If you should be there, go. I don't need someone sitting beside me every minute."

"This isn't your decision."

"Apparently it is about me."

"That doesn't make it yours."

Harry laughed once without humour. "Interesting rule."

"Harry."

"You said you didn't want being my guardian to make you glass. I don't want it to make me a cage."

The sentence struck Sirius visibly. Remus closed his eyes.

"No one said that," Sirius replied.

"Everyone looked at me."

"Because you're standing in the middle of the room."

"I'm always standing in the middle of the reason."

Heat entered Harry's face. He knew the argument had shifted. The safe house, the mission and Moody's decision had become every adult who ever claimed sacrifice on Harry's behalf. His mother. His father. Cedric nearly. Sirius, always ready to put Harry's needs before his own. Love arrived carrying a bill Harry had not agreed to incur.

"If you stay and someone dies," Harry said, "you'll think it was because of me."

Sirius recoiled. "No."

"You already do."

"You have no idea what I think."

"Then tell me!"

Magic disturbed the room. The glasses upon the table trembled. One cracked from rim to base. A picture frame fell from the mantel and struck the floor face-down. Harry stared at it. His anger vanished as quickly as it had risen, leaving shame and too much silence. Sirius's face had closed.

"The decision has been made," he said. "I'm staying."

He walked out. The hall door shut hard enough to wake every portrait. Harry remained beside the broken glass. No one spoke.

Then Moody grunted. "Could've gone worse."

Tonks stared at him. "How?"

"Fire."

Narcissa began repairing the glass with small, precise movements. Lucius watched the door through which Sirius had disappeared.

"Do not follow him immediately," he said.

Harry rounded on him. "I wasn't going to."

"Then we have accidentally agreed."

"He'll think I don't care."

"No. He will think you are both angry. For once, that is accurate."

Draconia stepped to Harry's side.

"Father."

Lucius drew on his travelling cloak.

"Black has spent half his life unable to choose where he stands. Give him ten minutes in which no one chooses his feelings for him."

It was the most generous thing Harry had ever heard Lucius say about Sirius. Lucius appeared to resent having said it. He turned to Draconia. Narcissa had already reached her. She fastened the top of Draconia's shirt where it had come loose during training and smoothed the disturbed collar as though the small correction could make the coming separation orderly.

"Return time?" Draconia asked.

Lucius glanced towards the parchment upon the library floor.

"Tomorrow before noon. You will hear from us by ten, whether or not we are delayed. If we miss that check, contact Dumbledore or Amelia and let them establish what has happened. You do not come after us."

"Verification question?"

Lucius considered.

"Ask what colour I have chosen for the east drawing room at the Manor."

"What is the answer?"

"Orange."

Draconia stared. "You hate orange."

"Which should make it memorable."

Hermione wrote the answer beneath the question. Narcissa copied it into the second book, added Remus's phrase and handed him the page to check before they left. Narcissa placed a hand upon Lucius's cheek. They looked at one another without speaking. Their affection had never resembled softness; it was too controlled, too accustomed to surviving public observation. In private, the stillness between them said what display did not. Lucius covered her hand with his.

"Before noon," he repeated.

He kissed Draconia's forehead. Then he, Remus, Tonks and Moody left through four different exits. Protection, Harry thought, had an inconvenient number of shapes. Sometimes it looked like going. Sometimes it looked like being made to stay.

Sirius went to the workshop because everything there could be repaired by force. The outbuilding stood beyond the kitchen garden, half concealed by an ash tree and connected to Hawthorn by a stone path which never remained straight. Its windows were too small. Its roof leaked above the only chair. Every tool possessed a place Sirius ignored. The motorbike occupied the centre.

He had removed the exhaust from the garden wall and set it upon the workbench. The chain Harry had seen that morning lay beside it, newly cleaned and still warped by heat. Sirius picked up a spanner. Put it down. Picked it up again.

The quiet inside the workshop was not silence. Metal cooled with small ticks. Bees moved through foxglove beyond the open door. From the house came no sound at all, though Sirius could feel its wards around him: layered magic recognising his presence and the wand at his side. Home as a structure. Home as an agreement.

I don't want it to make me a cage.

Sirius closed his eyes. The worst part was that Harry had seen correctly. Not completely. Never completely. Sirius would have remained if Hawthorn contained no one but an elderly kneazle. Moody was right about his face, his rusted field instincts and the danger of sending three people chosen for the task with a fourth who wanted a fight. But Harry had seen the moment resentment found a target. Sirius had looked at him. He could still see Harry's face when he had said it. Sirius pressed a hand against his own ribs, as though the movement might ease something. He threw the spanner.

It struck a cabinet, knocked the top drawer open and sent a stack of old photographs sliding onto the floor. Sirius swore. He crouched to gather them. Most had come from the storage vault James established during the war: people at parties, Hogwarts tables, ruined campsites and Order rooms which no longer existed. Sirius had sorted them after moving into Hawthorn, then hidden the collection in a workshop drawer because choosing which dead to display felt obscene. One photograph had fallen face-up. James occupied the centre with Lily between his knees, his chin upon her shoulder and his glasses reflecting the sunset. Peter sat at their feet, laughing before the joke reached him. Remus held a book he had not read for several minutes. Mary Macdonald leaned against the chimney with both eyes closed. Sirius recognised himself last.

He lay across three roof tiles with his head in Marlene McKinnon's lap. Her dark hair had been cut unevenly at her jaw because she had attacked it with kitchen scissors that morning. Marlene kissed him to stop the argument. Then the moment began again. Sirius sat upon the workshop floor.

The roof had been warm beneath his back.

"That was imported," Sirius complained.

Marlene looked over the parapet. "It's in Scotland now. Very sophisticated."

"You could've asked."

"You'd have said no."

"That is generally why people ask."

"You think refusal makes you interesting."

"It has done so far."

"James is eighteen."

"Chronologically."

The answer disrupted him. Marlene saw that and smiled—not kindly, exactly, but without victory. Below them, the castle windows held the evening. They were eighteen. "Stop deciding what everyone else can survive," she said.

"I don't."

"You do it constantly. You tell me things are too dangerous, then go and do them yourself."

"Me. From hearing anything you can't turn into a joke."

"You can tell me," she said against his mouth. "I won't tell James."

He tried to smile. Her hand stayed against his cheek until he stopped trying.

The workshop returned around him. Sirius held the photograph by one corner. Marlene had been dead fourteen years. At least, that was the sentence everyone had used. The McKinnon house was destroyed. Bodies were recovered. Names entered the list. Sirius had arrived two hours too late and spent the night helping Moody identify wands from ash. He had never seen her face.

For years that detail meant nothing. War left bodies people should not be asked to recognise. Then Azkaban turned absence into an animal which worried every uncertainty until it bled. Dead was simpler. Dead did not require Sirius to ask why no grave contained enough of her to prove it. He returned the photograph to the workbench.

Harry had inherited James's instinct to place his body between love and consequence. He had inherited Lily's ability to see where adults disguised fear as logic. "Brilliant," he told the motorbike. "Excellent parenting."

"Don't start."

"He's not with me," she said.

"Your face did."

"Only metaphorically. She remains thorough."

"Not at present."

You can tell me.

Harry had found the pond during his first summer at Hawthorn. It lay beyond the orchard in a shallow fold of land, hidden from the house by willow branches and a crumbling wall. Reeds crowded one bank. Water lilies occupied the other with the calm certainty of creatures which had never been asked whether they belonged. The surface reflected sky imperfectly, broken by insects, falling leaves and the occasional silver movement of fish beneath. On difficult evenings, Harry sat upon the flat stone near the edge and threw pebbles badly. Hermione sat beside him now.

She had removed her shoes and placed them neatly upon the grass. Her toes almost touched the water. Harry still wore his, though one lace had come undone and trailed through mud.

"You were cruel," she said.

Harry threw another pebble. It struck the water once and sank.

"I know."

"You were also right about part of it."

He glanced at her.

"You usually choose one."

"People are inconveniently capable of being two things."

"Hagrid said that once."

"Hagrid is very wise when he isn't acquiring animals."

"Or eggs."

"Especially eggs."

"No. It's more accurate."

"I did not."

"There are insects."

Hermione did not answer immediately. He expected denial. She was good at producing facts which proved his worst thoughts mathematically unsound. "It changes things," she said.

Each sentence landed exactly where the thought already hurt. Hermione's eyes remained upon the pond.

"I told him he'd blame me if someone died."

"That was the cruel part."

"I know."

"Then you know why it hurt."

Harry gave her a tired look, but he could not argue with her.

"So have I."

"Yes. Which is why you knew exactly what to say."

The accuracy made Harry flinch. He had wanted Sirius to stop looking at him like the reason a door had closed. He had found the place where Sirius feared the same thing and pushed.

"I should apologise."

"When you can say it without starting the argument again."

"Lucius said to wait."

"Lucius was right."

"Don't let him hear you."

"I would like it entered into the record that I said it once under exceptional circumstances."

Harry looked at her.

"What?"

"You sometimes behave as though we are trapped in your life by the number of times you've nearly died. We aren't."

Harry's throat tightened. They had admitted liking one another beside the Hogwarts lake. They had asked for time and left without deciding what it meant for the three of them. "I suppose I still am."

"You love her too."

It was not accusation. That would have been easier.

Harry stopped breathing. Draconia continued examining the pebble, but the stillness in her body exposed the effort required not to retract the question. The answer existed. It had existed in the Chamber when her body lay upon stone and Harry discovered the world could narrow to one person breathing. It existed in every train compartment, hospital wing and letter. Love was not the difficult part.

He had told her he liked her beside the lake, but they had stopped short of this. Now she had heard what Hermione said, and she was asking where she stood. Harry did not want to give her an answer merely to keep the afternoon from hurting more. He wanted it to be true.

Draconia's father had just left for a place associated with death. Harry had hurt Sirius. "Not just because we're frightened," she agreed. "I would rather you wanted to when you weren't trying to make either of us feel better."

"Do not become accustomed to it."

"Never."

"Sirius asked whether I loved you."

"I told him I didn't want to discuss it. He had a very irritating look upon his face."

"Yes. Before I change my mind and stay here all evening."

The first real test of Hermione's procedure began at ten the following morning. Lucius had promised word by then, even if the group still expected to return before noon. At five minutes to ten, everyone in Hawthorn pretended not to watch the clock.

Narcissa sat at the kitchen table with an untouched cup of tea. Draconia stood at the window. Hermione had copied the return rules twice and was checking both versions for discrepancies which did not exist. Sirius repaired the same tear in his sleeve three times. Harry held Remus's compass.

The needle marked help pointed towards the front path, one of the places Harry had deliberately marked when they agreed where returning patrols would be met. Home pointed beneath his feet. Shelter indicated the familiar cellar, and food the bread box. None could tell him where Remus was. The compass remembered places he had visited and marked; it did not follow people or certify that a destination was safe. Ten o'clock arrived. Nothing happened. Narcissa's hand tightened around the cup.

Sirius took out his mirror and contacted the duty coordinator. He gave the missed time before asking whether the team had sent word.

Harry understood how rules failed. Not through stupidity. Through each second developing an argument of its own. Perhaps the message had been intercepted. Perhaps waiting wasted the only time in which rescue remained possible. Perhaps someone lay upon a floor expecting them to know that procedure mattered less than love. Remus's Patronus shook moonlight from its fur and opened its mouth.

The wolf dissolved. Harry looked at Hermione. The final sentence was Remus's agreed phrase, written in both books before he left. Harry recognised it. He did not know whether recognition would have felt this reassuring if the voice had belonged to someone else. One channel.

"A wound in his side," Draconia said quietly.

"Hawthorn gate."

"Year?"

"Nineteen ninety-five."

"What is happening?"

"You're hurt."

"Presently, yes. What are you doing?"

Harry forced air into his lungs.

"Waiting while they take the curse off you."

Remus's mouth moved into the shadow of a smile. "That's right. Keep watching what they're doing."

The curse came free. Lucius caught it inside an empty phial. Tonks sealed the glass. The wards around the gate flared gold, recognised Remus and opened. He took one step through. Harry reached him before he could take another.

He did not throw himself blindly forward. He saw the injured side and went to the other, fitting an arm around Remus's back while Sirius supported his weight from the left. Together, they carried him home.

Once Remus was inside and the healer had examined him, they were given a brief account of the mission. Harry listened from the kitchen doorway while Sirius fetched clean towels.

The safe house had been empty. Its occupants appeared to have left shortly before the Order arrived and destroyed most of what remained. Moody recovered fragments of ward diagrams. Tonks found correspondence written in a substitution code. Hermione offered to examine a copy if the Order could share it without exposing anyone. Lucius identified an enchantment used to manufacture obscuring masks. Remus had triggered the final curse while preventing it from attaching itself to Tonks.

They had found no names and could not yet connect the recovered material to a particular attack. Harry listened to those limits while the healer measured another potion. There was work still to do, but the four who had left Hawthorn were all back within its wards.

That evening, Remus slept in his old room beside Harry's with a bandage around his ribs and three potions upon the bedside table. Draconia had given it back without argument, carrying what she needed to Hermione's temporary room, where Dobby arranged a second bed for the night. Lucius rested downstairs while Narcissa repaired his cloak. Tonks stayed for dinner, one-handed and cheerful after pain draught. Moody departed through the window again because changing a habit after one objection would imply weakness.

Sirius opened the dining-room doors once the papers had been secured. The privacy wards would still be used for meetings; this evening nobody had a reason to leave them in place.

The old photograph stood upon the mantel where the picture frame had fallen the day before. Harry had repaired the glass himself. Before framing it, he had asked whether they could crop the lower edge where Peter sat. Sirius had agreed without asking Harry to defend the choice, and kept the untrimmed original with the other private photographs. In the copy upon the mantel, James laughed soundlessly and Lily leant back against him. Remus read. Mary slept. Marlene stole Sirius's cigarette, kissed him and began again. Not every part of the past could be arranged into innocence and betrayal. Some questions remained because nobody present had seen enough to answer them. Harry sat at the kitchen table with the people who had returned.

Hermione amended the verification procedure in blue ink. Draconia held her father's hand beneath the table where he could pretend she did not. Sirius cut bread. Remus, awake despite every instruction, called through the ceiling that no one should allow Sirius near the stove.

"It's bread," Sirius shouted back.

"You burnt water last week."

"The pot burnt. The water escaped."

Harry laughed, then looked across the table at Draconia. Her hand was still beneath her father's, and she did not withdraw it when Lucius reached for his cup. Hermione crossed out a line in the procedure and wrote its replacement carefully: a missed check meant contacting the coordinator immediately, even if they hoped it was only the clocks.

"Do we all agree?" she asked.

Sirius glanced up from the bread. "I do."

Draconia nodded. "Keep both versions. Father will try to argue with the wording after he's slept."

"I can hear you," Lucius said.

"Good. Then you can agree now."

Harry took the pen Hermione offered him and added his name, roughly folded the signed page then passed it to back to Hermione.
