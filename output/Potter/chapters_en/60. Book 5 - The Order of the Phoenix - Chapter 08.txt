8

Dawn pressed weakly against the dormitory windows as rain tapped a familiar rhythm against the glass. The remains of another nightmare clung to Harry without pictures: constriction around his chest, the smell of wet stone, and the certainty that something terrible waited on the other side of waking.

His shoulder ached beneath its bandage. The pain was real. The train had been three days ago. Yesterday, Madam Pomfrey had finally allowed them back to their dormitories, with instructions about meals, stairs and the symptoms which meant coming straight back. Harry had nodded at every instruction. Last night, faced with his own bed, he had almost asked to stay.

He had missed the two beds beside his. In the hospital wing he could hear Hermione turn a page and see Draconia's curtains move when she stretched. Here, the other beds contained boys who snored, dropped shoes and occasionally shouted in their sleep. He knew them. It still took effort to remember that a different room did not mean something had happened to the girls. Sirius was at Hawthorn. Hermione slept in the girls' dormitory on the other side of the common room. Draconia was beneath the lake. Harry would see both of them at breakfast, provided he got up before Hermione came looking for him. He was at Hogwarts, in Gryffindor Tower. He could hear Neville wrestling with a shoelace and Dean searching for something beneath his bed. Harry let those ordinary sounds settle around him before pushing himself upright.

Harry opened the curtains. Seamus Finnigan sat upon the end of his bed holding a letter. Dean stood beside him half dressed, one sleeve of his shirt hanging empty. Neville had frozen halfway through tying his shoes. Ron faced Seamus from the other side of the room with his tie looped around one hand. Nobody looked as though breakfast remained the subject.

"Sorry," Harry said. "Could you repeat that? I wasn't properly awake for the accusation."

Seamus's face flushed.

"I didn't accuse you."

"Excellent. Saves time."

Harry reached for his glasses. The movement pulled his shoulder. He hid the reaction badly enough that Neville noticed and well enough that Seamus pretended not to.

"Me mam wants me home," Seamus said.

Harry put on the glasses. The room sharpened. Five beds. School trunks. Rain tracking silver across the window. The folded front page of yesterday's Prophet upon the floor beneath Seamus's foot.

"Are you going?"

"I don't know."

"Then why are you asking me?"

Seamus gripped the letter. "She says the Tournament happened because of you. Then the train. Every year since we got here, something comes after Harry Potter and the rest of us end up in it."

Dean finally found the end of his sleeve.

"That's not what she wrote."

"You read it?"

"You read half of it aloud."

"She said the school is using us as protection for him."

Harry swung his legs from the bed. His feet touched cold stone. Something inside him wanted to apologise.

The instinct arrived before thought, polished by years at Privet Drive: take responsibility quickly, make yourself smaller, promise not to cause the trouble which had already chosen you. He looked at the newspaper beneath Seamus's foot. Unknown extremists. Attempted abduction. A photograph taken after the fighting.

"They came for me," Harry said.

Seamus's anger flickered.

"That's what I'm saying."

"No. You're saying I brought them."

"Is there a difference to the people on the train?"

Ron threw the tie onto his bed.

"Yeah. There is."

Seamus looked at him. Ron stepped between the beds. His tie dragged from the mattress behind him.

"He didn't invite them, Seamus."

"I know that."

"Then stop talking as if he did."

Seamus unfolded the letter again, though he did not look at it. "You weren't there when they broke the window in our carriage. Dean had glass in his hair. There was a girl under the seat who wouldn't stop screaming. I couldn't get her to move."

Dean stopped fussing with his cuff. Harry remembered a small hand tightening around his neck. He had carried that boy forward while the fingers stayed clenched in his collar. For a moment, nobody seemed inclined to say anything clever.

"I was there," Ron said, more quietly. "In a different carriage. They were all screaming in mine too."

"Me mam thinks if I come home—"

"Then talk about going home. Don't ask him why he hasn't made the Death Eaters behave."

"Ron," Dean warned.

"What? I spent half the attack dragging first-years out of compartments while he stopped the people trying to curse them. Now Seamus wants him to apologise for being there?"

"I didn't ask for an apology."

Harry had already begun constructing one. He hated that Ron could see it.

"You asked whether he plans to get us killed," Ron said. "Unless Potter has started scheduling Death Eaters, that's not his department."

Harry reached for his shirt.

"Thursday," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"I was planning death for Thursday. Before breakfast is inconsiderate."

Neville made a startled sound which became a laugh. Dean covered his mouth. Even Seamus's expression shifted before guilt returned it to anger.

"You think this is funny?"

"No."

Harry eased his right arm into his shirt, then worked the left sleeve up without lifting his strapped shoulder. The effort made his next words come out less casually than he intended.

"I think if I don't make jokes, I start apologising. I'm trying something new."

Nobody answered at once. Harry had meant to make the explanation sound like another joke, but he had given them too much of the truth for that. Seamus looked down at his mother's letter and ran his thumb along its folded edge.

"She is frightened."

"She should be."

"She thinks Dumbledore's lying about You-Know-Who."

"Then she should read something other than the Prophet."

"What if she's right?"

Harry fastened the buttons with one hand.

"About Voldemort?"

Seamus flinched at the name. "About you not knowing what you saw."

The graveyard entered the dormitory. Not visibly. Rain continued upon the glass. Neville's shoe remained half tied. Dean held his shirt cuff between two fingers. Harry felt rope around his wrists. He took the half-second Remus had taught him. Longer when he could afford it.

"Then she can read Cedric's account," he said. "Or Lucius Malfoy's. Or the statements from people all along the train who saw the Mark on Friday. She doesn't have to trust my memory to know someone is trying very hard to stop everyone else using theirs."

Seamus had no immediate answer. Harry managed the buttons, although the last one needed two attempts. His tie proved worse. He pulled the narrow end through the wrong loop and tightened it before he noticed. Ron watched for several painful seconds.

"You've strangled the narrow end."

"It was hostile."

"Give it here."

Harry stared at him.

"The tie."

"I know."

"Then stop making it strange."

Harry handed it over. Ron untangled the knot and redid it with the ease of someone who had spent years wearing school clothes inherited from people of different heights.

"Mum does this too tight," he said. "Fred leaves it loose enough to conceal contraband. Percy measured the distance from the collar."

"And you?"

"Usually forget until Hermione sees me."

He passed it back. The knot was straight. Harry slipped it over his head; Ron lifted it clear of his glasses without making a performance of the help.

"Thanks."

Ron shrugged. The gesture did not ask Harry to ignore what had happened before it. Neither did it require payment. Seamus folded the letter.

"I'm not leaving today."

Harry looked at him.

"All right."

"That isn't an apology."

"No."

"But I shouldn't have asked it like that."

"Probably not."

Dean threw a sock at Harry.

"He's trying."

Harry caught it with his good hand.

"So am I."

He threw it back. Dean caught the sock against his chest and returned to getting dressed. Seamus tucked the letter into his trunk rather than leaving it out for another argument. Harry still felt the accusation, but at least he had not apologised for surviving. That would have to be enough for one morning.

The owl post had acquired inspectors. Four Ministry employees stood beneath the western gallery in the Great Hall wearing pale robes and dragon-hide gloves. A silver hoop hung beneath the high windows, and the inspectors were trying to direct every arriving owl through it. Letters glowed according to categories Harry could not read. Green envelopes continued. Amber ones were drawn gently down towards a locked Ministry basket while their carriers protested above it. Red caused the inspectors to raise their wands. Already two owls had perched upon the hoop itself, refusing to move. The owls hated it. Hedwig hated it personally. She entered through the highest window, saw the hoop and changed direction with a sound of profound offence. An inspector whistled. Hedwig accelerated.

"Oh no," Hermione said.

Harry looked up from his porridge. Hedwig circled the enchanted ceiling once. Two Ministry wands tracked her, but one inspector lowered his when she swept above a table of first-years. She folded a wing, dropped below the hoop and landed in Harry's breakfast hard enough to overturn the milk. Porridge covered his sleeve. Hedwig extended one leg.

"Good morning to you too."

She clicked her beak. An inspector reached the Gryffindor table.

"That bird bypassed a lawful screening ward."

Harry wiped milk from the envelope attached to her leg. "I'll explain the law to her."

"The letter must be examined."

Hedwig rotated her head towards the man's fingers. He stopped reaching. The envelope bore the black Hawthorn seal and three smaller marks beneath it: registered guardian correspondence, a private curse-screening seal and active protective warding. Across the front, Sirius had written in large letters:

TO HARRY. NOT THE GOVERNMENT.

Hermione closed her eyes.

"Subtle," she said.

"He considered red ink," Harry replied.

The inspector examined the seals. "Guardian correspondence may still be screened for active curses."

"It's already been screened. That's one of the seals."

"Those are private wards."

"Very observant."

The man touched the envelope with his wand. It barked. The bark broke into Sirius's unmistakable voice, loud enough to reach the staff table.

"GET YOUR OWN POST."

Every head turned. Fred and George Weasley began applauding.

The inspector's face darkened. "Obstruction of Ministry process—"

Professor McGonagall arrived beside him.

"The envelope contains a registered guardian ward approved by the Wizengamot. Your screening charm activated its notification response."

"It shouted at me."

"Efficiently."

"I must report this."

"Spell Mr Black's name correctly. He is particular."

McGonagall waited until the inspector had returned to the gallery, then touched the seal with her own wand. A fine white light passed over the envelope. "No active curse," she said, quietly enough for Harry alone. "Next time, your guardian might provide a less theatrical notification." Harry doubted it. He removed Sirius's letter while laughter travelled through the Hall. Sirius had crammed the visible message onto one side of the sheet.

Harry,

The house remains standing. Remus says this should not be noteworthy. He is wrong.

Mirror at lunch. Eat breakfast. Do not allow anyone in pink to confiscate anything without obtaining their request in writing, preferably in triplicate.

Padfoot

When Harry placed his thumb upon the Hawthorn seal, different ink rose beneath the signature.

You are loved. Come home sooner if you need to.

The words disappeared after he read them. Harry folded the letter carefully. The hidden words had vanished, but he knew where they had been. His thumb rested over the empty space for a moment before he tucked the sheet away.

Yesterday Sirius had helped him carry his things up to the tower and stood in the doorway until Harry said he could manage. Harry had been pleased to say it. He had also watched the door after it closed. A letter arriving at breakfast should not have made so much difference, yet suddenly he could imagine the kitchen at Hawthorn continuing without him: the mugs, the chair Remus preferred, somebody opening a window because Sirius had burned the toast. Hermione pretended to repair the spilled milk. Ron became interested in the ceiling.

Across the Hall, Draconia watched Harry tuck the envelope inside his robes. She wore her prefect badge, although Madam Pomfrey had excused her from duties for a week. Her face remained pale against the dark green collar. Pansy sat upon one side and Theodore Nott upon the other, separated from Draconia by an empty seat which had not existed yesterday. Theodore was not eating.

A copy of the Prophet lay folded beside his plate. His father's name appeared above the crease. Draconia looked from the paper to Harry. For an instant, something vulnerable passed through her expression. Then Pansy said something, and Draconia became composed again. Hermione followed Harry's gaze.

"She shouldn't be wearing the badge."

"Tell her."

"I did."

"What happened?"

"She explained that being medically excused is not the same as becoming incompetent."

"Sounds right."

Hermione buttered toast with more force than necessary. Harry looked more closely at her. The bandage had been removed from her head, but a faint bruise remained at her temple. Shadows darkened the skin beneath her eyes. She rubbed one of them with the heel of her hand, then blinked hard at her toast.

"What time did you get to sleep?"

"I did sleep."

"That wasn't the question."

Ron put another piece of toast on Harry's plate. "Don't involve me. I haven't finished waking up."

Hermione's notebook lay beside the butter dish. A strip of parchment protruded from it, covered in much smaller writing than she usually used.

"The statements?" Harry asked.

"I only meant to finish the index. Then I found two accounts of the same compartment with different numbers, and if I left them—"

"Someone could ask today."

"I know."

She said it too quickly. Harry had heard that voice when the Time-Turner made every unfinished task feel like a personal failure. He hated how readily he recognised it now.

"How much sleep?"

Hermione looked towards the staff table. "About two hours."

"Pomfrey let you out yesterday. She didn't say you could stop sleeping."

"People are helping. Padma's doing the Ravenclaw accounts. Ernie has the Hufflepuff copies."

"Good. Let them do the index as well."

Harry reached towards the notebook. Hermione put her hand over it, and he stopped before touching the cover. Taking it would only give them something else to argue about.

"At lunch," he said. "Could you give it to Padma and Ernie until tomorrow? They know what they're doing. You don't have to stay awake to make the work count."

"Harry, I can't keep handing things over whenever you're worried."

"You could try it once. For variety."

"You are not my prefect."

The badge wound was too new for the sentence not to find it. Hermione realised after speaking. Harry looked down at his porridge.

"No," he said.

"Harry, I didn't mean—"

"I know."

Knowing did not stop the hurt. Hermione's hand moved across the table and covered his.

"I didn't mean you weren't allowed to care," she said. Her fingers tightened gently over his. "I meant you can't order me to stop. I didn't mean you weren't allowed to ask. I'm tired, and that came out horribly."

Harry looked up.

"Then let me worry a bit. You'd be doing exactly the same if it was me."

The resistance left her face.

"Lunch," she agreed.

"Could lunch just be lunch? No reading while you eat."

"I will limit the reading," Hermione said, with the air of someone offering a considerable concession.

"No."

"One article."

"Half."

"That is not how articles work."

"Then choose a short one."

Hermione's mouth twitched. Across the Hall, Draconia had stopped pretending not to watch. Harry caught her eye and pointed towards Hermione, then mimed closing a book. Draconia understood immediately. She gave one decisive nod. Hermione saw the exchange.

"You recruited her?"

"Efficiently."

"This is a conspiracy."

Ron drank his tea. "Apparently being your friend means getting told when to eat."

Harry smiled.

"You're learning."

By the time Draconia reached the dungeons, the wound beneath her ribs had begun to burn. She did not alter her pace. Pansy noticed anyway.

"You are limping."

"I am walking carefully."

"With one side."

"Most people possess two, Parkinson. Perhaps you could try walking with yours instead of inspecting mine."

Millicent moved closer upon Draconia's other side without commenting. Together, they descended the final staircase while the castle's warmth faded behind them. The Slytherin corridor had changed during the two nights Draconia spent away from it. No decoration had moved. Silver lamps still burned inside their wall brackets. The same line of carved serpents guarded the common-room entrance, eyes opening as students approached. Yet conversation lowered before Draconia reached it. Two third-year boys stepped aside too quickly. A sixth-year witch who had spent the previous term requesting invitations to Malfoy functions examined the stone floor. The Manor no longer made proximity desirable. Draconia gave the password. The wall opened.

Green light moved across the common room in slow bands. The lake pressed against the high windows, dark with rain. Schools of silver fish turned beyond the glass as one body, their scales flashing and vanishing between weeds. Every seat nearest the fire was occupied. Every conversation stopped. Pansy looked around.

"Subtle," she said.

The room remembered itself. Pages turned. A chess piece moved. Someone near the windows began a sentence in the middle and used the wrong verb. Draconia crossed towards the dormitory stairs.

"Malfoy."

Graham Montague waited by the fire. The seventh-year had one elbow upon the mantel and a small audience upon the nearest sofa. He waited until the room was listening before he went on.

"Montague."

"My father wants to know whether yours arranged the train attack."

Pansy stopped. Millicent's hands closed. Draconia looked at Montague.

"Does he?"

"The paper says former associates may be retaliating against him. Convenient way to make himself important."

"Father already possesses methods of becoming important which do not involve having me cut open."

Montague's gaze fell to the guarded way she held her side. It rose again.

"Perhaps he miscalculated."

Fear moved through the room. Not fear of Draconia. Fear that Montague had spoken aloud what several families wrote privately.

Draconia had been raised to answer insult through precision. Identify the weakness. Apply pressure. Leave the other person uncertain whether they had been threatened until acting upon it was no longer useful. She could list Montague's father's debts, his brother's failed Ministry application and the origin of the money which purchased their summer home. Lucius would have. The old Lucius. The one who could make a room admire the knife before noticing who bled. Draconia's wound burned.

"Your father can write to mine," she said. "He doesn't need you asking me in front of an audience."

A laugh escaped near the sofa. Montague's face darkened.

"You think Potter protects you now?"

The question struck closer than he knew. Draconia saw Harry upon the roof, both hands locked around her wrist while the track moved beneath her and the tether strained against its anchor. She felt his arm around her upon the broom, heard him ask still here? as though the answer held his world in place.

"Harry didn't teach me to answer back," she replied. "You've known me long enough to remember that."

Montague looked towards Pansy and Millicent. Neither had moved away.

"Your families may regret this."

Pansy smiled without warmth. "Mother regrets most things eventually. She survives."

Millicent cracked one knuckle. The conversation ended. Draconia climbed the stairs.

She reached the dormitory before allowing one hand to touch the wall. Pain travelled from her ribs through her back. The curse had been removed, but her body did not understand the difference between healed and unthreatened. Each step pulled the new skin. Sweat cooled beneath her collar.

"Sit," Millicent said.

"I require my books."

"Pansy can find books."

Pansy opened Draconia's trunk. "I can hear the insult."

"Good."

Draconia sat upon the edge of her bed. The mattress dipped. Lake light shifted across the canopy. For one disorienting second, the green became the Chamber's reflected water and the closed curtains became stone walls too high for escape. Her fingers scraped across the blanket. For a moment she expected the smooth edge of the diary beneath them, as though finding it could tell her where she was. Nothing lay beneath them. The diary had been destroyed just over two years ago. Harry had found her. The hand against her wrist belonged to Pansy.

"Draco."

She focused. Pansy's face sharpened above her. Millicent stood beside the bed holding the pain potion Madam Pomfrey had placed inside Draconia's satchel.

"I am fine," Draconia said.

"You attempted to claw through the blanket."

Draconia looked down. Four threads had pulled loose beneath her nails.

"The light changed."

Pansy glanced towards the lake. She knew enough not to ask what Draconia had seen.

"Drink this."

Millicent held out the potion. Draconia accepted it. The liquid tasted of aniseed and left her tongue numb. Warmth spread beneath the wound, lowering the pain from flame to pressure.

"You could have stayed in the hospital wing," Pansy said.

"I have lessons."

"You have an excuse."

"So does everyone who wishes to avoid something unpleasant."

Pansy's expression hardened. "Nearly dying is not avoidance."

Draconia placed the empty vial upon the bedside table.

"I know."

"Do you?"

The words sounded like Harry. That made them more irritating. Pansy removed books from the trunk one at a time. Potions. Charms. Transfiguration. She reached beneath a folded cloak and found a slim black notebook. Draconia stopped breathing. Pansy froze. The cover was ordinary. No initials. No green leather. A new school ledger Narcissa had purchased in Diagon Alley and packed without thinking. Blank pages pressed beneath black cloth. Ink disappearing into paper. A voice asking what Harry feared.

Draconia's hands became cold. Millicent took the notebook from Pansy. She did not throw it into the fire. Sudden destruction was its own kind of fear. She wrapped it inside the folded cloak and placed both upon the highest shelf.

"It can stay there," she said.

Draconia forced air into her lungs.

"It is a notebook," Draconia said. "An ordinary one."

"Then it won't mind waiting up there," Millicent replied.

"I should be able to use it."

Pansy closed the trunk. "Use the loose parchment today. You've got enough things to be miserable about."

Draconia looked at the high shelf. Part of the cloak hung over its edge; she could see the stitching Narcissa had repaired last Christmas. The notebook had been put away without anyone demanding that she explain herself, or declaring her brave for remaining in the room with it. She had not known how much she wanted that until it happened. Millicent began searching for a clean handkerchief. Pansy complained about the state of Draconia's trunk, although she had finished arranging every book before the complaint ended. Neither looked directly at her. Draconia took another breath and found it easier than the last.

Pansy pushed the Potions book into line with the others, then moved it again because the edge was still crooked. Draconia watched her for a moment and decided to leave the fussing unchallenged. A voice rose from the bottom of the dormitory stairs.

"Draconia?"

Pansy looked towards the open door.

"That is Theodore."

"A remarkable identification."

"He is shouting into the girls' corridor."

Millicent gathered Draconia's books. Pansy took the satchel. Draconia stood without accepting either arm offered to her and descended with them. Theodore Nott waited beyond the final step in a curtained alcove off the common room. The old staircase wards kept both of his feet firmly upon the green carpet. He looked thinner than he had in June. His dark hair had been cut too short around the ears, as though someone unfamiliar had done it quickly. The school robes fitted, but the cuffs belonged to last year's uniform and had been let down by hand. The heir of one of Britain's oldest families wore altered robes. Draconia noticed, then wished she had not. She knew nothing about who was looking after him while his father remained a fugitive, or whether there was anyone he could ask for something as ordinary as a new uniform.

"Shouting into the girls' dormitory is generally discouraged," Pansy said.

Theodore glanced at the silver runes glowing above the first step. "The stair made the position clear."

"I need to speak with Draconia."

Millicent remained between him and Draconia. Draconia adjusted her posture until the wound no longer showed upon her face.

"What is it, Theodore?"

He looked at Pansy and Millicent.

"Alone."

"Not with Pansy and Millicent sent away," Draconia said. "You can tell me here."

He accepted the refusal too quickly.

"Umbridge summoned me last night."

Draconia's attention sharpened.

"Why?"

"Student welfare."

Pansy made a disgusted sound.

"What did she ask?" Draconia said.

"Whether my father contacted me over summer. Whether I knew anyone involved in the attack. Whether your father had spoken about old associates at school."

"And?"

"I told her no to all three. She asked the first one again before she let me leave."

His answers were prepared. That did not make them false. Theodore reached inside his robes. All three girls drew their wands. He stopped.

"It is parchment."

"Remove it slowly," Draconia said.

Theodore did. He held out a folded Ministry notice. A pink seal marked the corner. Pansy accepted it, examined both sides and passed it to Draconia.

The notice confirmed a weekly welfare appointment with the Commissioner's office. Attendance compulsory. Discussion confidential except where a threat to student safety required disclosure. Theodore's name had been written in Umbridge's round script.

"She wants you to report on Slytherin," Draconia said.

"She wants me to believe that refusing makes me resemble my father."

"You don't have to do what she asks simply to prove you're different," Draconia said.

His eyes lifted to hers. "Would you believe that if it was me saying it?"

The question carried years. His father's diary inside Draconia's trunk. The Chamber. The Tournament. The attack upon the train. Every newspaper using Nott as a complete explanation because one fugitive made Voldemort easier to deny. Theodore had not placed the diary. Harry had told her what Riddle said in the Chamber; Nott's father had been responsible. Whatever Theodore knew now, she had no evidence that he had helped him then.

He had not stood in the graveyard. He had also never challenged the advantages purchased by the man who did. Neither had Draconia, until those advantages became a weapon inside her own hands.

"No," she said. "It makes you a person refusing an order. What you do after matters more."

Theodore's expression did not soften. Relief did not always resemble gratitude.

"She asked about Potter's mirror," he said.

Draconia's grip tightened upon the notice.

"How does she know about it?"

"She mentioned it during the appointment. I don't know who told her."

Draconia thought of the hearing, where Sirius's guardianship had been examined in public, then of the field and the hospital wing. Too many people had seen the mirror for its existence to remain private. She knew the mirror carried privileged guardian communication, immediate access to Sirius and perhaps the only channel she could not inspect without challenging his legal guardianship directly.

"What did you tell her?"

"That I've never used it and don't know how it works. Then I said Potter owns plenty of ugly objects. She didn't enjoy that part."

Pansy smiled despite herself. Draconia folded the notice.

"Why warn me?"

Theodore looked towards the lake windows.

"Because everyone in this alcove is waiting to learn whether I become him."

"I was waiting to hear what you wanted," Draconia said. "That isn't quite the same thing."

"That isn't an answer."

"No."

Draconia placed the notice upon the side table.

"Your father put the diary in my room," she said. "You didn't. I know that. But if you start giving her things to use against us, that's you."

Theodore glanced towards the folded notice.

"I haven't."

"Then keep not doing it."

Theodore absorbed this.

"Fair."

"Do not mistake fairness for trust."

"I wouldn't."

He turned to leave. At the edge of the alcove, he stopped.

"Montague's father is organising a letter among Slytherin parents. They intend to request that you be removed as prefect because your family cannot be politically neutral."

Draconia looked at the green-edged badge upon her robes. Harry's missing badge appeared in her mind. Umbridge had called his trauma disqualifying. Now Slytherin families would call Draconia's loyalties dangerous. So Harry was supposedly too damaged to be trusted with a badge, while she might lose hers for refusing to condemn her father. Draconia pressed the enamel between her fingers until its edge hurt. Neither objection seemed to have much to do with helping younger students find their classrooms.

"Thank you," she said.

Theodore left. Pansy drew the curtain across the alcove.

"Do you believe him?"

"Enough to verify."

"And after?"

Draconia touched the badge.

"After, we learn who signs."

Millicent handed her the Potions book.

"Harry is going to notice you're in pain."

"Potter notices distress only when it belongs to someone else."

Pansy lifted one eyebrow. "He noticed you across the Great Hall."

Draconia's face warmed.

"He was looking at Theodore."

"Of course."

"Pansy."

"I said nothing."

"You are enjoying yourself far too much."

Pansy smiled. Draconia stood carefully. The pain potion had dulled the wound. It had not removed the letter, the black notebook above her bed or the Ministry appointment beneath Theodore's name. She fastened her robes over all of it. Then she went to meet Harry in the dungeons.

Harry knew Draconia was in pain before she reached the classroom. She walked normally. That was the problem.

Every step possessed the same measured length. Her shoulders remained level. She held her books close against her body, but her right elbow scarcely moved as she walked. It was meant to look composed; Harry recognised the care she was taking not to pull at the healing wound. Before Friday, Harry might have believed her. Now he knew what strategic leaning looked like. He left the queue outside Potions and met her halfway along the corridor.

"Should you be here?" Harry asked.

"Good morning to you too."

"Draco."

"Pomfrey said I could attend if I took the potion and came back when it wore off. I did one. I intend to do the other."

Hermione joined them carrying twice as many books as the timetable required.

"She took it," she said.

Draconia looked at her. "How could you know that?"

"You smell of aniseed, and you're trying very hard not to swallow. I had the same potion on Saturday."

Harry examined Draconia's eyes. She turned her face away.

"Granger's surveillance has become invasive."

"You're wearing your badge," Harry said.

"Wearing it isn't the same as doing rounds."

"Pomfrey excused you," Harry said. "Nobody should be asking you to look after them yet."

"She did not remove my appointment."

Harry looked at the green enamel. Her gaze dropped briefly to the place on his chest where a badge might have been. Her expression softened.

"Do not use your own argument against me," she said.

"It's an excellent argument."

"It is unfair when you know perfectly well why I want to keep wearing it."

"I know," Harry said. "You can wear the badge and still let us carry a few books."

Hermione took the upper half of Draconia's books. Draconia allowed it. She usually made him ask twice. Harry took care not to show how much he noticed. The classroom door opened. Professor Snape stood inside.

"If the three of you intend to conduct a medical conference, the hospital wing contains fewer cauldrons to contaminate."

Harry entered. "Madam Pomfrey let us out yesterday."

"Her first error of the term."

"Did you miss us, then?"

Snape's eyes narrowed. Then they moved to Draconia. The hostility did not disappear. It adjusted.

"Your seat has been moved to the front."

Draconia looked towards the first table. Three chairs stood where two usually belonged. The aisle beside them was wide enough for Madam Pomfrey's stretcher charm. A cushion had been placed upon the chair nearest the wall and disguised badly as part of the dungeon furniture. Draconia looked back at Snape.

"Unnecessary."

"Then you will survive the inconvenience."

Pansy smiled behind her hand. Harry took the chair beside Draconia before anyone else could. Hermione sat on his other side.

"This arrangement appears familiar," Harry said.

"There is more room," Hermione replied.

"A tragic loss."

Draconia opened her book. "Try to endure it."

The other students settled around them. Gryffindor and Slytherin divided themselves by habit except where the train had disturbed it. Millicent sat beside Neville because both arrived at the same cauldron and neither felt strongly enough to move. Ron chose a table with Dean, then shifted one seat when Seamus entered and left the decision open. Theodore Nott sat alone in the second row. Harry noticed Draconia look at him once. He noticed the folded pink notice protruding from Theodore's bag. Before he could ask, the dungeon door opened again. Umbridge entered carrying a clipboard.

Every conversation stopped. Her robes were a softer pink than yesterday. A black velvet bow sat upon her head. Harry could not look at the bow without thinking how carefully she must have chosen it that morning, while children in the hospital wing were still waiting to have their dressings changed. Snape did not greet her. He pointed his wand at the blackboard. Instructions appeared in white chalk.

THE STABILISING DRAFT

Stage One: Prepare a neutral suspension.

Stage Two: Introduce volatile magical residue without activation.

Stage Three: Bind, clarify and bottle.

Beneath the steps appeared a list of ingredients and a warning in capital letters.

IMPRECISE HEAT WILL PRODUCE AN EDUCATIONAL EXPLOSION.

"Today," Snape said, "you will discover whether four years of instruction have produced the capacity to follow twelve consecutive steps without improvisation, conversation or permanent scarring. The Stabilising Draft is used to contain magical residue before analysis. Aurors, curse-breakers and competent healers rely upon it. The rest of you may consider passing your OWL an ambitious preliminary application."

Umbridge wrote something upon the clipboard. Snape looked at her.

"If the Commissioner requires parchment, I have several students who will not need theirs after the first stage."

"Please continue as though I am not here."

Snape's gaze travelled over the pink robes, clipboard and chair she had placed beside his desk.

"An advanced exercise."

Harry looked down before he laughed. The first ingredient was powdered moonstone. Hermione measured it to the fraction of a gram. Draconia adjusted the flame. Harry cut valerian roots with his right hand, keeping the board close enough that he did not have to brace it with his injured arm. The sling lay folded on his lap; Madam Pomfrey had allowed him to remove it for careful work at a table, provided he put it back before leaving. His slices became progressively less equal. Draconia watched the fourth.

"That one is twice the required width."

"It has ambition."

"It has ruined the surface ratio."

"Hermione?"

"She's right."

"You could have pretended to consider it."

Hermione reached for the knife. Harry kept his fingers around the handle, and she stopped before trying to take it.

"I can cut roots."

"I can see that. I was hoping we might cut them to the same width as well."

"They had it coming," Harry said, examining the largest slice. "Especially that one."

Draconia placed her hand over his. The argument stopped. Her fingers were cool against his knuckles. She adjusted the angle of the blade without taking it away.

"Use the heel," she said. "Not the point. Let the blade do the work."

Harry looked at their hands. Draconia did too. Neither moved. Hermione added the moonstone with unnecessary concentration.

"The potion requires valerian before the surface settles."

"Yes," Draconia said.

Her hand left Harry's. He cut the next root perfectly.

"Show-off," she murmured.

"Excellent teacher."

Colour entered the tips of her ears. Harry smiled at the cutting board. The Stabilising Draft turned from clear to silver as Hermione added the roots. For ten minutes, the classroom contained only knives, low voices and the hiss of regulated flames.

Umbridge moved between tables. She did not examine the potions. She examined the students. At Neville and Millicent's cauldron, she asked whether Snape regularly used the possibility of bodily harm to motivate compliance. Millicent glanced at the flame Neville had turned down just in time. "Mostly he wants us to watch what we're doing." Neville looked grateful for approximately a second, until Snape asked why their moonstone remained on the scales.

At Ron's table, Umbridge asked whether practical brewing so soon after a traumatic incident caused distress.

"Not as much as the train," Ron said.

"Comparison does not invalidate concern."

"Didn't say it did."

She wrote upon the clipboard. When she reached Harry's table, the air altered. Harry continued slicing valerian. Umbridge watched the way he kept his left elbow close to his side.

"Mr Potter, does your injury prevent you from participating safely?"

"Madam Pomfrey said I could do this."

"With limitations."

"Mostly on how many roots I can insult."

Draconia's knee touched his beneath the table. A warning. Or encouragement. With her it could be both.

Umbridge looked at the shared cauldron. "I understood individual assessment was essential during OWL preparation."

Snape answered from behind her.

"I assess the product. Cooperation does not prevent incompetence from becoming visible. It frequently accelerates it."

"Three students are producing one potion."

"Three injured students are completing a practical exercise which requires lifting a cauldron at two stages."

Harry glanced towards Snape. The professor's face contained no defence of them. Only contempt for interference. It felt oddly similar.

Umbridge smiled. "You made an accommodation without documenting it through the Commissioner's welfare office."

"I moved a chair."

"The principle remains."

"The chair does too."

Hermione coughed into her sleeve. Umbridge wrote another note.

"How long have you taught at Hogwarts, Professor Snape?"

"Fourteen years."

"And you originally applied for the Defence position?"

The knife stopped in Harry's hand. Snape's voice became softer.

"Repeatedly."

"Yet successive Headmasters have considered you better suited to Potions."

"There has been one Headmaster during my employment."

"Quite."

Umbridge made a deliberate mark.

"Your curriculum includes poisons, volatile ingredients and illegal substances. Do you consider that appropriate for children already exposed to dark activity?"

Snape looked at her clipboard.

"Ignorance does not neutralise poison."

"Theory may be taught without preparation."

"One may also teach swimming without water. The results become apparent later."

Harry liked him for nearly two seconds. Snape noticed.

"Potter, if your root becomes any thinner, it will qualify as dust. Five points from Gryffindor."

The feeling passed. Umbridge returned to her chair. Draconia's knee remained against Harry's. He added the final root. The draft cleared. Silver sediment drew together inside the cauldron, capturing every stray trace of magic and suspending it in a bright sphere above the bottom.

"Stage two," Hermione said.

Harry reached for the vial of residue. It contained harmless charm remnants collected from discarded classroom objects. The substance moved like oil and smoke, green at the edges where the flame shone through it. Draconia's hand shook upon the ladle. Only once. The green light from the cauldron touched her face. Harry saw her pupils widen.

Her gaze had stopped following the vial. Harry knew that stillness: her eyes remained open, but whatever held her attention was no longer upon the table. The light reminded him of the Chamber, though he could not know whether she was remembering the same thing. He did not say her name loudly enough for the room to turn. He placed his hand beneath the table, palm open. Draconia looked down. For a moment, she did nothing. Then her fingers slid into his. Harry held them loosely. No restraint. No demand that she return faster. Hermione continued reading the instructions aloud in the same measured voice.

"Add three drops. Counter-clockwise stir. Allow the residue to reach the suspension before applying the binding agent."

Draconia breathed. She glanced at Hermione's book, then at the ladle. Her thumb moved once against Harry's, and he felt her grip ease. Her hand steadied.

She released Harry and lifted the ladle. Harry held the vial over the cauldron and counted three drops into the suspension while she stirred. The potion held. Draconia watched its surface settle, then set down the ladle before her hand could shake again. Harry said nothing. Her shoulder rested against his for the remainder of the lesson; whenever she needed more room to work, she moved away, then returned without looking at him.

Snape detained Harry after the other students left. Hermione stopped in the doorway. Draconia stood beside her with one hand upon the frame.

"Alone, Potter," Snape said.

Harry looked at them. "Lunch. You promised me you'd hand the index over."

Hermione frowned. "This is not the time."

"Then remember it for when it is."

Draconia almost smiled.

"We will wait outside."

Snape reached for the door. Harry put his hand against its edge before it closed.

"Not all the way, please."

The professor looked at his hand, then at him. He left a narrow gap. Neither girl moved away from the corridor, but Harry could no longer see their faces watching the conversation. Harry turned.

"They'll be offended."

"A condition with which they have extensive experience."

Snape cleared the front table with one sweep of his wand. The failed potions vanished. Cauldrons arranged themselves along the shelves. Umbridge's chair scraped backwards into a dark corner with unnecessary force.

"The Headmaster informed me of your request."

"One Occlumency lesson. Remus present."

"Professor Lupin."

"He lets me call him Remus."

"His lapses in judgement are not today's subject."

Snape placed both hands upon the desk.

"You will misunderstand the purpose of Occlumency if you believe it consists of constructing a wall and hoping the Dark Lord becomes bored. Thought suppression fails. Emotional denial fails more dramatically. You will learn to recognise intrusion, control what it reaches and remove yourself from the emotional state which provides access."

Harry remembered the train corridor.

"Can you tell if the thought was his?"

"Possibly."

"Dumbledore said he didn't know."

"The Headmaster does not share the Dark Lord's habits."

"You do?"

Snape's eyes became black and flat.

"I understand them."

Harry took the correction.

"What happens during the lesson?"

Snape seemed faintly surprised by the question.

"We begin without Legilimency. You will identify a recent memory, establish its sensory boundaries and practise withdrawing attention from it without destroying detail. If you demonstrate sufficient control, I will test the edge of the memory with your permission."

"And if I say stop?"

"I stop."

"No points?"

"Not for stopping. Failure to prepare remains available."

Harry almost smiled.

"Remus stays in the room."

"He may observe. Either of you may end the exercise. We will discuss any interruption after the spell has stopped."

"Sirius wants—"

"I have received Mr Black's six-page list of demands."

"Only six?"

"The seventh page concerned my cauldron."

Harry looked down. It did not help. The laugh escaped. Snape's mouth tightened.

"Friday. Eight o'clock. Professor Lupin will escort you. Flitwick must first test the barrier we discussed; there will be no Legilimency until he has done so. We will also use a monitoring instrument. Neither instrument nor barrier should be mistaken for a guarantee."

"And if I can't say stop?"

"If you freeze or cannot answer, Lupin ends the exercise. Any deliberate test will remain within the memory you have agreed to use. Unrelated memories are not material for my amusement, Potter."

"All right."

Harry reached the door.

"Potter."

He turned. Snape looked towards the place where Draconia had sat.

"What occurred during stage two?"

Harry considered how much belonged to him.

"The light reminded her of something."

"The Chamber."

It was not a question.

"Probably."

"Did she lose awareness of the room?"

"For a few seconds. She came back."

Snape's gaze lowered to Harry's hand.

"You anchored her."

"Hermione did too."

"I did not ask who deserves credit."

Harry waited. Snape spoke with visible reluctance.

"The method was appropriate."

Praise from Snape arrived disguised as a medical report. Harry accepted it in the same form.

"I'll record the successful outcome."

"Leave."

Harry opened the door. Hermione and Draconia stood exactly where they had promised.

"Well?" Hermione asked.

"Friday, if Flitwick has tested the barrier. Remus stays, and he can stop it if I can't. Snape agreed to the limits." Harry let out a breath. "Sirius also appears to have threatened a cauldron."

Draconia looked towards Snape's closed door.

"Which cauldron?"

"All of them, possibly. The letter was long."

They began walking towards Defence Against the Dark Arts. The Ministry had reached the classroom before them. Pink notices covered the corridor walls. They had been placed over old defensive diagrams, club announcements and a faded list of duelling champions. Each sheet bore Commissioner Directive One and a second notice issued that morning.

APPROVED DEFENSIVE EDUCATION

Practical spellcasting may occur only where necessary to a documented curriculum outcome and after appropriate emotional-risk assessment.

Students must not imitate, rehearse or sensationalise recent violent activity.

Someone had drawn a small broom beneath the final sentence. It flew towards the edge of the page whenever a Ministry seal looked away. Fred and George, Harry assumed. Hermione read every notice. Draconia read the gaps between them.

"There used to be a fire-response diagram here," she said.

Harry looked at the pink parchment covering it.

"Improved safety."

"Obviously."

The Defence classroom no longer contained training dummies. The duelling platform had been removed. The cabinets of dark artefacts stood empty behind locked glass. Every desk faced directly forwards in six exact rows, each one holding a copy of Defensive Magical Theory. Fresh curtains covered the windows despite the grey daylight outside. Upon the blackboard, round white letters announced:

SAFETY THROUGH UNDERSTANDING

Harry chose a desk in the third row. Draconia took the chair beside him, lowering herself carefully onto its hard wooden seat. Hermione paused by the narrow gap, then sat across the aisle rather than squeeze between them.

"How are we supposed to practise shields in here?" Harry whispered.

Hermione glanced at the bolted desks. "Perhaps that is the point."

Umbridge entered before he could reply.

"Wands away, please."

She spoke gently, with a patience that seemed practised rather than felt. Harry remembered the same voice asking him to describe torture at the hearing. Students placed their wands inside bags, sleeves and robes. Harry returned his to the forearm holster. The movement remained visible. He wanted it to. Umbridge stopped behind her desk.

Three ornamental plates hung upon the wall above it. Painted kittens chased balls of pink wool around their borders. One became distracted by the class and pressed both paws against the porcelain to stare.

"Good morning."

Several students answered.

"Good morning," Umbridge repeated.

The rest joined reluctantly.

"That is much better. Order begins with shared expectations. You have experienced an unsettling start to the term. Some of you may be feeling frightened, angry or unusually interested in dangerous magic. These responses are understandable. They are not educationally useful."

Harry opened the textbook. The first chapter was entitled The Value of Non-Provocation. The second asked whether defensive spellcasting could increase hostility by implying distrust.

"For several years," Umbridge continued, "your Defence education has lacked continuity. Temporary instructors introduced advanced and, in certain cases, wholly inappropriate material before departing under regrettable circumstances."

Quirrell had tried to steal the Stone with Voldemort attached to his head. Lockhart had assigned his own books and removed the bones from Harry's arm while attempting to repair it. Remus had taught Harry more than every other instructor combined before resigning because parents learnt he was a werewolf. Crouch Junior had worn Moody's face and trained Harry while arranging his death. Regrettable covered a great deal.

"This year, we will restore a calm and structured approach. You will study defensive principles, conflict avoidance and the responsible role of qualified authorities. No practical magic will be required until the Ministry determines that theoretical competence and emotional readiness have been established."

Hermione's hand rose. Umbridge looked at it.

"Yes, Miss...?"

"Granger."

"Of course."

She knew. Harry could hear it in the way she said the name.

"How will theoretical competence be assessed without demonstrating the spells?" Hermione asked.

"Written examination."

"The Defence OWL contains a practical component."

"Assessment standards are under review."

"They are at the end of this year."

"Then you have ample time to develop the patience expected of advanced students."

Hermione lowered her hand. Her expression promised the patience would not benefit Umbridge. The commissioner opened her own copy of Slinkhard.

"Chapter one. You will read silently and produce an eighteen-inch summary of the principles by the end of the lesson."

Pages turned. Harry read the first paragraph.

Conflict often begins not with hostile intent, but with the perception of hostility. A witch or wizard who reaches for a wand communicates aggression before any spell is cast. Safety therefore depends upon maintaining a non-threatening posture while trained authorities assess—

He raised his hand. Draconia closed her eyes.

Hermione whispered, "At least make her choose you."

Harry kept his hand raised. Umbridge continued reading until several students began watching his raised hand instead of their books.

"Yes, Mr Potter?"

"What do we do if the trained authority isn't there?"

"You remove yourself from danger and seek assistance."

"What if the doors are sealed?"

Several students looked up.

Umbridge's smile remained. "The book discusses general principles."

"What if the windows are broken and the train is moving?"

"You are referring to exceptional circumstances."

"They felt fairly specific."

A laugh came from the back row. Umbridge looked towards it. The sound ended.

"Recent events have understandably affected your perception of ordinary risk. That does not mean every student requires advanced spellcasting because you experienced a targeted attack."

Targeted. Harry heard Seamus asking whether there was a difference to everyone else. He took another half-second.

"Every student was on the train," he said.

"Every student was not the target."

The sentence struck the room. No one moved. Umbridge had not accused Harry directly. She had merely separated him from the people beside him and allowed responsibility to enter the gap. Draconia's hand closed around the edge of the desk. Hermione sat straighter. Harry kept his voice level.

"The Killing Curse missed me and struck the carriage behind me. There were children back there. It didn't ask who the target was."

Umbridge's smile thinned.

"Which illustrates why students should not engage aggressors."

Ron raised his hand. She did not call upon him. He spoke anyway.

"There were children all through the train. It doesn't matter who they wanted if everyone else is in the way."

"Mr Weasley, you will wait to be recognised."

"How long?"

"Until I recognise you."

"Useful system."

Harry looked over his shoulder. Ron lifted one eyebrow. Something warm moved through Harry's chest despite the room. Umbridge tapped the textbook.

"The Ministry response prevented loss of life."

"The escort fought from the beginning," Draconia said. "We helped because they couldn't be in every carriage. The reinforcements came at the bridge."

Umbridge turned to her.

"Miss Malfoy, your recovery may be impaired by unnecessary agitation."

"My recovery would prefer a chair with a cushion. It has no objection to an accurate account."

Laughter broke across the Slytherin side. Umbridge's gaze dropped to the prefect badge.

"You are expected to model appropriate conduct."

"I am answering an inaccurate claim."

"You are interrupting instruction."

"There has been no instruction."

Harry looked at her. Draconia's face remained perfectly composed. He loved her a little for the sentence.

The realisation arrived without drama. Not began to love. Not might one day. He had told her he loved her beside the pond at Hawthorn, and meant it. He had meant it again when he held her wrist above the moving track. Here, with her refusing to let Umbridge rewrite what had happened, he felt the ordinary wish to lean closer and make her smile when she looked at him. He was beginning to understand how much of that love was friendship and how much asked for something he had not yet learnt to name aloud. Harry turned towards the textbook before his face revealed it.

Hermione saw anyway. Her eyes moved from him to Draconia and back. Something flickered across her expression. Hurt, perhaps. Fondness too. Neither cancelled the other. Then Umbridge spoke.

"Perhaps your father's recent choices have confused you about which forms of defiance deserve admiration."

Draconia went white. Harry stood. His chair struck the fixed desk.

"Leave him out of it."

Umbridge looked up at Harry as though surprised he remained in the room.

"Sit down."

"You brought up her father."

"I reminded a prefect that public conduct carries responsibility."

"What has her family got to do with learning Defence?"

"Harry," Hermione whispered.

Not warning him to stop. Reminding him to choose. The corridor upon the train appeared. Floating glass. A man raising empty hands. The voice inside Harry's anger.

Show them.

Harry placed both palms upon the desk. He felt wood. The silver ward around its legs. Draconia's sleeve near his wrist. Hermione watching him from across the aisle. Classroom. Monday morning. No one was dying.

"Sit," Umbridge repeated.

Harry did. Disappointment moved through her eyes. She had wanted the outburst. He understood too late and still in time.

"Five points from Gryffindor for leaving your seat," she said.

"There it is," Harry replied.

"Excuse me?"

"The practical component."

Laughter erupted. This time it crossed both Houses. Umbridge waited. Harry looked at the open textbook and did not smile. Her voice became very soft.

"Detention. Eight o'clock tonight. My office."

"For standing?"

"For persistent disruption, disrespect and the deliberate provocation of fear among vulnerable students."

"Which part frightened them?"

"Your insistence upon discussing dark magic despite clear instruction."

"This is Defence Against the Dark Arts."

"Mr Potter."

"Just checking. The room changed."

Draconia's hand found the inside of his wrist. One squeeze. Enough. Harry became silent. Umbridge faced the blackboard.

"Twenty-four inches," she said. "The additional six will allow everyone to reflect upon the educational cost of interruption."

Quills moved reluctantly. Harry picked up his. His heart beat too hard. Sweat cooled beneath the bandage on his shoulder. He had remained seated. Kept his wand hidden. Used words instead of force.

He had received punishment anyway. For a moment he wanted to ask what keeping control had achieved. Then he looked at Draconia's hand beside his and remembered the glass scattering through the train. He had kept his wand down. He had not frightened her. Those things mattered even if Umbridge refused to notice them. Harry wrote the title of the essay. Beside him, Draconia wrote only one line.

Authority without competence is not safety.

She tore away the strip before Umbridge could see and slid it beneath Harry's hand. He read it. The anger remained. It no longer belonged only to him.

Theodore Nott waited until the essays were collected. He approached Umbridge's desk while everyone else packed. Harry watched from the doorway.

"Commissioner," Theodore said. "You told me to report any discussion of the attack involving unverified family allegations."

Umbridge looked pleased. "Yes?"

"You alleged that Mr Malfoy's choices influenced his daughter's conduct. Should I record that now or during our welfare appointment?"

The classroom stopped emptying. Umbridge's expression did not move.

"That is not what I said."

"Good," Theodore replied. "I wanted the record to be accurate."

He left the room. Draconia stared after him. Harry did too. Theodore walked alone towards the staircase, altered cuffs visible beneath his robes and his father's name following him even when nobody spoke it.

"What was that?" Harry asked.

Draconia folded the pink notice Theodore had given her and placed it inside Harry's hand.

"He warned me she was asking questions," Draconia said. "I didn't expect him to make quite so public a point about it."

Harry read the compulsory appointment.

"She wants an informant."

"She wants him to think helping her is the only way to prove he isn't his father. He showed me the notice this morning."

Hermione joined them. "Did he warn you?"

"This morning."

"Do you trust him?" Harry asked.

Draconia looked towards the empty corridor.

"I trust the warning."

It was not the same thing. For now, it was enough. They did not get as far as the Great Hall without stopping. On the second staircase, Draconia pressed her palm against the banister and waited while a group of first-years squeezed past. Harry stepped onto the landing with her. Hermione stood on the other side, no longer pretending that the bag upon her shoulder weighed nothing.

"Five minutes," Harry said.

Draconia looked towards the next flight. "We are nearly there."

"Then it'll still be nearly there in five minutes."

An unused classroom stood beside the landing. Hermione tried the handle, found it unlocked, and held the door. Nobody called the stop a medical conference. Draconia sat on the edge of the nearest desk while Harry eased his arm back into its sling. They could hear the crowd going down to lunch beyond the wall; inside, dust floated through a narrow stripe of daylight. Hermione put her bag down. The sound was louder than she seemed to expect.

"I hate that book," she said.

"The pink one?" Harry asked.

"Slinkhard. It keeps telling us to ask someone qualified, but it never says what to do if nobody answers."

Draconia worked a finger under the tight edge of her cuff. "Apparently we wait politely."

"I used to want to be good at everything they taught us," Hermione said. "Now I have to work out whether the things they teach us are any use at all."

Harry had no joke ready for that. He remembered her hand in their first-year classroom, always up before anyone else's. It had annoyed him sometimes. He missed how certain she had looked when a teacher gave her an answer.

"You're still good at things," he said.

"I know. That isn't—" She stopped, then rubbed her forehead. "I'm not explaining it properly."

"You don't have to explain it properly every time."

Draconia glanced at him. "A generous policy. We should extend it to your Potions essays."

Hermione laughed despite herself. The laugh left her looking tired rather than fixed, but she sat down instead of reaching for her notebook. Harry took the desk beside hers. For a few minutes they watched a spider repair its web in the window recess and listened to the castle carry on without them. When Draconia stood again, she did it more easily. Hermione let Harry take the lightest of her books, and he let her tell him it was the lightest. They went down the remaining stairs together. At lunch, Hermione surrendered the witness index. It required Padma Patil, Ernie Macmillan and two boxes to carry.

"The blue tabs are times we've checked against a second account," Hermione explained. "They still aren't all exact. Yellow means we need to ask something. Red marks contradictions, but please don't assume anyone's lying. The train was loud, people were in different carriages, and some of them didn't hear the first whistle—"

Padma tapped the lid of the upper box. "We have the key. Ernie is carrying these. Pomfrey said my ankle could handle stairs, not a library."

"And the spell-copy register?"

"In my bag."

"The identities remain separate from the compiled timeline."

"Hermione."

"Yes?"

"Give me the box."

Hermione held it for another second. Then released. Her hands remained shaped around the missing weight.

Ernie adjusted his grip. "We will bring it back before dinner."

"After dinner," Harry said.

Hermione looked at him. Draconia returned from collecting her next dose of pain potion and heard only the final sentence.

"Tomorrow morning," she said.

"This has become hostile," Hermione complained.

Padma smiled. "Tomorrow."

They carried the boxes away. Hermione watched until the doors closed. Harry placed a plate in front of her.

"No reading."

"I remember."

"You look bereaved."

"I gave Padma my indexing charm."

"It will come back."

"She uses commas inconsistently."

Draconia sat beside Harry. "Grief takes many forms."

Hermione began eating with tragic dignity. The trio occupied the end of the Gryffindor table because cross-House seating remained technically discouraged and nobody had been foolish enough to enforce it since the train. The empty space around them did not remain empty for long. Neville arrived first.

"Could you show me the shield you used in carriage seven?"

Harry lowered his fork.

"Which one?"

"The wide one. It covered the corridor."

"Protego Maxima."

"We haven't learnt it."

"Most fifth-years don't."

"You do."

Neville said it without accusation or awe. A fact requiring explanation. Susan Bones and Anthony Goldstein appeared behind him. Then a third-year Slytherin whose name Harry did not know. Each had a question. How had Hermione sent the Patronus through moving carriages? What spell transformed the metal panel beneath the collapsed roof? How had Draconia broken the hostile ward connection on the carriage roof? Why had the compartment shields broken when the outer wards reversed? The questions gathered faster than answers. Cedric reached the table carrying his lunch and sat upon the bench opposite.

"They've been asking me since breakfast."

Harry looked at him. "What did you tell them?"

"That I can teach emergency broom handling and shield formation. I can't explain what you did in the corridor."

The glass rising from the floor returned to Harry's mind.

"Neither can I."

Neville shifted. "Not that part. The shields before it."

"Professor Umbridge says practical magic encourages escalation," the Slytherin boy said.

Draconia looked at him. "Do you believe her?"

He stared at the table. "My sister was in carriage nine."

That answered enough. Hermione stopped mourning the index. She looked past the people in front of her and set her fork down. Harry recognised the pause. He had seen it before lists, timetables and plans which somehow became his responsibility before anyone asked him.

"No," Harry said.

She blinked. "I haven't spoken."

"You've stopped eating. That usually means we're about to get a timetable."

Draconia examined Hermione. "He has a point."

"We are not forming anything," Hermione said.

"Good."

"People are asking us for help. We could at least think about it."

"That is the same thing wearing a title."

Cedric tore a piece of bread.

"Nobody has to decide today."

Harry looked at the students around the table. Neville had spent Friday helping younger students along the crowded train. Susan's aunt worked inside a Ministry which now inspected her letters. Anthony's glasses remained cracked at one corner. The unnamed Slytherin's sister had escaped carriage nine just before it left the rails. They were not asking Harry to be unbeatable. They were asking him to explain what had kept people alive. That felt worse.

"I nearly lost control," Harry said.

The Slytherin boy looked confused. Neville did not.

"But you know that," he said.

"Knowing afterwards doesn't make me a teacher."

"Lupin knew when things frightened him."

The name landed softly. Harry looked towards the staff table. Umbridge's pink chair stood where the Defence professor should have been. Remus was at Hawthorn. He had resigned after parents learnt he was a werewolf, and Harry had never seen the school offer him a way back. Now the teacher meant to replace Crouch had been prevented from returning too. It seemed possible to remove every useful person from a classroom without once admitting that usefulness was the problem.

"Not today," Harry said.

Hermione did not argue.

"Not today," she agreed.

The students accepted the answer and left with more patience than Harry felt he deserved. Cedric remained.

"That wasn't no."

"It wasn't yes."

"I know."

"You sound like Dumbledore."

"Cruel."

Harry smiled faintly. Cedric stood, then hesitated with one hand on the table.

"Harry."

"Understood."

Rain had stopped over the Quidditch pitch. Clouds moved low above the stands, torn open occasionally by strips of pale blue. The grass shone with water. Every step released the smell of mud and autumn. Harry stood beside the goal hoops with his left arm back in its sling and his right hand tucked into his jacket pocket. He could feel the cold through the grass whenever he stayed still too long, but walking a circuit of the hoops helped more than sitting in the common room.

His Firebolt remained locked inside the school broom shed under a temporary medical charm. Madam Pomfrey had attached the charm to the handle herself. Fred had attempted to remove it and triggered a lecture charm. Pomfrey's voice had followed him around the changing room for ten minutes, describing healthy bone formation while George tried to ask it questions. Ron hovered twenty feet above the pitch upon an elderly Cleansweep. Angelina Johnson threw the Quaffle. Ron moved too soon. The ball passed beneath his left arm.

"Again," Angelina called.

Fred retrieved it. George circled the opposite hoop with one Beater's bat across his shoulders. Ginny waited near the centre line for Chaser trials on a borrowed school broom. Her own had broken during the escape; she had informed Harry that she intended to complain about the replacement until someone provided one which could turn left without first considering it. Three other hopefuls argued about formation beside her. Ron returned to the middle hoop. His ears were red. Angelina threw right. Ron followed her shoulder instead of the ball. He reached the correct side but overshot and caught the hoop with one elbow.

Ron looked down. "Helpful."

"Watch her hand."

"I was."

"No, you watched where she wanted you to look."

Angelina glanced at Harry. "Show him."

Harry looked at the broom shed.

"Pomfrey's grounded me. If she sees me in the air, you can explain why."

Fred grinned. "We have rope."

"No."

"Harness?"

"Still no."

"Very little commitment to the sport."

Harry walked beneath the hoops.

"Throw from the same position twice," he told Angelina. "Different targets."

Ron reset. Angelina's shoulder turned left. Her wrist remained loose until release. The Quaffle travelled right. Ron watched the hand. He caught it against his chest.

"Again," Harry said.

Angelina repeated the movement. This time the shoulder and wrist agreed. Ron hesitated, then committed left and blocked with both hands. The impact pushed him backwards. He kept the ball.

"Better," Angelina said.

Ron looked towards Harry. Pride and surprise fought across his face.

"Again."

Harry stayed beneath the hoops while Angelina organised the next drill. His shoulder had begun to ache, so he leaned his right elbow upon the low spectator rail and took some of the weight off his feet. The damp wood left a dark mark upon his sleeve. Above him, Ron kept glancing down after each catch, as though checking that the advice still belonged to him.

"Don't look at me," Harry called. "There isn't a ball down here."

Fred obligingly dropped the Quaffle at his feet.

"Now there is."

Harry picked it up with his right hand, slowly enough not to pull his ribs, and threw it back underarm. The throw went several feet short. George caught it and bowed as if accepting a magnificent gift.

"The star Seeker's terrifying recovery continues."

"Medical restrictions," Fred said solemnly. "Can't risk it."

Harry was grinning when Angelina told them both to be useful somewhere else. For several minutes he forgot to check who was approaching from the path. Ron improved when the others stopped offering advice simultaneously. He possessed fast hands and recovered quickly when he chose the wrong direction, dragging the broom back into the path of a shot rather than watching it pass. Years of avoiding objects thrown by six siblings had produced useful instincts. His confidence arrived unevenly. A perfect save made him reckless. A missed one made him expect the next failure early. "Stop apologising to the Quaffle," he called.

"I wasn't."

"You look sorry before she even throws it."

Ron hovered lower. "You have a lot of opinions for someone not flying."

"Convenient."

Harry looked at the locked shed.

"Painful."

Ron landed beside him while Angelina organised Chaser drills. Mud splashed both their shoes.

"You caught them."

"Susan told me about it. And I saw enough of your spells in eight. I was behind you for half of those—best view, unfortunately."

Ron rubbed mud from the broom handle with his cuff. "Tell them when they shouldn't."

"It doesn't always work."

"No. You've met Fred."

Sirius had told Harry that he was keeping a record of the connection in case someone disputed the detention afterwards. The office smelled of roses and burned sugar. Every surface had been softened. Pink fabric covered the chairs. Lace hung from the shelves. Bowls of dried flowers occupied the corners. Lucius had made him memorise the sentence. Umbridge recognised it.

Harry's heartbeat changed. She knew it was open. Perhaps the office wards detected communication magic. Perhaps Theodore had told more than he claimed. Perhaps Umbridge guessed because Harry had made no attempt to hide the shape beneath his jacket. He took the half-second.

"You are fifteen."

"He remains my guardian."

"Very well."

She opened a drawer and removed a long black quill. No ink bottle accompanied it. Harry looked at the sharpened point. The feather did not shine. "Black quill. No ink bottle."

Something inside his jacket warmed. Sirius had heard.

"I must not frighten others with unverified claims."

Harry picked up the quill. It was warm. Not body warm. Fever warm.

The surface had no grain beneath his fingers, though it appeared feathered. A dark magical object inside an office devoted to safe theory. Ordinary objects could carry very unordinary magic. Draconia's diary had taught all three of them that, though remembering it did not tell him what this quill did.

"Where is the ink?"

"It is a self-inking quill."

"Hermione's quill has a reservoir."

I.

The quill produced red ink. A sting crossed the back of his right hand, the one holding the quill. He stopped. The skin appeared unmarked. Umbridge waited. Harry wrote the second word.

must.

The sting deepened. Thin heat followed the motion of the quill, not across the parchment but beneath his skin. Each letter traced itself upon nerves rather than flesh. Harry lifted the feather. The red ink faded from the page.

"Continue," Umbridge said.

He looked at his hand. No mark. Not yet.

Dumbledore had allowed Harry to inspect the Portkey. Snape had just promised to explain each stage of Friday's lesson before attempting it. "What is it?"

"A corrective quill."

"The necessary medium."

"Which is?"

Umbridge folded her hands.

"You have been encouraged to believe that every adult owes you negotiation. A consequence loses value when the student controls its terms."

"So you won't tell me."

"I have told you enough."

He already had the sign. The urge to prove he could endure it rose immediately. One sentence. He had endured the Cruciatus Curse. A quill could not frighten him. Leaving after two words would allow Umbridge to tell the school Harry Potter required rescue from handwriting. Remus's voice returned.

You are not proving independence by staying where an adult hurts you.

"Sit down."

I must.

"Blood quill," he said.

"No."

"The mirror."

"Harry, what happens if she takes it?"
