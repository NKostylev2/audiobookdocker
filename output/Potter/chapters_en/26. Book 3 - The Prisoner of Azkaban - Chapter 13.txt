13

By the time the portrait hole slammed shut behind him, Sirius Black was already falling apart.

He reached the end of the corridor before the first shout rose from Gryffindor Tower. Pain burned across his ribs where Harry had driven them both into the bedpost. One sleeve hung torn from his shoulder, and blood from his split lip had reached his chin. He could hear the alarm spreading behind him: startled voices, doors opening, a prefect demanding that everyone remain calm in precisely the tone guaranteed to ensure they did not.

Sirius ran harder. The castle had changed since his school days. Corridors bent where he remembered them running straight, suits of armour occupied different alcoves, and several staircases had developed opinions about where he ought to be going. Yet the older bones of Hogwarts remained familiar. He knew the passages concealed behind fraying tapestries, the steps that complained beneath a careless foot and the turns where torchlight failed to reach. His body remembered even when his mind did not. A shout sounded on the floor below.

Sirius reached the concealed passage behind the statue of Gregory the Smarmy, forced himself through the narrow opening and dragged the stone panel closed. Darkness swallowed him. The air tasted of dust and old mortar, and the passage was so cramped that his shoulders scraped both walls. He made it six paces before his legs gave way. Sirius caught himself against the stone and bit down on the sound that tried to escape him. His lungs fought for breath. Every rib seemed to carry the memory of Harry's weight striking him. Not James's.

Harry's.

The difference should not have required reminding, yet Sirius had spent twelve years preserving a child in his head: a bundle of blankets, untidy black hair and small fists grabbing greedily for his fingers. The boy in Gryffindor Tower had been nearly thirteen, thin enough to make Sirius want to tear the castle apart brick by brick, with Lily's eyes and James's recklessness sharpened by a life neither of them had been there to witness.

Harry had stood between Sirius and Peter. He had not known who Peter was. He had not understood why a skeletal stranger had entered the dormitory with a knife. He had still stepped between them. Sirius pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until colours burst behind his lids.

"Brilliant," he whispered into the darkness. "Very reassuring entrance. Couldn't have gone better."

A faint scratching answered him. Sirius lowered his hands. Two yellow eyes watched from farther along the passage. The cat emerged slowly, his flattened face arranged into its usual expression of grave disapproval. Crookshanks carried something pale between his teeth. He crossed the dust without sound, dropped the object beside Sirius's knee and sat back upon his haunches. It was a handkerchief.

Sirius stared at it. Crookshanks stared back.

"Is that for the blood?"

The cat blinked.

"You're very thoughtful."

Crookshanks lifted one hind leg and began washing himself. Sirius pressed the handkerchief against the cut beneath his ribs. It smelled faintly of lavender and had the initials P.P. embroidered into one corner.

"Please tell me you stole this from Parkinson."

The washing continued.

"I'm keeping it either way."

Footsteps passed beyond the hidden wall. Sirius held himself still while torchlight entered through a crack in the stone. Someone called for Professor McGonagall. Another voice answered from farther away, and the corridor filled with the metallic clatter of armour being instructed to search. Crookshanks paused with one paw suspended beside his ear.

"We can't stay here," Sirius murmured.

The cat abandoned his bath, turned and walked deeper into the passage. Sirius shifted onto his hands and knees. His body protested every movement, but Azkaban had taught him the difference between pain and impossibility. Pain was merely a fact. Impossibility was what other people called a locked door before he found the weakness in it. He crawled. The passage descended between walls too narrow for an adult man, ending behind a broken cupboard on the ground floor. Crookshanks squeezed through first. Sirius listened, heard no one nearby and forced his way after him. The transformation hurt.

It always hurt now. Once, changing had been effortless—a breath, a thought, the world rearranging itself around him. Twelve years without practice had made the magic stubborn. Bones shortened with a series of blunt internal jolts; his spine bowed; hands struck the floor and became paws. Black fur spread over scars and protruding ribs until the gaunt prisoner disappeared and a vast, starving dog stood in his place. The curse wound remained, but the bleeding slowed. Crookshanks inspected him, apparently found the result acceptable and trotted away.

They crossed the entrance hall beneath the shadows of the moving staircases. Sirius could smell students gathering behind classroom doors, the sour agitation of Filch and the colder trace of the castle's protective enchantments awakening. Outside, wind pressed rain against the oak doors. The grounds beyond would be watched within minutes. Crookshanks vanished behind a suit of armour. Sirius followed and found a gap between the wall and the base of the statue. It opened into a service corridor descending towards the kitchens. Warmth breathed through the stones. The scents of bread, roasted meat and treacle struck him so violently that his stomach cramped.

He stopped. Crookshanks glanced back. Sirius forced his paws forward. Food could wait. It had waited before. They slipped past the kitchens, crossed a storage chamber and emerged through a low archway beside the greenhouses. Rain flattened Sirius's fur immediately. Somewhere above them, a bell began to ring.

The grounds brightened as windows flared to life across the castle. Sirius ran. The wet earth gave beneath his paws. He crossed the lawn in long, uneven bounds, keeping below the slope where the castle windows could not see him. Crookshanks travelled beside him with surprising speed, little more than a ginger streak against the grass. They passed Hagrid's hut, where a lamp already burned, and reached the first line of trees. The forest accepted them without question.

Under the canopy, the rain weakened to a constant patter. Roots twisted across the path, leaves brushed Sirius's back, and the scents of mud, bark and living animals crowded his mind until the castle receded behind him. Only then did he allow himself to think of the rat. Peter had been there. Sirius had smelled him before he saw him: old straw, sweat and the faint metallic tang of human fear trapped beneath an animal's skin. The scent had floated from Ron Weasley's curtains and struck Sirius with such force that the room vanished around him. Twelve years of waiting collapsed into that single breath.

He had lunged. Peter had changed. Not fully. Not enough for the children to understand. A flash of a narrow human wrist beneath brown fur, fingers clawing at the mattress, then a rat again—twisting, biting, vanishing through a rip in the bed curtains before Sirius could close his hand around him. The knife had cut cloth, nothing more. Then Harry had attacked.

Sirius should have spoken. He should have dropped the blade, raised both hands and said the boy's name. He had rehearsed versions of that meeting through countless nights, some angry, some hopeful, some so absurdly tender he despised himself for imagining them. None of them had begun with Harry throwing him into a bedpost. None had contained Hermione Granger standing in the doorway with her wand levelled at his heart or Ron Weasley kicking him while trying to shield the man disguised as his pet. James would have laughed for a week. Lily would have said he deserved worse.

The thought hurt more than Harry's spell. Sirius followed Crookshanks through the trees until they reached a shallow ravine. A cleft opened beneath the roots of an ancient beech, concealed by brambles and a curtain of wet ivy. He slipped inside.

The space beyond was scarcely large enough to qualify as a cave. Its ceiling bowed low enough that Sirius could touch it with his back, and rainwater gathered in small hollows along the floor. A heap of stolen blankets occupied the driest corner. Beside them lay a dented tin cup, several copies of the Daily Prophet and a paper parcel containing the remains of a loaf.

Home.

Crookshanks entered after him and shook rain from his whiskers. Sirius changed back only when he was certain they had not been followed. The return to human form left him curled on the stone, teeth clenched against the pain. Cold fastened itself to his wet clothes. His hands shook when he attempted to untie the paper around the bread. Crookshanks made an impatient noise.

"Yes, I know. Dignity itself."

The loaf had hardened at the edges. Sirius tore it in half, gave the softer portion to Crookshanks and chewed the rest without tasting it. His stomach objected to being remembered. He stopped after several bites and wrapped what remained. The cat ate neatly, then climbed onto the pile of blankets.

"You let him escape."

Crookshanks's ears flattened.

"I'm not blaming you. I just want to know what happened."

The yellow eyes narrowed.

"He fooled James too."

The cat turned his back. Sirius almost smiled. Rain whispered through the brambles. In the distance, Hagrid's boarhound began barking. Search parties would reach the forest soon. Dementors might be stationed along its edge by dawn. At the thought of them, the cave seemed to lose several degrees of warmth. Sirius dragged a blanket around his shoulders and forced his breathing to remain steady. He was not in Azkaban. Stone pressed behind him, but there was earth beneath it and rain outside. Crookshanks's tail moved against his ankle. He could smell wet leaves. He could think.

That was the important difference. In Azkaban, happiness did not fade at once. The Dementors drew it out gradually, returning every few hours to scrape away whatever the last visit had missed. Time became measured by the memories one lost. James laughing on the back of the motorbike had gone first. Then Lily dancing barefoot in the Potters' kitchen because the wireless played a song she liked. Harry asleep against Sirius's chest, warm and impossibly small.

Eventually even their faces became difficult to summon. Sirius had known he loved them because grief remained where the memories had been, but he could not always remember why. One fact survived. He had not betrayed them. It was not a happy thought. There was no warmth in innocence, no comfort or hope for the Dementors to devour. It was cold, hard and useless. It belonged entirely to him.

He had not betrayed James and Lily. Peter had. That truth had kept Sirius human long after almost everything else was stripped away. When even humanity became unbearable, he discovered he could retreat from it. Dementors sensed emotion, not thought; the mind of a dog was simpler, its grief less shaped by language. The cold still reached him, but it could not prise apart what it did not fully recognise. For years, that knowledge had changed nothing. Then Cornelius Fudge visited Azkaban.

The Minister brought newspapers under one arm and the scent of warm buildings upon his robes. Sirius watched from the floor of his cell while Fudge complained to the governor about prison funding and coastal weather. One copy of the Daily Prophet slid beneath the bars when he left. Sirius had intended to use it for warmth. Instead he saw Peter. The photograph showed a red-haired family in Egypt. Nine smiling people stood before a pyramid, sun-browned and waving. The youngest boy carried a rat upon his shoulder. Sirius recognised him immediately.

Time could alter weight, fur and the small indignities of age. It could not restore a missing toe. The rat's front paw lacked one digit. Beneath the photograph, the article announced that the Weasleys' youngest son would return to Hogwarts in September. The boy was the correct age. The school was protected.

Harry was there. Peter had not attached himself to a wizarding family by accident. He had hidden among children because adults overlooked pets, and he had remained close to the place where Harry Potter would eventually be sent. Perhaps he intended to deliver Harry to his master if Voldemort returned. Perhaps he merely wanted a shield no sane person would attack. Sirius had spent the night staring at the photograph while the Dementors glided beyond his door. They could not take what the image gave him, because what Sirius felt was not hope. It was purpose.

He began refusing food. The guards saw a prisoner growing weaker; they did not understand that his dog form slipped more easily between the bars with each pound lost. On a night when the sea battered the cliffs and lightning interfered with the Ministry wards, he transformed, squeezed through iron and crossed the outer passages under the Dementors' senses. The water nearly killed him. He welcomed it.

The sea was violent, alive and cold in a way Azkaban never managed. Waves dragged him beneath the surface and threw him against rocks. He swam until the prison became a darker shape against the night, then until his legs no longer belonged to him, then farther because Harry remained at the end of every mile.

Weeks followed in fragments: roads travelled after dark, bins searched behind inns, livestock fields crossed beneath hedges. He stole the occasional newspaper and tracked Hogwarts by rumours. He saw Harry once in Little Whinging, standing beneath a streetlamp with his trunk beside him, rage and fear pouring from him in equal measure. Sirius had wanted to approach. Then Harry looked directly at the black dog, stumbled backwards and raised his wand. Sirius fled before he could become another danger in the boy's eyes. He had been fleeing ever since.

The cave blurred. Sirius pressed his palms to his face. Exhaustion made the past dangerously vivid. He could see another night, another ruined house. Snow had not yet begun, but the October air smelled sharp enough to promise it. Godric's Hollow had been silent. That silence returned in dreams more often than the screaming. He had landed the motorbike in the lane and known before his boots touched the ground. The Fidelius Charm had broken. The cottage stood visible where empty space should have been, its upper floor torn open beneath the sky.

Sirius entered through what remained of the front door. James lay in the hall. No wand. No final duel. James, who had once attempted to challenge a thunderstorm for frightening Harry, had died reaching for his wand while Voldemort climbed the stairs.

Sirius knelt beside him until a cry sounded above.

Harry.

Hagrid arrived minutes later on Dumbledore's orders. Sirius remembered arguing with him among the wreckage, remembered trying to take Harry and hearing himself say that he was the boy's godfather. Hagrid refused. Dumbledore had instructed him to bring Harry to safety. Dumbledore's word carried more weight than Sirius's grief, and somewhere beneath the shock Sirius knew he could not care for a child while hunting a murderer. He gave Hagrid the motorbike. Then he found Peter.

A Muggle street. Morning traffic. Twelve people passing between shops. Peter standing in the open, weeping before Sirius even raised his wand.

"How could you?" Peter shouted.

The performance was perfect. Loud enough for witnesses. Broken enough to resemble betrayal.

"How could you betray Lily and James?"

Sirius saw Peter's wand move. The street exploded. Heat swallowed the pavement. Windows burst. Bodies vanished into smoke and blood. When Sirius dragged himself upright, there was a crater where Peter had stood and a severed finger resting near the drain. Peter was gone. People screamed.

Ministry wizards arrived. Sirius began to laugh. He laughed because Peter, timid little Peter, had beaten them all. James had trusted Sirius to protect his family, and Sirius's brilliant plan had delivered them to Voldemort. He had persuaded James to change Secret Keepers. He had argued that Voldemort would hunt Sirius first, that nobody would suspect Peter. He had called it clever. He laughed while Aurors took his wand.

He laughed while they bound his hands. He continued laughing because if he stopped, he would have to understand that Harry had no parents and Sirius had given away the right to protect him. No trial followed. No one asked. The world already knew its story: Sirius Black, heir to a Dark family, Voldemort's spy, murderer of thirteen. Barty Crouch sent him to Azkaban before the bodies were cold.

Sirius lowered his hands. Crookshanks had turned around again. The cat watched him across the blankets, crumbs clinging to his whiskers.

"I thought I could fix it," Sirius said. "That was the clever plan. Get out. Kill Peter. Possibly introduce myself to Harry afterwards, if he was feeling charitable."

Crookshanks placed one paw upon Sirius's knee.

"He looks like James."

The claws pricked through the fabric.

"But he isn't."

Sirius closed his hand gently around the cat's paw.

"He's quieter. Watches people before deciding what to give them. That's Lily, I think. James never waited long enough to decide anything."

Crookshanks extracted his paw.

"And he has friends."

That mattered. Hermione had stood beside Harry without hesitation. Ron Weasley had fought to protect what he believed was an ordinary pet. Draconia Malfoy had not been in the tower, but Sirius had watched her beside Harry often enough to recognise another guard dog when he saw one. Harry was surrounded, and beneath envy sharp enough to shame him, Sirius felt relief. James and Lily had died to keep their son alive.

Harry had continued the work.

In Professor Lupin's office, Harry waited for the world to rearrange itself. It did so with insulting reluctance.

The Marauder's Map remained spread across the desk. Hundreds of tiny inked names moved through miniature corridors. Students returned to their dormitories beneath the watch of professors and prefects. Professor McGonagall's dot travelled repeatedly around Gryffindor Tower. Professor Snape's swept the dungeons. Peter Pettigrew's name had vanished. Lupin stood behind his chair with both hands braced against its worn back. The fire snapped beside him, lighting hollows beneath his cheekbones. He had aged within the past few minutes. His face possessed a grey, exhausted stillness that Harry had previously seen only after the full moon.

Hermione sat at the side of the desk, rigid with concentration. Draconia remained beside Harry, her wand lowered but not put away. The clock on the mantel ticked. Harry read the four names written across the top of the map. Messrs Moony, Wormtail, Padfoot and Prongs. The words had been familiar for months. Now they pressed against one another, demanding a shape.

"You said you made it," Harry said.

Lupin lifted his eyes.

"Yes."

"Who is 'you'?"

The answer cost him.

"James Potter. Sirius Black. Peter Pettigrew. And me."

Harry looked at the map again. Hermione's fingers tightened around the edge of her chair. Draconia did not move at all.

"Which one were you?" Harry asked.

"Moony."

The name settled beside what Harry already knew.

"Because you're a werewolf."

Lupin flinched despite Harry's quiet voice. "Yes."

"Wormtail is Pettigrew," Hermione said.

"Yes."

"Padfoot," Draconia said, "is Sirius Black."

Lupin nodded. Harry did not need to ask who Prongs had been. His gaze found the delicate antlered mark scratched beside the name.

"My father."

"James."

A strange pressure gathered behind Harry's ribs. He had become accustomed to receiving his parents in pieces: a photograph, an old friend's expression, a story shortened because the adult telling it became uncomfortable. Even their mistakes were usually polished by grief before being handed to him. The map was different. James had made it. His hands had passed over the parchment. His thoughts had shaped its corridors. At thirteen, Harry and his parents occupied the same castle through ink and memory, separated by years no magic on the page could cross.

"What do the names mean?" Harry asked.

Lupin glanced towards the office door. With a movement of his wand, the lock clicked. A second charm settled over the walls, softening the sounds of the castle beyond them.

"They were our nicknames. We chose them during fifth year, although Sirius insisted he had invented all four much earlier. James disputed that until the day he died."

"That sounds productive," Draconia said.

"It rarely was."

Lupin's mouth nearly formed a smile. It disappeared before it settled. He moved around the chair and sat.

"When we arrived at Hogwarts, none of them knew what I was. My transformations took place in the Shrieking Shack. The Whomping Willow was planted over the entrance to the tunnel so that no student could follow me."

Hermione leaned forward. "That's why the tree was planted?"

"Yes. Madam Pomfrey escorted me there each month. The house was abandoned, isolated and strong enough to contain me. Villagers heard the noises I made and believed the building haunted. Dumbledore allowed the rumour to spread."

Harry remembered the pale tunnel drawn beneath the Willow on the map.

"You transformed alone."

"For the first few years."

Lupin's gaze rested on a crack in the desk.

"James, Sirius and Peter discovered the truth during our second year. I expected them to be disgusted. Instead, James asked whether it hurt, Sirius demanded to know why I had never told them, and Peter fainted."

Draconia's brow lifted.

"He recovered quickly," Lupin said. "They began researching Animagi almost immediately."

Hermione's mouth opened. "That requires years of study."

"It did."

"It's advanced human transfiguration. There are registrations, monitoring charms, monthly conditions—"

"They ignored the registrations."

"Of course they did," she muttered.

"By fifth year, all three could transform. Sirius became a large black dog. James, a stag. Peter was a rat."

Harry's eyes moved instinctively towards the door. The black dog on Magnolia Crescent. The dark shape near the Quidditch stands. The animal Crookshanks met beside Hagrid's hut. Not a Grim.

Sirius.

"He's been near me all year," Harry said.

Lupin's expression tightened. "I believe so."

"He watched the Quidditch match."

"Likely."

"He was outside the Dursleys'."

Hermione looked at him sharply. Harry had mentioned the dog in fragments, but not the fear that accompanied it or how often he had thought of Trelawney's warning.

"He found you there?" Lupin asked.

"He didn't do anything. He looked at me and ran."

Draconia considered this. "If his intention was to kill Harry, Privet Drive gave him an excellent opportunity."

"That does not prove he never intended harm," Hermione said, "but it makes the accepted explanation less consistent."

Harry barely heard them.

"You knew he was a dog."

Lupin went still. The office seemed to contract around the sentence.

"You knew," Harry repeated, "and you didn't tell anyone."

Lupin's hands closed upon one another. "Yes."

"Not when he escaped."

"No."

"Not when he got into the castle the first time."

"No."

"He stood over my bed tonight with a knife."

Each word emerged more evenly than the last. Harry could feel anger spreading through him, cold rather than hot. It was the kind that made every detail painfully clear.

"And you still didn't tell Dumbledore that he could turn into a dog."

"I should have."

"That wasn't my question."

"No," Lupin said. "I did not tell him."

Hermione shifted uneasily. Draconia's shoulder touched Harry's, but she did not interrupt.

"Why?"

Lupin looked older than any professor had a right to look.

"Because I would have had to tell him how often I had broken my promise."

Harry laughed once, without humour. "That's it?"

"No. It is not enough. But it is the truth."

Lupin faced him fully.

"Dumbledore gave me a place here when most parents would have demanded I be kept away from their children. He arranged the Shack, the Willow and Madam Pomfrey's help. In return, I was expected to remain there during transformations. Once James and Sirius could accompany me, we began leaving."

Hermione stared. "You roamed the grounds as a werewolf?"

"The forest. Hogsmeade. Once, very nearly, the village itself."

"You could have killed someone."

"Yes."

The agreement stopped her more effectively than a defence would have done.

"We believed James and Sirius could control me," Lupin continued. "Most months, they could. We were fifteen and mistook repeated luck for proof of our competence. There were close calls. More than I care to remember."

His thumb pressed hard against the knuckle of his opposite hand.

"I told myself that informing Dumbledore after Sirius escaped would expose James and Peter too, even though James was dead and Peter was believed to be. I told myself Sirius might have lost the ability after twelve years in Azkaban. I told myself the Dementors and the school wards would stop him."

"And when they didn't?" Harry asked.

"Then I was ashamed I hadn't told him sooner. Every day made it harder to say."

"You protected your job."

Lupin swallowed. "Yes."

"Your reputation."

"Yes."

"While Sirius knew passages into the castle."

"Yes."

Draconia's voice cut cleanly through the room. "You allowed Harry to believe a mass murderer could reach him through a method nobody was guarding because you were embarrassed."

Lupin accepted that too. "I did."

Harry shoved his chair backwards and stood. The wood struck the wall. He wanted Lupin to make an excuse. He wanted anger he could attack and defeat. Instead Lupin remained seated, offering no defence, and the disappointment had nowhere to go.

"You knew what the Dementors did to me," Harry said. "You knew he was supposed to be hunting me. You taught me how to defend myself because you thought I might meet him."

"I know."

"You could have told Dumbledore how to find him."

"I know."

"Stop saying that."

Lupin's face tightened. "What would you prefer I say?"

"That you had a better reason."

"I did not."

Silence struck harder than shouting. Harry turned away. The shelves lining the office were crowded with books and jars of creatures suspended in cloudy liquid. A Grindylow drifted behind green glass, webbed fingers opening and closing. On the mantel sat the photograph Harry had noticed during his first Patronus lesson, facedown since Christmas. He reached for it.

Lupin half-rose. "Harry—"

Harry turned the frame upright. Four boys crowded the photograph. They were perhaps sixteen. James occupied the centre, one arm around Lupin's shoulders and the other attempting to pull Sirius into the frame. Sirius was laughing, handsome and careless, his hair falling across his face. Peter stood slightly behind them, smaller than the others, grinning with the eager delight of a person pleased merely to have been included. Lupin looked thin but happy. Harry had rarely seen him happy.

"How did it happen?" Harry asked.

Lupin did not pretend to misunderstand. "The Secret Keeper?"

"Yes."

Lupin sat again.

"During the war, we knew someone close to the Order was passing information to Voldemort. Missions failed. Safe houses were attacked. People vanished after plans were shared with only a few trusted members."

"Who did you suspect?" Hermione asked.

"Sirius suspected me."

Harry looked around.

"I suspected Sirius," Lupin admitted.

The confession weighed upon him differently from the Animagus secret. This shame was quieter, worn smooth by years.

"Why?" Harry asked.

"Sirius came from a family closely associated with Voldemort. He hated them, but hatred did not always seem sufficient proof in those days. He was volatile. Secretive. Often absent on assignments he would not discuss."

"Those sound like awful reasons," Draconia said.

"They were. Fear made them seem better."

"And Pettigrew?" she asked.

"Peter encouraged neither suspicion openly. He listened. Agreed with whoever spoke to him last. Expressed concern without making accusations."

Draconia's expression cooled. "He kept both possibilities alive."

"Yes."

Harry examined the boy in the photograph. Peter looked harmless. Forgettable. The sort of person a room might lose while still looking directly at him.

"Everyone thought Sirius was the Secret Keeper," Hermione said.

"He was intended to be."

Lupin drew a slow breath.

"The Fidelius Charm conceals information inside a living soul. Once cast, the protected secret cannot be discovered except through the Keeper's voluntary disclosure. It cannot be tortured out, extracted with Legilimency or forced under Veritaserum. The Keeper must choose to reveal it."

"So whoever gave Voldemort the Potters' location did it willingly," Hermione said.

"Yes."

Harry's stomach twisted, though he had understood this before. Hearing it stated so plainly removed the last shelter of ambiguity.

"My parents chose Sirius."

"James did. Lily agreed because James trusted him completely."

"But they changed their minds."

"I did not know that until tonight."

Lupin touched the edge of the map.

"If Peter is alive, then Sirius must have persuaded them to switch Keepers. It resembles him. He would have expected Voldemort to come after him first and believed Peter too insignificant to suspect."

"Did Sirius know you suspected him?" Harry asked.

"Yes."

"And he still didn't tell you?"

"No."

"Because he suspected you."

Lupin's eyes closed briefly. "Most likely."

Harry looked at the photograph again. Four friends laughing while their future selves constructed a tragedy from secrets, fear and mistaken certainty.

"You all trusted the wrong people."

No one corrected him.

Hermione moved closer to the map. "If Sirius wasn't the Keeper, they couldn't prove he gave them away."

"He was found at the scene of Peter's supposed murder," Lupin said. "Twelve Muggles were dead. Witnesses heard Peter accuse him of betraying James and Lily."

"What physical evidence was collected?"

"I don't know."

"Were their wands examined?"

"I don't know."

"Did Sirius receive a trial?"

The last colour left Lupin's face.

Draconia saw the answer before he spoke. "He didn't."

"No."

Harry stared at him. "They sent him to Azkaban without a trial?"

"Barty Crouch authorised it during the final months of the war. Emergency powers permitted detention when evidence was considered overwhelming."

"So they didn't have to ask questions," Draconia said.

"There was a confession of sorts," Lupin said. "Sirius was laughing when the Aurors found him."

Harry remembered the man in the dormitory: hollow cheeks, fever-bright eyes, fury powerful enough to keep a ruined body moving.

"Laughing isn't confessing."

"No."

"Why didn't you ask?"

The question was quieter than the others. Lupin gazed at the four boys in the photograph.

"Because believing Sirius guilty hurt less than admitting I had doubted an innocent friend until he believed he could not trust me."

Harry wished he had shouted instead. Hermione rose and began walking around the desk. Her thoughts revealed themselves through movement now, the way Draconia's disappeared into stillness.

"The map shows names," she said. "Not appearances."

"Yes."

"It identifies people under Invisibility Cloaks and Polyjuice Potion."

"Yes."

"And Animagi?"

"An Animagus remains a person. The map shows the name."

"Could Peter have enchanted the map to display his name after his death?"

"No. He helped create the enchantments, but none of us could use it remotely. The map reads the castle through a web of revelation and tracking charms. It does not repeat what it remembers. It shows who is present."

"So Peter Pettigrew is alive," Harry said.

Lupin's gaze met his.

"Yes."

The word unlocked a different anger. Peter had eaten at the Weasleys' table. He had slept beside Ron for years. He had travelled to Hogwarts with them and hidden within reach of Harry while the wizarding world celebrated Sirius's imprisonment. He had been in the boys' dormitory last night.

"Where did he go?" Harry asked.

"We do not know."

"Then open the map."

"It is open."

"Search it."

Hermione moved beside Harry. "There are hundreds of names. The writing overlaps."

"We'll take sections."

"Harry," Lupin began.

"He could be leaving."

"And Sirius could still be inside the castle."

"Then we find both."

Lupin stood. "You are not hunting either of them."

"I wasn't asking permission."

"No. You were not."

The steadiness in Lupin's voice made Harry turn. The professor had not regained authority by pretending the last ten minutes had not happened. Guilt remained visible across his face. So did fear.

"I failed to give Dumbledore information that might have protected you," Lupin said. "I will not compound that failure by allowing three students to pursue two desperate adult wizards through a castle filled with Dementors."

"Peter isn't desperate," Harry replied. "He's been living in someone's pocket."

"Peter is cornered. That may be more dangerous."

Draconia crossed her arms. "What do you intend to do?"

"Tell Dumbledore everything."

The answer came immediately. Hermione nodded once. Harry did not.

"And then?"

"We watch the map. Quietly. If Peter knows we have discovered him, he may flee. If the Ministry learns before we can produce him in human form, they may allow the Dementors to administer the Kiss to Sirius first and ask questions afterwards."

Harry's skin crawled.

"They can't."

"There."

"Near the kitchens."

"He's still in the castle."

Lupin touched his wand to the parchment.

"Mischief managed."

Harry looked at Lupin. Trust would depend upon what he did next. Lupin drew the Marauder's Map from the drawer.

Nott.

Professor Dumbledore believed the map. He did not offer belief easily, nor did he confuse it with certainty. He listened while Lupin confessed everything in McGonagall's presence. He examined the parchment beneath three different revealing charms, asked questions about its construction and requested that each student separately describe where Peter's name had appeared. Sirius had attacked five students. Sirius had kidnapped a first-year. Sirius had duelled Professor Lupin and transformed into a wolf. According to Seamus, who heard it from Dean, who heard it from a Ravenclaw prefect, Harry had chased Sirius through three floors while wearing no trousers.

"I noticed."

Hermione nodded. She did not ask for more. The Great Hall smelled of porridge, fried tomatoes and damp wool. Rain streaked the tall windows, blurring the mountains beyond them. "You stole breakfast," Hermione said.

"Excellent power, Mr Potter," Flitwick said from behind the falling cushions. "We shall now work on direction."

Between lessons, Hermione collected sources. During lunch, Draconia wrote to her father. After dinner, Harry returned to Lupin's office. He came alone. The room had changed. The overturned chair was upright. The map waited in the centre of the desk beside three mugs and a plate of sandwiches. The photograph remained standing on the mantel.

Lupin looked up from a roll of parchment.

"Where are Hermione and Draconia?"

"Library."

"That is almost reassuring."

"They're researching how to force an Animagus to change back."

"Less reassuring."

Harry closed the door.

"Dumbledore?"

"Searching the grounds with Professor Snape. They found animal tracks near the greenhouses, but rain destroyed most of them."

"Dog?"

"Possibly."

Harry approached the desk.

"Did you tell him everything?"

"Yes."

"Even the part where you left the Shack during full moons?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"That disappointment is most useful when it changes future conduct."

"That sounds like him."

"He also removed fifteen points from Gryffindor retroactively."

Harry stared.

"A joke," Lupin said.

It took Harry a second.

"You make those?"

"Rarely. I may be out of practice."

Harry sat opposite him. The map remained blank between them.

"Open it."

Lupin studied him. "You should be sleeping."

"So should you."

"Professors are permitted hypocrisy."

"That explains Snape."

Lupin's mouth moved.

He touched the map and said, "I solemnly swear that I am up to no good."

Ink spread. Harry leaned closer. They searched in silence. Lupin took the lower floors and grounds; Harry examined the towers and dormitories. Peter did not appear.

"Could he have left?"

"Yes."

"Would the map show Hogsmeade?"

"Only the tunnels and the immediate edge of the grounds. The enchantments are anchored to Hogwarts."

Harry traced the passage beneath the Whomping Willow.

"Why didn't you tell me about this tunnel?"

"I was afraid you would use it."

"That was sensible."

"I thought so."

"I would have used it."

"I know."

The admission eased a small part of Harry's anger despite himself. He sat back.

"Tell me about the map."

Lupin looked relieved by the safer subject, which nearly made Harry withdraw the question. He did not.

"It began because Sirius wanted to know when Madam Pomfrey left the passage beneath the Willow," Lupin said. "He drew a crude plan of the grounds and enchanted a dot to represent her. James improved the tracking spell. Peter discovered how to tie it into the castle's existing protections."

"Peter did?"

"He was talented when the task interested him. He preferred people to assume otherwise."

Lupin touched one corner of the parchment.

"We worked on it for nearly two years. The hardest part was identifying everyone by name. Hogwarts already possesses enchantments that recognise those inside it. We persuaded the map to listen."

"Persuaded?"

"James said 'borrowed.' Professor Flitwick would have said 'dangerously interfered with.'"

"How did Filch get it?"

"Sirius attempted to charm every suit of armour into singing the school song backwards. Filch confiscated it during the escape."

"Did the armour sing?"

"For three weeks."

Harry smiled. The photograph drew his eye again.

"What was Sirius like?"

Lupin's expression became careful.

"You do not have to make him sound good," Harry said. "I'd rather know."

"Sirius was loyal to the point of self-destruction. Cruel when angry. Generous without restraint. He could make an entire room feel dull if he left it."

"That doesn't make sense."

"It did with Sirius."

Lupin stood, took the photograph from the mantel and placed it between them.

"His family made him earn every bit of affection they showed him. He came to school determined to hate everything they loved. Sometimes he had a good reason; sometimes he just wanted to annoy them. James was the first person he trusted to like him without asking anything in return."

"And my father?"

"James thought he could improve anything if we let him interfere. Being wrong never discouraged him for long."

"That sounds terrible."

"It often was."

"But you loved him."

"Very much."

Harry touched the frame. The figures moved. James pulled Peter forward. Sirius shoved James in response. Lupin rolled his eyes while smiling.

"Did Sirius know me?"

"Yes."

"How well?"

"You were fifteen months old when James and Lily died. Sirius visited whenever he could. He was your godfather in every sense they could give him."

"Why him?"

"James would have chosen no one else. Lily resisted because she believed Sirius should first demonstrate an ability to keep a houseplant alive."

"Could he?"

"No."

"Fair."

"She agreed eventually."

Lupin watched the photograph.

"Sirius bought you a toy broom for your first birthday."

Harry's fingers stopped.

"I had a broom?"

"It hovered two feet above the floor. Lily wrote that you nearly killed the cat and destroyed a vase Petunia had sent as a wedding gift."

"Aunt Petunia sent them a vase?"

"It was an exceptionally ugly vase."

Harry tried to imagine Privet Drive connected to his parents through an ordinary parcel. The image refused to settle.

"Sirius was delighted," Lupin continued. "Lily threatened to confiscate the broom. James argued that your steering demonstrated natural talent."

"Did it?"

"You flew into the mantel."

"That doesn't answer the question."

"You are very like him."

Harry looked down at the photograph. The compliment did not feel uncomplicated anymore. James had been brave, reckless, loyal and wrong. Sirius had loved him and concealed the most important decision of their lives. Lupin had doubted them both. Peter had smiled from the edge of every picture.

"Was Peter ever my friend?" Harry asked.

Lupin frowned.

"I mean, did he care about me at all?"

"I cannot know what he felt."

"He visited?"

"Yes."

"Held me?"

"Yes."

"And then gave Voldemort the secret."

"Yes."

Harry looked from the cage to her. "Should I ask?"

"No," Draconia said.

"Excellent."

She placed it on the desk. The cage was small enough for a rat, constructed from closely woven silver bars. Runes had been carved around its base. A narrow door opened on one side, secured by three linked locks set into a single plate. So long as all three remained sealed, the runes formed one continuous ward.

"My father sent it."

"That quickly?" Lupin asked.

"His owls are quicker than ours."

Hermione distributed books across the remaining desk space.

"We found the reversal procedure."

Lupin picked up the top volume. "This is restricted."

"Madam Pince made an exception."

"No, she didn't," Harry said.

Hermione looked offended. "She was distracted."

"By what?"

"Draconia told her someone was folding page corners in the west aisle."

Draconia sat. "There may have been panic."

Lupin looked between them.

"I see."

Hermione opened the book to a marked page.

"An Animagus can be forced into human form using a coordinated Transfiguration reversal. It requires at least two competent spellcasters if the subject resists."

"Three is safer," Lupin said. "One maintains containment while the others perform the reversal."

Harry examined the diagram.

"What happens if it goes wrong?"

"The transformation may stop halfway," Hermione said.

The illustration beside the text depicted a woman with feathers growing from her face. Harry closed the book slightly.

"Let's not get it wrong."

"The cage is charmed to contain transformation," Draconia said. "Once Peter enters, the bars will expand if he becomes human. The ward will then prevent him becoming small enough to escape—provided the locks remain sealed."

"How do we persuade him into it?"

"We put food inside."

"He's a wizard, Draco."

"He has spent twelve years pretending to be a rat. Habit must count for something."

Hermione ignored them.

"We also need to establish whether the Fidelius Charm could have been broken another way."

"It cannot," Lupin said.

"You're certain?"

"Yes."

"We should still verify."

"I expected you to say that."

"You sound tired."

"I am regretting telling Miss Granger to be thorough."

Harry took a sandwich from the plate. Draconia immediately noticed.

He sighed. "Don't make it an event."

"I said nothing."

"You looked."

"I possess eyes."

Hermione continued turning pages. For the first time in weeks, their concern did not make Harry want to run. It settled around him without closing. Draconia pushed the plate nearer but returned to the cage. Hermione asked whether he wanted the last cheese sandwich without watching to see how much he ate. They had learned. So had he.

"We need to watch the map in shifts," Harry said.

Lupin shook his head. "The professors will watch."

"The professors teach."

"Yes. Very inconvenient of them."

"Peter knows your habits. He helped make the map. He'll move when he thinks nobody is looking."

Draconia considered Harry's argument. "He will also expect adults to search obvious hiding places. Students move unpredictably."

"Especially these students," Lupin murmured.

Hermione drew a timetable from her bag.

Harry stared. "You already made one?"

"I started during History of Magic."

"She gave me midnight to two," Draconia said.

"You are usually awake."

"That is not the same as granting permission."

Lupin held up one hand.

"You may assist from this office. Nobody follows a dot without an adult. If Peter appears, you send the silver message charm to Dumbledore and wait."

Harry opened his mouth.

"Beforehand," Draconia reminded him.

He looked at her.

"King's Cross," she said. "Your agreement with my father."

"I agreed to tell a professor before doing anything dangerous."

"And Lupin is a professor."

"That feels like exploiting wording."

"It is how agreements function."

Lupin's tired smile returned. Harry leaned back.

"Fine. We wait."

"Convincingly," Hermione added.

"I said fine."

"You said it in the voice you use immediately before redefining a word."

Harry pointed towards the schedule. "Put me on Draco's shift."

Draconia lifted a brow. "Why?"

"So someone can stop you investigating alone."

"That is offensively reasonable."

"And I want the two-to-four shift," Harry said.

"No," Hermione and Lupin answered together.

He looked at them.

Draconia opened the cage door. "You walked into that one."

Before leaving, Hermione and Draconia added one final enchantment to the cage. They tied the runes around its base to Peter's name upon the Map: if he entered Hogwarts while the parchment was active, the silver would warm and vibrate against the nearest linked wand. Lupin agreed to keep the Map activated and folded inside his robes during lessons, while Dumbledore or McGonagall would hold it whenever he could not. It was an inelegant system by Hermione's standards and a perfectly functional one by Draconia's.

Waiting became its own form of pursuit. For nearly three weeks, Peter did not appear. The trio attended lessons, completed enough homework to avoid suspicion and returned to Lupin's office whenever their assigned shift began. The map covered most of the desk. Candles burned low around it. Rain passed and left the castle wrapped in a damp silver haze. Hermione watched by method.

She divided the map into sections, checked each in order and marked the time in her notebook. Every quarter hour, she began again. The system might have been unbearable from anyone else. From Hermione, it became a promise that nothing would escape merely because they were tired. Draconia watched like a hunter. She learned the routes of professors, ghosts and prefects until deviations stood out. She noticed students entering corridors without leaving them and discovered four cupboard passages absent from the map's drawn lines. Her theories accumulated on separate pieces of parchment she refused to label as theories.

Harry watched the way he had watched Privet Drive. He looked for hesitation. Names that doubled back. Dots that paused before entering rooms. Movements shaped by fear rather than destination. Peter remained absent. Sirius appeared twice.

The first time, his name flickered at the forest's edge shortly after midnight. Draconia saw it and gripped Harry's arm hard enough to hurt. They sent the silver message. Lupin arrived within a minute, robes thrown over his nightclothes. By then Sirius had vanished beyond the map's reach. The second time, he appeared near Hagrid's hut. Crookshanks's name was not beside him. The map did not show ordinary animals.

Harry pictured the ginger cat slipping beneath branches, carrying messages between castle and cave. The following afternoon, Crookshanks entered the library with leaves tangled in his tail. Hermione lifted him onto the chair beside her.

"Where have you been?"

Crookshanks yawned. Harry drew a folded note from his pocket.

Draconia watched the cat. "If he gives that to a professor, I will deny involvement."

"If he gives it to Filch, Sirius will be arrested for poor penmanship."

"My handwriting is fine."

"It leans."

"So does yours."

Hermione took the note and added a charm that concealed the writing until Sirius touched it.

"What are you telling him?" she asked.

"That we know about Peter. And to wait."

"He has not demonstrated a talent for waiting," Draconia said.

"Neither have you."

"I have practised."

Harry tied the note loosely beneath Crookshanks's collar.

"Take it to Padfoot."

The cat looked at him. Harry felt ridiculous.

"To the black dog."

Crookshanks stood, stretched and walked across the table, stepping directly over Hermione's notes.

"Mind the ink."

He jumped to the floor and disappeared between the shelves.

Draconia stared after him. "If that works, I intend to reassess every conversation I have had in his presence."

"It worked before," Hermione said. "He stole Neville's password list for Sirius."

"Borrowed," Harry said.

"That list allowed a fugitive into the castle."

"You sound judgemental."

"I am."

By dusk, Crookshanks had crossed the wet grounds and found the cave beneath the beech roots. Sirius heard him forcing his way through the ivy and reached instinctively for the stolen wand inside his boot. The cat emerged carrying a rolled piece of parchment beneath his collar and regarded Sirius as though the journey had been made necessary by someone else's incompetence.

"Where did you get that?"

Crookshanks offered no explanation. Sirius freed the parchment and unfolded it twice. The page appeared blank until his thumb brushed the edge. Ink spread beneath his hand.

Three lines appeared in an untidy, unfamiliar hand.

We know Peter is alive.

Do not go after him alone.

Please.

Sirius stopped breathing. He did not need to recognise the handwriting. Harry knew Peter was alive, and Harry had written please.

The Firebolt had been an act of spectacular cowardice. After learning that Harry's Nimbus had been destroyed, Sirius had found an old advertisement for Quality Quidditch Supplies and spent three days composing an order that would not reveal his location. Crookshanks took it to the post office in Hogsmeade. Sirius instructed the shop to remove the money from his vault and deliver the broom anonymously. A godfather ought to know more about his godson than what broom he wanted.

Sirius knew almost nothing, so he had sent the best one available. The gift had been confiscated, which was sensible. Sirius recognised that now. At the time, he had spent a very satisfying hour imagining creative punishments for whichever professor was responsible.

The note was not forgiveness. It was not belief. It was a request. Sirius read it again while Crookshanks settled against his side, purring so loudly that the vibration travelled through the blankets.

"I suppose this means no murder."

The purring continued.

"Very inconvenient."

Sirius folded the parchment and placed it inside his shirt. He could not promise patience. Twelve years had burned most of his away. He could, however, attempt not to make another clever plan.

Near the end of the third week, they waited with Lupin while late sleet whispered against the windows and silvered the grounds upon the Map's edges. The fire burned lower than usual, and Lupin sat wrapped in a patched cardigan, his face lined with the approach of the full moon. Harry noticed him rub his wrist.

"When is it?" he asked.

Lupin understood. "Three nights."

"Do you have the potion?"

"Professor Snape delivered it this afternoon."

Draconia glanced towards the covered goblet on the shelf.

"Have you taken it?"

"It must be taken each evening during the week preceding the transformation."

"That was an impressive number of words for no," Harry said.

Lupin looked between them. "Not yet."

Hermione reached for the goblet.

"I am capable of—"

She handed it to him. Lupin drank. His face contorted at the taste.

Harry grinned. "Good?"

"Remarkable. I may ask Severus for the recipe."

"He would give you poison."

"This is difficult to distinguish."

Lupin set down the empty goblet. The map remained quiet. Hermione checked the ground floor. Draconia examined the dungeons. Harry watched Hagrid's hut and the Willow.

"Tell us another story," he said.

Lupin glanced up.

"About what?"

"My parents. Sirius. All of you."

"Harry, we should concentrate."

"I am concentrating."

"On irritating me."

"It helps."

Lupin looked towards the map. "James once spent an entire month pretending to be afraid of rabbits."

Hermione paused. "Why?"

"Sirius bet him he could not convince Professor McGonagall."

"Did he?"

"No. He convinced Professor Slughorn."

"That raises more questions," Draconia said.

"It began during a Transfiguration lesson. James transformed his teacup into a rabbit, the rabbit bit him and Sirius laughed. James claimed the bite had awakened a profound terror."

"Your friends were idiots," Hermione said.

"Frequently."

"What did Peter do?" Harry asked.

Lupin's faint smile faded at the edges.

"He kept a lettuce leaf in his pocket and threw it at James whenever he entered a room."

Harry pictured Peter as the photograph showed him, laughing behind the others.

"Was he always pretending?"

"No."

The answer came after thought.

"That would be easier," Lupin continued. "If every kindness were false, we could remove him from our memories without losing anything. Peter could be funny. He was patient when others were not. During one transformation, James injured his leg and Sirius had to remain with me. Peter returned to the castle alone, stole bandages from the hospital wing and came back before dawn."

"Why did he betray them?" Harry asked.

"I have asked myself that for twelve years."

Draconia spoke without looking away from the map. "Fear."

"Perhaps."

"He attached himself to people stronger than he was. When Voldemort became the strongest person in the war, Pettigrew adjusted his loyalty."

Hermione frowned. "That makes it sound inevitable."

"It was a choice," Draconia said. "Fear explains choices. It does not excuse them."

Lupin studied her.

"My father says loyalty based entirely upon safety is simply delayed betrayal," she added.

"Your father has led an interesting life."

"You have no idea."

Harry watched the name Lucius Malfoy moving through his imagination: Death Eater, father, rescuer, politician, man trying to change without surrendering the instincts that had kept him powerful.

"Do you think he'll find evidence?" Harry asked.

Draconia's confidence hesitated.

"If evidence exists."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," she said quietly. "But it is what he would answer."

She moved a small wooden marker across the map. Harry nudged her shoulder. She did not move away. Near midnight, Hermione's head began to lower towards her book. Draconia watched it descend.

The instant Hermione's forehead nearly touched the page, Draconia slid a cushion beneath it. Harry covered his mouth.

Hermione woke. "I was reading."

"With your eyes closed?" Harry asked.

"I was thinking."

"Very deeply."

She glared at them both. Lupin rose from his chair and took the map.

"That is enough."

"Our shift lasts another hour," Hermione protested.

"Your schedule was not approved by a member of staff."

"It was approved yesterday."

"I am exercising the rarely used revision clause."

Draconia looked interested. "There is a revision clause?"

"There is now."

Harry stood. At the door, he looked back. Lupin had returned the photograph to the mantel. This time, he left it facing the room.

"Professor?"

"Yes?"

"I'm still angry."

"I know."

Harry waited.

"But I'll be here tomorrow."

Lupin's hand tightened around the map.

"I will too."

The reply from Alastor Moody arrived inside a dead rat. Lucius discovered it upon his breakfast plate. Narcissa looked up from the morning paper.

Dilly, the youngest house-elf, hovered beside the table in horror. "Dilly is not placing dead creatures upon Master's china."

No formal interview.

No trial record.

Explosion residue classified as inconclusive. Witnesses reported Pettigrew stood with wand raised before blast. Suppressed in summary.

Peter reappeared on Thursday, the day of the full moon. Harry was in the final Defence Against the Dark Arts lesson when the silver cage began vibrating inside Draconia's bag. She froze. Professor Lupin continued explaining the defensive use of concealment charms, but his eyes flicked towards her desk. The alert enchantment had activated: Peter's name was somewhere upon the Map. Draconia raised two fingers.

Lupin turned to the blackboard.

"Read chapters nine through eleven. Silently."

The class groaned.

"No complaints. Mr Finnigan, the instruction includes you."

While parchment rustled, Lupin crossed the room and crouched beside Draconia's desk as if examining her work.

"Where?"

"I cannot tell without the map."

Hermione had already packed her book.

Lupin straightened. "Miss Granger, Miss Malfoy and Mr Potter, remain after class."

Harry looked at the clock. Twenty-three minutes. The cage vibrated again. Peter could cross half the castle in that time. Harry attempted to read. The words refused to remain still.

When the bell rang, students poured into the corridor. Seamus asked what they had done. Harry said they had developed a rare allergy to homework and closed the door in his face. Lupin drew the map from inside his robes.

"I solemnly swear that I am up to no good."

Ink unfolded. They crowded around the nearest desk. Peter Pettigrew moved along the corridor behind the kitchens.

"There," Hermione said.

His dot travelled slowly, stopping at intervals beside storage rooms.

"He's searching for food," Harry said.

"Or a route outside," Draconia replied.

Lupin conjured the silver message and sent it through the wall.

"Dumbledore is with the governors," he said. "Professor McGonagall will meet us near the Great Hall."

"Meet us?" Harry repeated.

"You are going to Gryffindor Tower."

"No."

"She will."

"What," he said softly, "are you doing?"

"Following Peter Pettigrew."

"He is moving beneath the kitchens."

"You."

"Yes."

"She is not involved in my decision."

Draconia inclined her head. "And Hermione keeps the receipts."

Snape's eyes returned to Lupin.

"You concealed information that endangered every student in this castle."

"Yes."

"He knows."

"Of course he does."

"Show me where Pettigrew was last seen."

Lupin hesitated.

"He may have transformed and entered a space the map cannot read," Hermione said.

"Pipes," Draconia replied.

"There," Harry said.

A narrow pipe travelled towards the courtyard, then split: one branch led to the greenhouses, another descended beneath the grounds.

Snape examined it.

"The old refuse channels. They emerge near the vegetable gardens."

They moved. McGonagall met them outside the Great Hall. She objected violently to the students accompanying the search. Harry objected with equal conviction. Draconia explained that the cage's containment runes had been keyed to her wand and could not be activated by anyone else.
