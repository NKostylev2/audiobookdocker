The Goblet of Fire

The house on the hill had begun showing signs of life again.

Nobody in Little Hangleton admitted to watching it. Watching the old Riddle house suggested curiosity, and curiosity implied that a person had nothing better to do than concern themselves with a building which had stood empty for the greater part of fifty years. The villagers preferred to observe it indirectly—from upstairs windows while drawing the curtains, through the steamed glass of the pub, or over newspapers held at angles which fooled no one.

For most of the summer, there had been very little to see.

The house stood as it had for decades: dark windows, crooked chimneys and ivy thick enough to swallow entire sections of stone. Heat had bleached the long grass surrounding it. The iron gates leaned towards one another without meeting, and the drive had narrowed beneath nettles until even the foxes used it reluctantly.

Then a light appeared on the second floor.

It did not burn every night. Sometimes a week passed without it. Sometimes it lasted until dawn, a narrow yellow wound behind curtains too rotten to close properly. Once, shortly after midnight, every window in the eastern wing flashed green at the same instant. The village electricity failed three minutes later, providing an explanation sufficiently ordinary that most people accepted it aloud.

Frank Bryce did not.

Frank had been the Riddles' gardener before most of Little Hangleton was old enough to remember the Riddles alive. He had kept the position after their deaths, despite there being very little garden left and no living Riddle to care whether the roses survived. The owners who followed paid him irregularly, visited rarely and appeared satisfied so long as the roof did not collapse in a manner requiring paperwork.

At seventy-four, Frank considered this an acceptable arrangement.

The cottage provided with the grounds had a sound roof, a stove which worked when threatened and a clear view of anyone foolish enough to approach the property. Frank kept the lower path open, repaired what he could and ignored the villagers who still lowered their voices when he entered the pub.

They had suspected him once.

The police had asked questions about the Riddles' final evening, the locked dining-room door and three bodies without a mark between them. Frank answered. Then he answered again. He spent a night in a cell while men with cleaner fingernails discussed whether military service had made him violent.

The doctors found no poison, wound or disease. The police released him.

The village did not.

Frank stayed partly because leaving would have resembled an admission and partly because the cottage belonged to the only life the war had not taken from him. The vegetable patch was his. The stove was his. The narrow bed beside the east-facing window was his. He did not care whether anyone considered that stubbornness. He had survived men with stronger opinions and better weapons.

Near the end of July, he saw the light again.

The day had been painfully hot. By sunset, clouds had gathered above the valley in purple layers, but no rain fell. Thunder moved beyond the hills without approaching, and the air hung heavy enough to make every breath feel second-hand.

Frank sat beside his open kitchen window with a mug of tea cooling near one elbow. A plate containing two slices of bread and half a tomato rested upon the table. The wireless muttered about motorway delays and storms expected across the Midlands before morning. Frank listened to none of it.

He kept watching the house.

A narrow strip of yellow showed behind the curtains of the upper drawing room.

Frank placed his mug down.

The light had not been there when he finished locking the tool shed. He would have noticed. The house stood black against the evening whenever he crossed the lower field, and he had spent enough years studying its shape to recognise the smallest change.

Someone was inside.

He waited five minutes.

No car stood near the gates. No vehicle had climbed the road. The drive was too overgrown to conceal tyre marks and too dry to erase them. Whoever had entered had come on foot or approached from the rear fields.

Neither possibility improved Frank's opinion of them.

He rose with a quiet curse and took the heavy torch from beside the stove. The drawer beneath it held a carving knife, a hammer and an old service revolver he had not fired since 1945.

Frank checked the chamber.

Empty.

He had known it would be. The police removed the ammunition years ago after several boys broke into his cottage and discovered the weapon beneath his mattress. Frank kept it because an unloaded gun still encouraged reflection when viewed from a distance.

He placed it inside his coat.

The first drop of rain struck the window as he opened the door.

Outside, the garden smelled of dust and approaching weather. Long grass brushed his boots. Frank crossed the lower path without using the torch, allowing his eyes to adjust to the dark. The moon had disappeared behind cloud, but the old house rose above him, its roofline cutting into the unsettled sky.

The light still burned.

Halfway up the hill, Frank saw movement beside the yew trees.

He stopped.

Something long passed between two trunks. It moved without disturbing the grass, low to the ground and briefly pale where the distant window touched it. Frank heard the dry drag of scales before the shape disappeared beneath the hedge.

A snake.

Not one belonging to any English garden he had tended.

Frank tightened his hand around the torch and continued.

The iron gate opened beneath his palm with a sound like a person drawing breath. He had oiled the hinges three days earlier. The squeal irritated him almost as much as the light. He eased it shut and followed the ruined drive towards the house.

The front door was locked.

That meant little. The kitchen entrance at the rear had not fitted its frame properly since the winter of 1962. Frank followed the wall, stepping over fallen guttering and a dead branch. Rain began spotting the shoulders of his coat.

The kitchen door stood open by several inches.

Warm air escaped through the gap.

Frank pushed it wider.

The smell inside stopped him.

The abandoned rooms usually smelled of damp plaster, old timber and the nests of whatever animals had claimed them. Tonight, something bitter occupied the air. Herbs, perhaps, though none he recognised. Beneath them lingered the metallic scent of heated coins and something faintly sweet which reminded him unpleasantly of meat left too long beside a stove.

He turned on the torch.

The beam crossed cracked tiles and empty cupboards. Someone had swept a path through the dust. Mud marked the floor in narrow prints made by expensive shoes.

Frank entered.

The house listened.

Rain tapped the broken windows. Pipes shifted inside the walls despite the water having been disconnected for years. From somewhere above came the murmur of voices.

One belonged to a man.

The second did not sound as though it belonged inside a human throat.

Frank extinguished the torch.

He knew the ground floor well enough to cross it in darkness. He passed the old pantry, the servants' corridor and the dining room where the Riddles had been found. Its door was closed.

Frank did not look towards it.

The staircase curved through the main hall. Half the carpet had rotted away, exposing boards beneath. He placed his weight near the wall, avoiding the fourth and seventh steps. The banister shifted beneath his palm.

The voices grew clearer.

"The arrangements in Britain are more complicated than they were when I left."

The speaker was educated. Older than Frank first expected, though not elderly. Every word arrived carefully measured, as though carelessness belonged to people beneath him.

"Complicated," the second voice repeated.

The word contained a soft, breathless amusement.

Frank stopped below the landing.

"You permitted one frightened man to compromise nearly every name he possessed," the high voice continued. "You allowed Lucius Malfoy to recognise your intentions. You fled without securing the object entrusted to your circle. Now you offer complications as though they occurred without you."

Silence followed.

"Pettigrew deceived all of us, my Lord."

"Pettigrew served himself. That should not have surprised anyone."

Frank frowned.

My Lord.

The title sounded ridiculous inside the ruined house. Criminals, perhaps. Some political group. Little Hangleton had acquired several wealthy men over the years who enjoyed titles no one else remembered granting them.

He climbed another step.

"Pettigrew surrendered names before escaping a Ministry transfer," the man said. "Properties have been searched. Accounts have been frozen. Had I remained—"

"You would have occupied the cell he abandoned."

"Yes."

The admission carried no shame. Only resentment.

"Then perhaps his betrayal has proved useful. Fear has finally compelled you to locate me."

"He will search for you as well."

"When every safer door has closed." The high voice held no affection now. "A rat's loyalty is not admirable, but it is predictable."

Frank reached the landing.

The upper corridor stretched through darkness. The voices came from the drawing room at its far end. Its door had been left slightly open, allowing a blade of firelight to cross the floorboards.

He moved closer.

"I searched for months," the man said. "The forests were not empty."

"They rarely are."

"There were rumours in Tirana. Dead animals. Missing travellers. A village where every mirror cracked during the same night."

"And you followed them."

"I followed you."

"Do not confuse the two."

Thunder rolled above the house. Old glass trembled in its frames.

Frank reached the narrow table outside the drawing room. Its surface held a silver tray recently cleaned of dust. Upon it rested a glass of dark liquid and a folded newspaper.

The photograph upon the front page moved.

A thin man stood behind a numbered board. He raised his face, laughed without sound and returned to his original position. The movement repeated.

Frank leaned closer despite himself.

The headline above the photograph had been printed in heavy black letters.

PETTIGREW NAMES FORMER ASSOCIATES BEFORE ESCAPE

Beneath it, smaller words mentioned a magical council, an unlawful transformation and the overturned conviction of someone named Sirius Black.

Frank read the first sentence twice.

From within the room, the high voice spoke again.

"And the Ministry woman?"

"She told us what she knew before she died."

"What she knew," the voice corrected. "There is always a distinction."

Frank's hand left the newspaper.

"She confirmed the Tournament," the man continued. "Hogwarts will host it. The other schools arrive in autumn."

"Dumbledore agreed?"

"He objected. The Ministry wishes to reassure the public after Black's exoneration and Pettigrew's arrest. International cooperation photographs well."

The creature in the room laughed softly.

"Governments are never more useful than when attempting to appear calm."

Frank shifted closer to the gap.

Fire burned in a grate which had not been connected to a usable chimney for decades. The room had been cleaned only where necessary. Dust sheets covered the furniture. Candles floated above them without stands or wires, their flames perfectly still despite the draught.

A man stood beside the fireplace.

He was tall and narrow, dressed entirely in black despite the heat. Grey threaded his dark hair near the temples. One hand rested upon the back of a chair, and a heavy ring bearing a black stone caught the firelight whenever his fingers moved.

Frank could not see the person seated opposite him.

He could see one small white hand curled around the chair's edge.

It had fingers too long for an infant and nails which ended in translucent points.

Frank's stomach tightened.

"The boy cannot enter," the standing man said. "The age restriction—"

"—is an instruction given to an object. Instructions can be altered."

"Dumbledore will examine it."

"Then he must examine exactly what we intend him to see."

The man beside the fire inclined his head, though reluctance stiffened the movement.

"There remains the problem of access."

"You have already given me the answer."

"Crouch's son."

The small hand tightened around the chair.

"My most faithful servant."

The words were almost affectionate. Frank found that more disturbing than the talk of the dead woman.

"He remains under his father's control," the man said. "The old fool keeps him beneath an Imperius Curse. The elf watches him whenever Crouch leaves the house."

"And Crouch will attend the sporting event."

"Half their government will."

"Then half their government will be looking in the wrong direction."

The standing man considered this.

"The grounds will be crowded. International visitors. Patrols. Families."

"Chaos requires an audience."

"Freeing him will expose us."

"Only if you are caught."

Frank recognised the silence which followed. He had heard it from officers instructing younger men to cross ground they had no intention of crossing themselves.

The man drew one slow breath.

"I will retrieve him."

"You will do more. Give him a face the castle trusts. Give him a place from which he can watch the boy. When the final path opens, my servant will ensure Potter follows it."

"And the others?"

"There are always others."

The fire shifted.

For the first time, Frank saw what occupied the chair.

The body was no larger than a child's and wrapped in dark cloth. Its head was almost hairless. Skin stretched raw and pale across a shape which looked unfinished, as though whatever lived inside had been forced into flesh too small to contain it. Two eyes reflected red beside the flames.

Frank had seen men burned inside armoured vehicles. He had helped carry what remained of them. Nothing in the chair resembled an injury medicine might have prevented.

It resembled something waiting to become worse.

He stepped backwards.

The floorboard beneath his heel creaked.

Inside the drawing room, the conversation stopped.

Frank did not breathe.

The man beside the fireplace turned towards the door.

For several seconds, nobody moved.

Then something cold touched Frank's ankle.

He looked down.

The snake lay across the corridor behind him.

It was longer than a man was tall and thick through the centre, its scales pale beneath the candlelight. Its head rested near Frank's boot. A dark tongue passed between its jaws.

Frank reached for the revolver.

The snake hissed.

From inside the drawing room came an answering sound.

Not imitation.

Conversation.

The pale head in the chair turned towards the door.

"Bring him in, Nott."

The man moved quickly.

Frank pulled the revolver and pointed it through the gap. Nott flicked one hand. A length of polished wood appeared between his fingers. He spoke a word Frank did not understand.

The revolver tore itself from Frank's grip.

It crossed the room and struck the wall hard enough to split the handle. Frank barely had time to register the impossibility before his body became rigid.

His arms locked against his sides. His knees straightened. He stayed upright only because whatever held him refused to allow him to fall.

Nott stepped into the corridor.

Up close, his face was leaner than it had appeared beside the fire. Rain had darkened the shoulders of his robes, though Frank had heard no door before entering. A pale scar passed from his right temple into the hairline.

His eyes moved over Frank without surprise.

"The groundskeeper."

Frank attempted to answer. His jaw would not open.

"He was meant to have left years ago," Nott said.

"And yet he did not."

"I can alter his memory."

The creature in the chair made a thoughtful sound.

"You have already failed to appreciate one apparently insignificant man. I would prefer not to build our return upon repetition."

Nott's mouth tightened.

He raised the polished stick again. Frank lifted from the floor.

The invisible force carried him into the drawing room and placed him upright before the fire. Movement returned abruptly. Frank staggered, caught himself against the covered table and turned towards Nott.

"Whatever you're doing here," he said, "people know I came up this hill."

It was a lie. Nobody knew. His nearest neighbour believed Frank had stopped walking farther than the pub after dark.

Nott looked towards the chair.

The red eyes stayed upon Frank.

"Do they?"

The voice was weaker than any voice had a right to be while carrying that much certainty.

Frank straightened.

"Police check the place."

"The police."

"You may find them less impressed by your tricks than an old man."

Nott's expression suggested Frank had misunderstood the situation so profoundly that correction would be tedious.

The creature appeared entertained.

"You have seen magic before."

"I've seen men frighten people with things they thought made them powerful."

"And what became of those men?"

"Most of them died."

Nott shifted. The creature smiled.

Its mouth was thin and lipless.

"An answer built from experience. What is your name?"

Frank hesitated.

"Bryce."

"Frank Bryce."

Hearing his full name spoken by something he had never met filled him with a colder fear than the moving newspaper or the stolen gun.

"You worked for my father."

Frank looked at the ruined face.

The resemblance was impossible. Yet the statement changed the room around him. The dining room below. The bodies. The locked door and three people dead without injury.

"Tom Riddle's boy," Frank said.

Nott inhaled sharply.

The creature's eyes narrowed.

Frank remembered the younger Tom Riddle as the village remembered him: handsome, wealthy and certain the world would arrange itself around his comfort. He remembered photographs in silver frames, a wedding spoken about for months and the years after it failed when the name became something people mentioned only to one another. Whatever sat before him bore almost nothing of that face.

Almost.

Frank remembered the night the family died.

"You killed them."

Nott pointed his wand.

"Mind your—"

"Leave him."

Nott lowered it.

The creature regarded Frank with open interest.

"The village believed you responsible."

"For a while."

"You remained regardless."

"It was my home."

The red eyes moved briefly towards the rotting walls.

"People attach extraordinary meaning to places which have offered them very little."

Frank thought of the cottage, the vegetable patch and the kettle which required striking against the stove before it boiled.

"Sometimes very little is still yours."

The room became quiet.

The snake crossed the threshold. It moved around Frank's boots and coiled beside the chair. The creature reached down, passing long fingers across pale scales.

"You are not afraid to die."

Frank considered lying again.

"Everyone's afraid of something."

"A better answer."

The creature lifted the wand resting across its lap.

Frank understood the object now. The newspaper, the floating candles, the revolver torn from his hand. He could not explain any of it, but explanation mattered less than the direction in which the wand pointed.

"You murdered that woman," he said.

"Yes."

"And you're planning to murder a boy."

"Eventually."

Nott watched Frank as though waiting for panic to improve the scene.

Frank's heart beat painfully beneath his ribs. He wanted the cottage. He wanted the tea cooling beside the wireless. He wanted another morning in which nothing more consequential occurred than a broken fence.

Wanting those things did not make the room less real.

"Then I hope he gives you trouble."

The creature smiled again.

"He usually does."

Green light filled the drawing room.

Frank experienced no pain. One moment he stood beneath the floating candles with rain beginning against the windows. The next, the room continued without him.

His body struck the floor.

Nott watched the dead man's fingers uncurl against the boards.

Rainwater had begun entering through a crack in the ceiling. One drop struck the floor beside Frank Bryce's open hand. Another darkened the shoulder of his coat. The old man looked smaller in death, although nothing else about him had become less inconvenient.

"The Ministry will investigate," Nott said.

His master's attention had already returned to the fire.

"They did not understand the first deaths in this house. Time has not improved them."

"Potter may see this one."

The white hand tightened around the wand.

"Yes."

The answer cooled the room more effectively than the storm.

Nott looked away from the body. "The connection survived?"

"The diary was never its source."

The diary.

His master did not need to say more.

Nott still remembered the object when it had been only one among dozens stored by families who preferred not to ask how their collections began: a small black book without a title, unimpressive enough to be overlooked and dangerous enough to make that oversight useful.

He had placed it in Draconia Malfoy's room to punish Lucius.

The intention had been elegant. Lucius would lose the child around whom he had reorganised every ambition, and whatever scandal followed would turn his new political caution into public humiliation. Nott had expected the consequences to remain contained within Hogwarts.

Instead, the diary had been destroyed.

The girl had survived.

Harry Potter had survived.

Lucius had turned his household against men whose secrets he once protected.

Nott carried each outcome like an unpaid debt.

"Lucius continues searching for me," he said.

"Let him."

"He knows too much."

"He knows what he was trusted to know. His imagination will make the gaps more dangerous than the truth."

"His daughter remains close to Potter."

The red eyes reflected the fire.

"Then Lucius has given us another means of reaching him."

Nott did not answer.

Thunder broke directly above the house. Rain struck the windows in sudden sheets, washing channels through dust which had not been disturbed for decades. The snake lifted its head from beside the chair.

"The World Cup begins in three weeks," Nott said. "Crouch will bring the elf. If the son attends beneath concealment, she will be the only person watching him closely enough to interfere."

"She will choose the family she was taught to obey."

"And if she does not?"

"Then you will remind her what disobedience costs."

Nott's ring pressed into the back of the chair beneath his hand.

"Once Crouch's son is free, he will need time. The castle has protections even you cannot dismiss."

"I do not dismiss them." His master's voice became soft. "I allow those protections to persuade our enemies they are safe."

The distinction settled unpleasantly inside Nott.

"And Potter?"

The creature raised one white hand and examined the skin as though another body waited beneath it.

"His mother placed something in his blood which has denied me for thirteen years. I will not merely overcome it."

The flames bent towards the chair.

"I will take it into myself."

Bone of the father. Flesh of the servant. Blood of the enemy.

Nott knew the ritual. Every old family kept texts it claimed never to have opened.

His gaze moved towards his own hands.

The creature noticed.

"You wished to prove that Pettigrew was not the measure of those who remained loyal."

"I am not Pettigrew."

"No." The lipless smile returned. "You will have the opportunity to demonstrate the difference."

Nott closed his left hand until the ring cut into his finger.

Outside, rain erased the tracks leading to the kitchen door. It flattened the grass along the drive, filled the ruts beneath the gate and washed the lower path clean. By morning, the hill would appear undisturbed.

Frank Bryce would be found eventually. There would be questions. A doctor would fail to name the cause, and policemen would discover that the dead man had once been accused of three other impossible deaths.

The village would remember the version of him it had always preferred.

Nott looked at the body again.

"When you return," he said, "Dumbledore will prepare for war."

"Of course."

"The Ministry may be more difficult."

His master laughed.

"The Ministry has spent years teaching itself that comfort and truth are the same thing. I see no reason to interrupt the lesson."

"And Lucius?"

"Lucius will discover that changing direction does not erase the road already travelled."

The creature's eyes moved past Nott, towards the storm-darkened window and the country beyond it.

Nott pictured the narrow bedroom in Surrey whose address had appeared in too many failed reports. He knew the blood protection upon it and how many people now watched the road. Perhaps Potter slept. Perhaps the scar had already pulled him towards this room. Nott did not know what the boy dreamed.

He saw only the brief satisfaction upon his master's ruined face.

"What would you have me do first?"

Lord Voldemort turned towards the window.

"Begin."
