5

Harry was trying to stop Sirius stealing his last piece of toast when the Hogwarts owls appeared beyond the kitchen window. It was Friday, the twenty-fifth of August. Until that morning, the approaching end of summer had meant shopping lists, unfinished packing and Sirius finding excuses to put both off. By the time the letters were open, Harry would have another reason to dread it.

Three tawny owls descended upon the garden during breakfast. They came in formation, their wings passing through the warm air with the grave efficiency of birds carrying examination results, court summonses or both. Dobby opened the windows before they struck. One owl delivered Hermione's letter to her plate. Another dropped Draconia's beside the teapot. A third placed Harry's directly upon the toast he had just buttered.

"That one was mine," Harry told the owl, looking at the claw marks in his butter.

The owl stole a slice of bacon.

"Apparently my breakfast is yours as well."

Sirius looked over the top of his newspaper. "Hogwarts tradition."

Around the table, seals broke. The Grangers had come for breakfast to settle arrangements for the following day's shopping; Mrs Granger still had their list beside her cup. Hermione had spent part of the week at home, and the familiar way her mother reached past her for the milk made Harry glad they had been here when the letters arrived.

Hermione opened hers carefully. Draconia used the silver knife. Harry tore through the top because Hogwarts letters had once been the only post capable of finding him anywhere and part of him still trusted their arrival more than their contents. His book list came first.

Fifth-year students required Defensive Magical Theory by Wilbert Slinkhard, Intermediate Transfiguration, A Survey of Sentient Flora and a revised edition of Practical Potions. Beneath the list lay information about the year's OWLs, a reminder concerning Hogsmeade conduct and a pale pink notice explaining that all student organisations would be reviewed during the first month of term. No badge. Harry had not expected one. He decided this before he saw silver flash in Hermione's hand. She stared at the small letter P resting upon her palm.

"Oh. Mum—look at this."

Pride brightened her face, immediate and unguarded. She looked younger when happiness surprised her.

"Hermione," Mrs Granger said from the other end of the table. "Is that what I think it is?"

"I'm a prefect."

Mrs Granger rose so quickly that her chair scraped the floor. Mr Granger stood too. They embraced Hermione from either side while she laughed and attempted to protect her tea.

"We knew you would be," her mother said.

"You said they never explain how they choose."

"I can believe in you and distrust a school at the same time."

"A useful parental skill," Sirius observed.

Harry smiled, and meant it. Then Draconia placed a second badge upon the table. Hers was identical except for the green enamel around its edge. Narcissa touched her daughter's shoulder, quiet pride softening the lines of her face. Lucius inclined his head with the solemnity of a man acknowledging an appointment to the Wizengamot.

"Well earned," he said.

Draconia's fingers rested on the edge of her badge, but she was looking at Harry. He had expected her to hold it up for Narcissa, or make some remark about the school finally recognising good judgement. Instead, she watched him with a small crease between her brows. His smile remained in place.

"Congratulations," he said.

"Thank you," she said, more quietly than he expected.

Sirius turned a page without reading it. Hermione lowered her badge, and Harry wished they would all stop trying so hard to give him a moment.

Harry unfolded his book list again. It had not developed a badge beneath the Hogsmeade notice. He checked the envelope because paper sometimes adhered inside the fold. Nothing. The disappointment embarrassed him by existing.

He did not want rounds, rule enforcement or the responsibility of telling other students to stop doing things he might have attempted himself. Prefects used bathrooms protected by passwords, missed the beginning of the feast to supervise corridors and were expected to recognise every school regulation. Hermione had been preparing for the role since she learnt it existed. Draconia had been born expecting authority to arrive in engraved silver.

Harry only wanted someone to have considered him. He had not known that until he saw how pleased Hermione was, or how quickly she tried to hide it for his sake. That was the worst part: being happy for her and disappointed for himself, and feeling as though one ought to cancel the other. He read the first title upon his list for the third time.

Defensive Magical Theory.

A second envelope slid from behind the books parchment. His name was written upon it in Professor McGonagall's narrow hand. Harry opened it.

Dear Mr Potter,

I consider it important that you hear the following from Hogwarts rather than through rumour. Your name was included among Gryffindor's prefect recommendations. The Special Commissioner declined to confirm the appointment under her newly granted safeguarding authority, citing concerns regarding your recovery, emotional burden and involvement in an ongoing Ministry inquiry.

Professor Dumbledore challenged that decision. The challenge was denied yesterday evening. He and I disagreed upon whether further contest at this stage would serve you or provide the Commissioner's office with additional authority over all prefect appointments.

None of the stated concerns alters my assessment of your courage, judgement or value to your House. I am sorry that another person's description of your welfare has been used to limit a choice which should have been yours.

Yours sincerely,

Minerva McGonagallDeputy Headmistress

Harry reached the end and began again. The kitchen sounds thinned around him. A spoon touched a saucer. Wings beat above the garden. Someone breathed his name and stopped.

"What is it?" Sirius asked.

Harry passed him the letter. Sirius read quickly. The newspaper lowered.

"She did what?"

Hermione came around the table. Harry let her take the parchment when she held out one hand. Draconia read over her shoulder.

"She can veto a prefect?" Hermione said. "That wasn't in the public decree."

"It was buried in the implementation schedule," Lucius replied.

"You knew?"

"I knew the office could review appointments involving students named in active inquiries. I did not know she would apply it before term began."

Draconia picked up the badge she had laid on the table and placed it beside Harry's plate.

"No," Harry said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You were going to resign."

"You don't know that."

"You put it down like it insulted your family."

"Well, it has. I'm not putting it on while you sit there with that letter."

Hermione looked at the silver P still pinned to her dress. Her pride had vanished beneath guilt so quickly that Harry felt worse for noticing it.

"Both of you keep them," he said.

"Harry—"

"If you refuse, Umbridge gets to replace you with people who won't ask why she wants access to student letters."

"It still feels awful to put it on after reading that," Hermione said. "I was so pleased a minute ago, and now—"

"It makes it useful."

Draconia studied him. "You are allowed to be angry."

"I am."

"Even a little at us. You don't have to make this easier just because we're sitting here."

She looked down at the badge as she spoke. Harry could hear the effort in it; Draconia did not usually volunteer to be the person someone was cross with. Harry looked at the two badges.

"I'm not angry that they chose you. I'm angry they let her take it away."

Hermione's face tightened.

"I don't want you to turn them down for me," he continued. "You earned them. I'm allowed to be pleased for you and cross about this at the same time, aren't I?"

Draconia did not retrieve hers.

"Draco."

"Don't say my name like that. You sound like Mother when she knows she's going to win."

"What tone?"

"That very patient one. I'd rather you argued with me."

"Is it working?"

"Annoyingly well."

Harry picked up the badge. He stood and held it towards her. Draconia looked at his open hand, then at him.

"Turn around," he said.

"Why?"

"You ordered me to do it for the jacket."

"That involved tailoring."

"This involves authority. Much more dangerous."

Draconia rose. She did not turn. She stepped close enough that Harry could reach the front of her blouse and lifted her chin.

"Fine."

Harry looked at the pin, then the blouse, then—with growing awareness—at the place the badge was meant to sit. Sirius's newspaper rose until it covered his entire face. Hermione made a sound suspiciously like a cough.

"You could do this yourself," Harry said.

"You were very confident a moment ago."

"The mechanism looked simpler from the table."

"It is a pin, Harry," Hermione said.

"Thank you."

Draconia's mouth curved. Harry fastened the badge carefully against the dark fabric above her heart. His knuckles brushed her collarbone. Draconia's breath caught quietly enough that only someone standing too close would hear. Harry heard. He withdrew his hand.

"There. Terrifying."

"The pin or me?"

"I'm going to say the pin. That seems safer."

Colour touched her cheeks.

Hermione folded McGonagall's letter. "Since Harry is apparently providing installation services—"

She unpinned her own badge and held it out.

Harry stared. "You already had it on."

"Unevenly."

"It was straight."

"I'd like it straight, please."

Draconia narrowed her eyes. Hermione smiled with remarkable innocence. Harry accepted the badge. He could feel every adult in the kitchen pretending not to watch. Mr Granger failed most visibly. Narcissa hid amusement behind her teacup. Lucius looked as though he had encountered a political situation for which no precedent existed. Hermione stepped in front of Harry.

She was slightly shorter than Draconia and made no effort to lift her chin. Her eyes remained upon his while he found the place above her heart. The blue of her dress made the silver brighter.

"Don't stab me," she said.

"Helpful."

"You looked nervous."

"I have survived dragons."

"Dragons don't require fine motor control."

"That explains their handwriting."

Hermione laughed, and the movement nearly drove the pin into Harry's thumb. He secured the badge. His fingers brushed one curl caught beneath it. He freed the strand, and Hermione went very still. For half a second, the crowded kitchen receded. Then Draconia reached between them and adjusted the badge by an amount too small to see.

"Uneven," she said.

"It was not," Harry replied.

"Well, it's straight now. You're welcome."

Hermione looked down, cheeks warm. Sirius lowered the newspaper.

"School authority appears complicated."

"Eat your breakfast," Harry said.

Sirius grinned. The front bell rang. Dobby returned with a small brown owl cupped carefully between his hands. Pigwidgeon kept trying to wriggle free, his wings beating against Dobby's fingers and the letter on his leg flicking from side to side.

"Dobby has caught an unannounced visitor!"

"That's Pig," Harry said. "Ron's owl. He always does that."

"He is wearing no verification token," Dobby said.

"He's an owl," Harry replied.

"Hostile impersonators may be any size."

Pigwidgeon shrieked agreement or outrage. Dobby set him on the back of an empty chair and let Hermione untie the letter. The untidy W pressed into its wax was familiar, though it was not proof of who had sent it. Dobby checked the parchment for concealed enchantments before giving it back; Pig used the delay to hop onto the table and investigate Sirius's plate. The letter contained three sheets written by different Weasleys.

Molly's note asked whether they would meet in Diagon Alley on Saturday and explained that school shopping should be done early because new Ministry-approved texts were already selling out. Ginny added a list of things the twins had attempted to hide beneath the shopping itinerary. Fred and George denied all charges in the margin. Ron's message occupied the final page.

Harry,

I got made prefect. I think there might've been a mistake, but Mum checked the name twice and Fred says Percy would've sent it back for spelling. Hermione probably got it too. Don't let her make a timetable for the train before we arrive. I know that's impossible. Thought I'd ask.

Also, are you trying out for Seeker again? Stupid question. Obviously. Ginny says she's trying for Chaser. I might try Keeper if nobody laughs. You don't have to say anything about that part.

Ron

Harry read the page twice. The first time, resentment arrived before he could stop it. Ron too. Someone in Gryffindor Tower had considered Ron capable of authority while an official who barely knew Harry had turned his nightmares into disqualification. The second time, Harry noticed the line about a mistake.

He remembered Ron at his birthday, predicting the open hoop and watching Harry fall from his broom without pretending it was impressive. He imagined Ron opening the envelope at the Burrow while the twins laughed, Percy existed elsewhere, and Molly compared one son to another through pride she meant kindly. Harry took out a clean sheet of parchment.

Ron,

There wasn't a mistake. You see the whole pitch, which is more than most people with badges manage. Try out for Keeper. If anyone laughs, save their shot and ask them to explain the joke.

Hermione has already made three timetables. I burnt two. Tell her I said that.

Harry

Hermione read over his shoulder.

"You did not burn anything."

"Ron doesn't know that."

"He will when I write to him."

Harry added:

She is threatening correspondence.

He gave the reply to Pigwidgeon, who took off with such enthusiasm that he flew directly into the banner.

"Natural prefect judgement," Sirius said.

The owl escaped through the window trailing the final word of Harry's birthday. Only BIRTH remained above the table.

"Ominous," Remus said from the doorway.

He had been coming downstairs without help for two days and had used the improvement mostly to argue that he no longer required rest. The tracking curse had left a green line along his ribs and weakness in one leg. He walked with a cane he disliked and made jokes whenever anyone noticed. Harry watched him cross the room. Remus noticed that too.

"Still attached," he said before Harry could ask.

"The leg or the cane?" Harry asked.

"The leg. The cane is beginning to feel permanent."

He took his time crossing the last few feet. Harry pulled the chair out without making him ask. He passed over McGonagall's letter. Remus read it standing beside Sirius.

"I see," he said.

"Everyone keeps saying that as though it helps," Harry replied.

"It rarely does."

Remus placed the parchment down. "If you'd been asked, before any of this happened—would you have wanted it?"

The question was more difficult than outrage.

"I don't know."

"That should have been yours to discover."

Harry looked at him.

"McGonagall said that."

"Then she remains irritatingly competent."

"What do I do?"

"About the decision?"

"About it hurting when I didn't even want it."

Remus took the chair beside him carefully.

"You didn't ask to be judged unfit for it. Not wanting the job doesn't make that pleasant."

Harry studied the letter. "So I can be angry without deciding I desperately wanted to patrol corridors?"

"I should hope so."

"I thought I received authority at fifteen."

"Only opinions. Authority is withheld without explanation until at least forty."

Sirius looked offended. "I'm thirty-five."

"Then your case may be permanent."

Harry laughed despite himself.

Draconia touched the prefect badge upon her blouse. "We should know what access these provide before term."

"Prefects' carriage," Hermione said. "Bathrooms, patrol schedules, meetings. We can report infractions—"

Harry looked at her. "You've been doing that for years."

"Yes, but now somebody might actually listen," Hermione said, and then smiled at his expression. "Occasionally."

"A terrifying development."

"And access to student complaints," Draconia added. "If Umbridge intends to control information, she will either use the prefect system or work around it."

Hermione's posture straightened. Harry looked between them.

"Keep the badges," he repeated.

This time, neither argued. Sirius reached across the table and tapped McGonagall's letter.

"And we keep this."

"Evidence?" Harry asked.

"Blackmail, eventually."

"Hermione's changed you."

"I'm so sorry," Hermione said.

Outside, the owls lifted one by one from the garden and turned towards homes across Britain. They carried supply lists, timetables and silver letters which would change who sat where upon the train. Harry looked down at his ordinary envelope.

Harry folded McGonagall's letter along its original crease and put it in his pocket. He would answer it when he could do more than write that he was angry. Across the table, Hermione was already asking Lucius where to find the implementation schedule, while Draconia turned her badge over to check the pin. Neither of them had put it away.

Diagon Alley had learnt how to stare. The first time Harry entered it, shop windows moved, owls called above the crowd and the whole hidden street opened like proof that the world was larger than a cupboard. Since then, people had whispered his name in wonder, suspicion and fear. He had learnt to ignore pointing fingers, photographs taken at poor angles and adults who believed his forehead belonged to public history. This was different. The crowd did not gather. It parted.

Conversations lowered as Harry passed. Faces turned, then turned away when he looked back. Copies of the Daily Prophet protruded from bags and coat pockets with his name visible above folded headlines.

BOY WHO LIVED REQUIRES QUIET RECOVERY, MINISTRY ADVISES.

COMMISSIONER PROMISES SAFE YEAR AT HOGWARTS.

GRAVEYARD INQUIRY FINDS NO EVIDENCE OF WIDER THREAT.

Harry walked between Hermione and Draconia beneath late-summer sunlight. His new jacket was too warm, but he wore it anyway. The concealed holster rested against his forearm. The Hawthorn key lay beneath his shirt. Two emergency Portkeys occupied separate pockets. Sirius walked several paces behind with Remus and the Grangers. Lucius and Narcissa moved ahead, drawing nearly as many glances as Harry.

Tonks wore iron-grey hair and a tired expression Harry had watched her rehearse over breakfast. She was carrying a basket and complaining to anyone who listened about the price of turnips. Kingsley, whose position Sirius had pointed out before they entered the Alley, appeared to have become a shop clerk outside Flourish and Blotts. A third member of the escort pretended to repair a hanging sign. Discreet protection, apparently, required half the street to develop sudden employment.

"Stop counting them," Draconia said.

"I'm not."

"You looked at the same gutter twice."

"Interesting gutter."

Hermione glanced towards the roof. "There is someone behind the western chimney."

"Four," Harry said.

"Possibly five," Draconia replied. "The flower seller keeps looking this way."

"She's seventy."

"An excellent disguise."

The flower seller looked directly at Draconia and tapped two fingers against her basket. Sirius returned the small signal from behind them, then leaned close enough to say, "Yes, she's with us. Try not to stare at her all morning."

"Five before we've even bought a book," Hermione said under her breath. "At this rate we'll need a second shopping list."

Harry sighed. "I'm feeling safer by the person."

They reached Flourish and Blotts. A tower of pink books stood inside the front window. Each bore the Ministry crest and the title A Safe and Responsible Magical Education. Beneath it, a placard announced that the Commissioner's recommendations were available free with every fifth-year purchase. Draconia read the display.

"I would rather pay more."

"We could ask them to remove it from the bag," Harry said.

Hermione's eyes had fixed upon the stack of Defensive Magical Theory beyond the door.

"There are no practical companion volumes," Hermione said, moving closer to the window. "They've put out the whole fifth-year display. Where are the spellbooks?"

"Maybe theory gets lonely," Harry said.

"Harry."

"We'll buy it a friend. Something with spells."

They entered. The shop smelled of parchment, dust and the faint chemical sweetness of new ink. Towers leaned over narrow aisles. Ladders slid by themselves along the shelves. An enchanted globe of the moon turned above the reference section, shedding silver light upon anyone attempting to browse without buying. Students crowded every level. The reaction to Harry moved through them like wind through grass.

Whispers began near the counter. A Ravenclaw girl Harry recognised from fourth year lowered the book in her hands. Two younger Slytherins stared openly until Draconia looked at them, at which point they became fascinated by a dictionary. Harry continued to the Defence table. He picked up Slinkhard's book. It was thin.

"Perhaps it's very dense," he said.

Hermione took it from him and opened to the contents page.

"Chapter One: The Principle of Non-Provocation. Chapter Two: Calm Responses to Magical Conflict. Chapter Three: The Social Sources of Aggressive Spellwork."

"Any spells?"

She turned pages rapidly.

"There are diagrams of wand positions."

"For spells?"

"For safe storage."

Draconia opened another copy at random. "Page one hundred and six advises students confronted by a hostile creature to withdraw and notify a qualified authority."

Harry looked at the nearest shelf. "Does it explain where to find one?"

"Appendix B."

Hermione reached it. "Contact information for the Department for the Regulation and Control of Magical Creatures. Office hours nine until four, excluding Fridays."

"Excellent. Basilisks are known for respecting office hours."

A boy behind them laughed. His friend elbowed him sharply and looked towards the front counter. Harry saw the fear beneath the reaction. Not fear of him, exactly. Fear of being seen agreeing. The new school year had not begun, and Umbridge was already inside the room. Harry closed the book.

"We need something else."

"We already own something else," Hermione said. "Several shelves of something else."

"At Hawthorn."

Draconia placed Slinkhard's book into her basket. "The Hogwarts library contains more."

"Assuming she doesn't restrict it."

Hermione looked towards the rows of students collecting the same useless text.

"Then we make a list before term."

It was not yet a plan for an army. It was only three students in a bookshop refusing to accept that theory counted as defence. But Harry felt the first edge of something larger.

They bought the book because refusing would give Umbridge an excuse before she required one. Hermione carried it as though transporting contaminated material. Draconia asked the shop assistant whether copies could be returned after proving educationally useless. The assistant looked towards a Ministry notice beside the till and explained that approved texts were non-refundable.

"Of course," Draconia said. "They're quite happy to sell it to us. They just don't want to hear whether it's any use."

Harry paid for three supplemental Defence books from a shelf at the back. One contained counter-curses. One covered group shielding. The third was a practical guide to evacuation written by a witch who had survived the Grindelwald conflict and mistrusted buildings with only one staircase. Hermione approved of all three, which Harry suspected meant none would be entertaining. They were leaving the shop when a camera flashed.

White light filled Harry's vision. His hand moved to the holster. Draconia stepped between him and the source while Hermione's wand came up beneath the books. A young wizard in bottle-green robes lowered a camera.

"Mr Potter," he said. "Crispin Vale, Daily Prophet. Could I ask how you're responding to the Commissioner's new safeguards?"

Tonks appeared near the doorway, turnips tucked beneath one arm and her free hand inside her coat. She looked at Harry before turning towards the photographer. Harry's heartbeat slowed.

"You could," he said.

Vale waited.

"Well?"

"You asked whether you could."

A few nearby students laughed.

The reporter's smile remained fixed. "Do you feel the Ministry has taken appropriate steps to support your recovery?"

"They took away a badge I didn't know I wanted and sent me a book without spells. Recovery has been thrilling."

Hermione shifted beside him. A warning or encouragement; with her it could be both.

Vale raised his quill. "So you object to the appointment of Commissioner Umbridge?"

"I object to people calling control support because they think children won't know the difference."

The laughter stopped. The reporter's quill moved.

"And do you accept the inquiry's finding that no wider threat has been established?"

Harry looked at the headline visible through the shop window. No evidence of wider threat. The Ministry had recorded Voldemort's name, then constructed every sentence around it until truth became a dangerous object no one had to touch.

"The inquiry found credible evidence that Tom Riddle returned," Harry said. "Your paper printed the words around that finding. Perhaps you should try printing the words themselves."

Vale's smile weakened. Another flash came from the side. Draconia stepped beside Harry, blocking the photographer's next clear shot.

"He's answered you," she said. "Could you stop flashing that in his face?"

Tonks moved between them and the camera before the photographer could try another angle.

"Miss Malfoy, is it true your father provided false testimony to avoid prosecution for his own Dark associations?"

Draconia's hand tightened around the handle of her shopping bag. Harry saw her draw a breath, ready to answer for her father again. Harry stepped beside her.

"No," he said.

Vale turned eagerly. "You defend Mr Malfoy?"

"I was there. Were you?"

"The public has a right to ask—"

"Then the public can ask him. You're pointing the camera at his daughter."

The reporter opened his mouth. Lucius answered from behind him.

"I am available."

Vale went pale. Lucius stood upon the pavement with Narcissa on one arm and Sirius upon his other side. They presented three entirely different forms of danger. Lucius smiled without warmth.

"You wished to discuss my associations."

"Mr Malfoy, I—"

"My daughter is not here to answer for me," Lucius said. "Neither is Mr Potter. If you have a question about my testimony, ask me—and quote my answer in full."

Vale's quill trembled.

Sirius looked at Harry. "You all right?"

"He startled me."

"Want me to eat the camera?"

"Not in that form."

"Coward."

Harry smiled. The crowd had widened around them. People watched Lucius face the reporter, watched Harry remain beside Draconia and watched Sirius Black behave in public like a guardian rather than a headline. Harry wondered how many different versions of the exchange the crowd would carry away.

Hermione touched Harry's elbow. "We should go."

He allowed her to guide him away from the shop doorway. Draconia did not move. She looked at Lucius. Harry had heard Draconia defend her father's name often enough to recognise the pause. This time Lucius had come forward himself. She looked from the reporter to him, her hand still resting upon the lowered camera. Lucius met her eyes.

"Go," he said quietly. "I began this question long before today."

Draconia released the camera. She joined Harry without thanking him for his defence. He did not need her to. Her hand brushed his as they entered the street, then returned and caught two of his fingers deliberately. They walked that way until the crowd thinned.

The Weasleys occupied Quality Quidditch Supplies with the territorial certainty of a minor invasion. Molly stood at the counter arguing about the durability of school gloves. Arthur had discovered a Muggle bicycle bell among the racing accessories and was asking why broom manufacturers had never considered warning pedestrians. Ginny tested the grip upon a new Chaser's glove by throwing a practice Quaffle at Fred's head. He ducked. The Quaffle struck George.

"Excellent grip," George said from the floor.

Ron stood near the Keeper display wearing his prefect badge beneath a jumper which almost concealed it. Harry noticed before Ron could hide it completely.

"Congratulations."

Ron looked down. "Mum made me wear it."

"Terrible. People might recognise your authority."

"Fred bowed for an hour."

"Respectful."

"He called me Percival."

"Less respectful."

Ron smiled. His gaze found the place upon Harry's chest where no badge sat and moved away quickly.

"I heard about Umbridge," he said.

Harry glanced towards Hermione. She was helping Ginny compare gloves. Draconia had joined Narcissa beside the broom servicing counter.

"News travels."

"Mum said McGonagall wrote to Dumbledore. Dad swore about the Commissioner and then pretended he'd dropped something when Mum came in."

"Was it convincing?"

"He spent five minutes under the table."

Harry laughed.

Ron rubbed the back of his neck. "I'd give you mine, but Mum would probably sew it to me."

"You earned it."

"Did I?"

"You kept your head at the party better than I did," Harry said. "That's a start. Though I'd read the instructions before you start taking points."

Ron looked at him, startled. Harry grinned.

"That was a joke."

"Right. I knew that."

"Natural leadership."

Ron shoved his shoulder lightly. Harry had not expected it to feel so easy. Ron looked briefly surprised too, then shoved his hands into his pockets.

Harry turned towards the Keeper display before the moment required acknowledgement. Three pairs of gloves hovered above a wooden stand, opening and closing their fingers. One was padded heavily enough to make the wearer resemble a dragon handler. Another promised improved lateral reach through a charm the manufacturer refused to explain.

"Which ones?" Harry asked.

"I'm only looking."

"You said you're trying out."

"I said I might."

"You wrote it down. Legally binding."

Ron picked up the least expensive pair. The leather was scuffed where several students had tried them.

"They're all rubbish," he said.

"Those aren't."

"How do you know?"

"Charlie recommended that sort. In your book—the bit in green ink beside the glove diagram."

Ron looked at the label. "You read that?"

"You gave it to me. Of course I read it."

The fact that Harry had read the gift seemed to unsettle him more than praise.

"Oh. Yeah. He said the padding's better than the expensive ones."

"Try them."

Ron glanced towards Fred and George. The twins were demonstrating an enchanted whistle to Arthur. Neither appeared to be watching. Both were. Harry took a practice Quaffle from the rack. His shoulder no longer caught when he lifted his arm; Pomfrey had cleared the strain earlier that week, with a pointed warning that this did not amount to permission for another mid-air rescue.

"Put them on."

"In the shop?"

"We can ask permission, if you want your prefect career to begin responsibly."

Ron scowled and pulled on the gloves. They went to the testing alcove at the back, where a single hoop hung above a padded wall. Harry stood six paces away. Ron planted himself before the hoop.

"Ready?"

"Hang on." Ron flexed his fingers, tugged one glove tighter and settled in front of the hoop. "All right. Just don't aim at my face."

Harry threw towards his right shoulder. Ron caught the Quaffle one-handed and stared at it for a second before tossing it back.

"Not bad," Harry said. "Want a harder one?"

"I'm standing here, aren't I?"

"Could be admiring the hoop."

"Throw the ball, Potter."

Harry threw left. Ron moved before the ball left his hand and knocked it aside. Harry aimed high, low and once towards the floor to see whether Ron would expect the bounce. Ron did.

By the seventh attempt, Ginny had joined them. By the tenth, Fred and George stopped pretending not to watch. Hermione counted saves. Draconia leaned against the alcove entrance beside Narcissa, arms folded but attention fixed. Harry feinted right and threw left. Ron moved right. The Quaffle passed him.

"Finally," Harry said.

Ron turned. "You cheated."

"I moved my shoulder."

"Dishonestly."

"Seekers are allowed shoulders."

"Not like that."

"Keeper's hoop counts as part of you, but my shoulder is restricted?"

Ginny laughed. Ron retrieved the Quaffle. His hair had fallen across his forehead and colour warmed his face. He looked annoyed, competitive and happier than when Harry entered.

"Again," he said.

Harry raised an eyebrow. "Thought you were only looking."

Ron threw the Quaffle at him. Harry caught it.

"Go on, then," Ron said, squaring himself in front of the hoop again. "Same side. I want to try that one properly."

They continued until Molly announced that gloves which had survived Harry Potter's testing were likely durable enough and bought them despite Ron's protests about the price. Fred waited until she reached the till.

"Our little prefect has found a vocation," he said.

Ron went red.

"Shut up."

George looked at Harry. "Did he save more than five?"

"Fourteen out of seventeen."

The twins stopped smiling. Not because the number was poor. Because it was not.

"Harry cheated."

"Everyone cheats," George said. "Improve the left."

It was not ridicule. From Fred and George, that made it nearly an embrace. Ron looked down at the gloves.

"Yeah," he said. "All right."

Harry tossed him the Quaffle once more. Ron caught it without looking.

Cedric found Harry outside the apothecary. He wore ordinary jeans and a faded Hufflepuff jumper despite the heat. The sleeves had been pushed to his elbows, revealing the white line around one wrist where the graveyard ropes had burned through skin. Most people did not notice it before recognising his face. Harry always did.

"Birthday boy," Cedric said.

"That was weeks ago."

"At our age, the title lasts a month."

"What happens after eighteen?"

"People stop caring. It's very sudden."

They embraced briefly. Amos and Mrs Diggory stood outside the cauldron shop speaking with Sirius. Amos gestured with both hands. Sirius appeared to be enjoying whatever story required that much space. Cedric looked at the bags Harry carried.

"Slinkhard?"

"Unfortunately."

"I've read it."

"Why?"

"Ministry sent me a copy in July. Said it might support a safe return to education."

"Did it?"

"I fell asleep. Safest I've felt in months."

Harry laughed. The word return settled a moment later.

"You're going back."

Cedric nodded.

"Seventh year," Cedric said. "NEWTs in June, if all goes well. Sprout's helping me catch up on what I missed while I was training for the Tournament, and she's asked whether I'd help with the younger students once I'm settled."

"Does the Ministry know?"

"They objected."

"That sounds promising."

"Dad sent seventeen letters."

"Did they object to the letters too?"

"After number nine."

Harry smiled, then studied Cedric more carefully. The confidence remained, but Harry recognised the effort of keeping it in place. Cedric's shoulders tightened whenever someone approached too quickly. His attention moved to reflections before doorways.

"Do you want to go back?" Harry asked.

Cedric's smile faded.

"Some mornings I can't wait. Others I get as far as looking at my trunk and decide Mum might have a point." He gave a small, embarrassed shrug. "Today's a good morning."

Harry shifted his bags into his other hand. It was easier to stand beside Cedric when neither of them was pretending the answer had to be simple.

"Mum thinks I should wait. Dad thinks going back proves something. I think they're both trying to make the decision mean enough that it can't frighten them."

"What do you think?"

Cedric looked down the Alley. School robes passed between shops. Owls called from the emporium. A group of Hufflepuffs near the ice-cream parlour saw him and waved uncertainly.

"I miss it," Cedric said. "Not the fuss, or having people ask whether I'm all right every time I sit down. Just school. My friends, the pitch, even the greenhouse smell." He paused. "If I wait until none of it frightens me, I might be waiting a long time."

Harry understood.

"You don't have to prove anything."

"Neither do you."

"That has never stopped anyone."

"Exactly."

Cedric touched the scar around his wrist. "I want to finish. Not the Tournament. School. Something ordinary enough to have an ending."

Harry looked towards the Hufflepuffs. One of them held a yellow-and-black scarf despite summer.

"Sit with us on the train."

"The trio allows visitors?"

"There's an application process. Hermione has forms."

"References?"

"You survived me once. Strong qualification."

Cedric smiled. "I'll consider it."

"Girls have prefect meetings at the start anyway. I'll be abandoned."

"Tragic."

"Umbridge agrees."

Cedric's expression hardened. "I heard what you said to the reporter."

"I wasn't being especially quiet."

"Perhaps that's a good thing."

Harry frowned.

"Not what she did," Cedric said quickly. "I mean people hearing about it. If nobody knows, they can keep saying it's all for your benefit, and you're the one left wondering whether you're making a fuss."

Harry glanced at him.

"You've been speaking to Hermione."

"She sends reading material."

"Against your will?"

"Is there another kind?"

Harry laughed again. Across the street, the young Prophet reporter had finished with Lucius. His photographer lifted the camera towards Harry and Cedric. Cedric saw him. He stepped beside Harry rather than away. The camera flashed. Neither boy moved.

Harry could already imagine the photograph beneath a headline which made their survival easier to dismiss. The flash caught him blinking, but he kept his place beside Cedric. Whatever they printed above it, the picture would show them standing together. Sometimes evidence required no statement at all.

Madam Malkin's had not changed. The same purple sign swung above the door. The same bell gave a tired chime when Harry entered. Robes hung from brass rails in orderly black rows, and three footstools waited before long mirrors which made customers visible from angles nobody required. Harry stopped inside. Memory arrived without pain.

Eleven years old, drowning in Dudley's shirt. Hagrid somewhere beyond the window. A pale girl upon the neighbouring stool correcting a sleeve, then asking why Harry had apologised for smiling. Draconia entered behind him. She followed his gaze towards the stools.

"Oh," she said.

"You remember."

"I have an excellent memory."

"You called the Dursleys incompetent within five minutes."

"I was correct within three."

Madam Malkin emerged from the back room carrying a mouthful of pins. She removed them when she saw Harry.

"Hogwarts robes? Both of you?"

"Please," Draconia said.

"Up you get, then. You've both grown."

Harry climbed onto the left stool. Draconia chose the right. From there, the mirrors held both their reflections. Madam Malkin dropped robes over their heads and began pinning hems. The cloth settled around Harry, smelling of starch and the faint lavender used throughout the shop. His reflection appeared beside Draconia's in every mirror.

She had grown too. At eleven, her features had been fine and severe because she believed stillness made her older. She still held herself carefully, but her expression changed more readily now; Harry could recognise a smile before she allowed herself to show it. Her hair fell past her shoulders. The green prefect badge gleamed upon the blouse beneath her open fitting robe. Harry looked away before she caught him.

"You stared then as well," Draconia said.

He looked back. "I did not."

"You kept looking away whenever I looked back."

"You asked why I was apologising. I was trying not to give you another reason."

"That was not an improvement."

"I was eleven."

"So was I," she said, though a smile had begun at the corner of her mouth.

Madam Malkin moved between them, adjusting Harry's sleeve.

"Hold still, dear."

Harry raised his arms.

Draconia watched him in the mirror. "You were smaller."

"I was eleven."

"Your shirt looked as though it belonged to someone twice your size."

"Dudley's old things."

Her expression changed. Harry remembered her first bewildered look at Dudley's sleeve. Before second year, she had stood inside number four and seen the bars, the locked bedroom and the flap through which his meals arrived. She looked at his reflection now, and he wondered which of those things she was seeing.

"I should have told someone," she said.

Harry lowered his arms.

"You did. You told me. Repeatedly."

"I should have told an adult, Harry. Someone who could actually do something."

"You met me for five minutes."

"That has never limited my judgement."

"Clearly."

Madam Malkin moved to the back room for another size. The shop quietened around them. Outside the window, Diagon Alley passed in colour and noise. Inside, mirrors multiplied Harry and Draconia into a crowd of possible versions: the children they had been, the people they were becoming, every angle revealing a distance which looked smaller than it felt.

"Did you know who I was?" Harry asked.

Draconia hesitated, her eyes meeting his in the mirror before moving away.

"Not immediately," she said. "I thought you were lost."

"I was in a robe shop."

"You looked as though someone had placed you in the world without instructions."

Harry glanced at his reflection.

"Then I told you my name."

"Yes. Madam Malkin dropped the pins."

Harry smiled at the memory. "I thought you would start asking about the scar."

"You'd already said you didn't remember."

"Everyone else wanted to know anyway."

"That did not make it a better question."

He remembered her saying that forgetting might be for the best, then returning from the changing curtain to explain the Houses. She had been curious about his name. She had also managed to stay interested in the boy wearing it.

"Why did you say you'd see me at King's Cross?" he asked.

Her gaze shifted from the mirror to him.

"Because you looked relieved when I said your relatives were incompetent."

"That's all?"

"And I liked you."

Harry's heart stumbled. Draconia looked ahead again, colour reaching her cheeks slowly.

"You were strange," she added.

"Better."

"Your hair was worse."

"There she is."

"And you had no standards."

"I became your friend. That's established."

She smiled. Madam Malkin returned carrying new robes and an apology about the delay. Harry's sleeve had to be shortened. Draconia's collar required opening by half an inch. Tape measures flew around them, taking dimensions and muttering numbers. When Madam Malkin knelt to pin Harry's hem, Draconia reached across the space between stools. She straightened the collar of his robe.

The gesture had become hers over the years. On platforms. Before hearings. In corridors after Harry slept badly enough to button shirts incorrectly. Her fingers moved with familiar precision beneath his chin. Harry looked down at her.

"It wasn't crooked."

"It was beginning to consider it."

"Dangerous."

"I intervened early."

Her hand remained upon the collar. They were closer than the stools required.

"I'm glad you spoke to me," Harry said.

Draconia's eyes lifted. The shop mirrors held the moment from every side.

"You could try remembering that when I'm giving you advice."

"That almost sounded nervous."

"Don't get used to it," she said, looking down at his collar again.

Harry covered her hand with his.

"I'm still glad."

Draconia's thumb moved once against the fabric.

"So am I."

The bell above the door chimed. Hermione entered carrying enough books to compromise balance. She saw their joined hands in the mirror. One eyebrow rose.

"Am I interrupting tailoring?"

Draconia withdrew first. "Harry's collar was crooked."

"Again?"

"It is a persistent problem."

Hermione placed the books upon a chair. "Madam Malkin, he needs dress robes as well. His old sleeves are above his wrists."

Harry turned. "Why do you know that?"

"Because I let down the sleeves for your hearing," Hermione said. "There isn't any seam left to let out next time."

"I thought you'd fixed them."

"I did. I can repair robes, Harry; I can't persuade you to stop growing."

Draconia folded her arms. "She was monitoring your condition."

Hermione's cheeks warmed.

Harry looked between them. "This phrase is becoming very useful."

"Keep using it as an excuse," Hermione said, "and I'll stop fixing your sleeves."

"You wouldn't."

"I would," Draconia said. "I'd quite like to watch you argue with Madam Malkin in robes that stop at your elbows."

Madam Malkin looked from one to the other and returned silently to her pins. The mirrors, Harry thought, had far too many angles.

The Daily Prophet printed Harry's answer the following morning. It removed Tom Riddle's name. The article appeared on page six beneath a photograph of Harry and Cedric standing outside the apothecary. The image showed Cedric shifting closer as the camera flashed. Harry looked thin, guarded and older in the black jacket. Behind them, a witch buying nettles had turned to stare. The headline read:

POTTER QUESTIONS MINISTRY SUPPORT AS RECOVERY CONTINUES

The reporter described Harry as defiant, emotionally charged and still resistant to the inquiry's reassuring conclusion. Lucius's answer occupied two paragraphs, both edited until his admission of prior complicity sounded like a complaint about persecution. Draconia appeared only as the controversial former associate's daughter. Cedric's presence was not mentioned.

"They've used Cedric's photograph without even telling people who he is," Hermione said. "As if he's someone who happened to be standing there."

She sat at the library table with scissors, three copies of the article and an expression normally reserved for equations which refused to balance.

"Maybe he obstructed the composition," Harry suggested.

"He is half the photograph."

"Very inconsiderate."

Draconia cut out the inquiry's actual finding from an earlier transcript. "Put this beneath it."

Hermione arranged the pieces upon a clean sheet. Harry watched her reconstruct what the newspaper had separated: question beside answer, photograph beside named witness, Ministry conclusion beside the evidence it qualified.

"What are you making?" he asked.

"A correction. A proper one, with what you actually said next to their version."

"Where will you print it?"

Hermione looked up at him, then back at the sheet. A correction sent to the Prophet could be ignored, or cut down until it was no more useful than the original story. They had managed to get transcripts to some families, but Harry knew how many people would read the headline over breakfast and never see anything else.

"I don't know yet," she admitted. "But I want it ready when we find somewhere."

She placed the corrected page inside a folder. Harry thought of the frightened boy in Flourish and Blotts laughing at his joke before remembering someone might see. Schools contained their own presses: gossip, House tables, noticeboards and letters sent home. Umbridge knew that. Her powers over communication were not an afterthought.

"Take it to Hogwarts," he said.

Draconia's eyes sharpened. "The transcript?"

"Copies. Not to hand out yet. Just so we have them."

"Where?" Hermione asked.

Harry thought of the Marauder's Map, secret passages and all the unused classrooms they had passed without opening.

"Somewhere she doesn't know exists."

Hermione slowly closed the folder.

"We'll need concealment charms."

"And duplicates," Draconia said. "One hidden in each House would be safer."

"We only have access to two."

"Three if Luna helps."

Harry added, "Cedric for Hufflepuff."

The structure formed with alarming ease. Four Houses. Four copies, carried by people they trusted into separate parts of the school. Hermione took out another sheet and began listing who they would need to ask. Draconia disputed her choice of hiding place before she had finished the first line. Harry pulled a chair between them and started making copies; for the moment, it was something they could actually do.

Remus's final summer lesson began after dusk. He chose the rear lawn rather than the orchard because his injured side made uneven ground difficult. A wooden chair waited beneath the beech tree, though he remained standing until Sirius threatened to summon Madam Pomfrey by name. Remus sat with dignity and called Sirius oppressive.

"Healing environment," Sirius said.

"Tyranny with cushions."

"You're welcome."

The air smelled of dry grass and the smoke from a small garden fire. Beyond the boundary, fields darkened into long bands. Hawthorn's windows glowed behind them. Bats moved above the roof in abrupt, silent turns. Harry, Hermione and Draconia stood in a loose triangle upon the lawn.

"You can all produce corporeal Patronuses," Remus said. "Tonight we'll try giving them a message. Don't worry if the first few attempts arrive with nothing to say. It took me longer than I care to admit."

Hermione lifted her wand. "Patronus messages cannot be intercepted in the same way as owls."

"They're harder to interfere with than letters," Remus replied. "But somebody can still overhear a message, and you mustn't assume silver light means a familiar caster. Use the checks we agreed. A Patronus gives you another way to reach us; it doesn't replace the other precautions."

"Can Voldemort cast one?" Harry asked.

The name crossed the lawn without weakening.

Remus considered. "I don't know. A Patronus requires a form of positive focus he may be unable or unwilling to access. That does not mean no one serving him can produce one."

"So being unpleasant doesn't rule someone out?" Draconia asked.

Remus's mouth twitched. "It would make correspondence difficult if it did."

"Father will be relieved," she said. From the garden doorway, Lucius made a small noise of protest that nobody took seriously.

"The difficulty," Remus continued, "is maintaining the Patronus while giving it language. The memory summons it. Intention sends it. A message gives the intention exact shape. If you concentrate only upon the person, your Patronus may find them and arrive with nothing to say."

"Awkward," Harry said.

"Especially during an emergency."

"Can you send one accidentally?"

"Once we'd learned the message charm, Sirius sent James a Patronus which stared at him for twenty minutes."

Sirius folded his arms. "It communicated disapproval."

"It was a dog licking itself."

"James understood."

Harry laughed. Remus demonstrated first. He raised his wand. Silver light gathered at the tip, brightened and leapt into the grass as a wolf. It stood lean and alert, its translucent coat moving in a wind the evening did not possess.

"Sirius," Remus said, "please stop repairing my chair with Sticking Charms."

The wolf bounded across the lawn. It reached Sirius, sat and repeated the sentence in Remus's voice.

Sirius crouched before it. "Tell Moony the chair has never been more stable."

The Patronus looked at him.

"It doesn't return," Remus said.

"Poor design."

The wolf dissolved into mist. Hermione went first. She closed her eyes and summoned the memory. Harry did not know which she chose. Perhaps the lake in third year after everything came free. Perhaps a library afternoon or the moment her parents entered Hawthorn and found her safe. Silver light spun from her wand, gathered low to the earth and became an otter. It rolled once through the grass before rising upon its hind legs.

"Harry," Hermione said, concentrating, "the second copy is beneath the first stair."

The otter raced towards him. It circled his feet, stood upright and spoke in Hermione's voice.

"The second copy is beneath the first stair."

Harry smiled. "Why?"

"Because no one checks beneath the obvious step when there are twelve less obvious ones."

"That wasn't part of the message."

"Then the charm worked."

The otter vanished. Draconia lifted her wand. Her Patronus had become steadier since their first lessons with Remus. In third year, the magic had struggled into the shape of a small serpent no longer than her forearm. Now it held its form without the flickering gaps Harry remembered from practice. The creature which emerged now was as long as Draconia was tall, its scales made from overlapping light and its head lifted above the grass. It did not resemble the basilisk.

Harry had noticed the difference during practice: the narrow head and lithe, bright body, nothing like the creature beneath the school. He had nearly asked Draconia about it, then thought better of making her explain something that seemed to make her happy. The serpent coiled around her feet.

"Hermione," Draconia said, "Harry is about to do something inadvisable."

The Patronus moved. It flowed across the lawn like water, climbed the air before Hermione and delivered the message.

Hermione looked at Harry. "What are you about to do?"

"Nothing! I haven't even had my turn yet."

"Then the message is inaccurate," Remus said, though he was having difficulty keeping a straight face.

"Give him a minute," Draconia said. "It'll probably become accurate."

Harry raised his wand. "I feel supported."

He searched for a memory. The first which came was the roof upon his birthday: Hermione's hand beneath the blanket, Draconia's head upon his shoulder and Sirius arguing through the window below. It was recent enough to remain bright. Harry held it.

"Expecto Patronum."

Silver erupted from his wand. The stag landed heavily upon the lawn, antlers raised. Light travelled across its body in waves. Harry felt the spell connect to memory and then beyond it, searching for direction. He looked at Sirius.

"I'm coming home for Christmas."

The stag ran. It crossed the grass in three bounds and stopped before Sirius. Its mouth opened. Nothing emerged. The Patronus lowered its head.

Sirius touched one silver antler. "I got the impression."

Harry let the charm dissolve.

"Again," Remus said.

On the second attempt, the stag carried only the word Christmas. On the third, it informed Sirius, in Harry's voice, that Christmas was coming home.

"Threatening," Sirius said.

"Accurate," Draconia replied.

Harry tried a fourth time. The spell resisted. Harry could still picture the roof, but each time he reached for the warmth of the memory, he found himself thinking about how easily he could lose the people in it. Each failed attempt made the original warmth harder to reach. Remus saw the change.

"Choose something ordinary," he said.

"I've always needed something stronger than that."

"For a Dementor, perhaps. Tonight you're standing in your own garden, sending a message to someone ten feet away. Give yourself a smaller task."

Harry thought of toast crossing the table at Privet Drive under the magical agreement. Too close to misery. Sirius's birthday cake. Too close to the dream which preceded it. Flying. Cedric falling. The girls laughing upon the roof. Voldemort waiting beyond the year. Every happy memory had developed an edge.

"Harry," Sirius said.

He had moved closer.

"The motorbike chain," he said. "Tuesday."

Harry remembered. Sirius had shown him how to reset the links without magic. They sat upon the workshop floor with grease across their hands while the wireless played something loud and terrible. Nothing happened. No one arrived with news. Harry held the torch and Sirius swore at a bolt for twenty minutes. Ordinary. Harry raised his wand. The stag appeared.

"Sirius," Harry said, "I'm coming home for Christmas."

It crossed the lawn. This time, Harry's voice emerged clearly. Sirius smiled.

"Good," Sirius said, and then, because his voice had gone a little rough, "I'll warn Dobby. He'll need at least three months to plan lunch."

Harry lowered his wand, smiling. Remus told him to try once more while the memory was still easy to reach, and this time Harry did not dread losing it between one attempt and the next.

Packing began three days before term and became an argument about what counted as necessary. Hermione considered every assigned book necessary. Draconia considered three-quarters of her wardrobe necessary. Harry thought the socks already in his trunk would do until Dobby discovered that most of them were unmatched and took control of the packing.

"Harry Potter has fourteen pairs," Dobby announced.

"I only have two feet."

"Dobby has counted."

"Good. I was concerned."

Winky stood inside Draconia's open trunk compressing robes into one impossible corner. Hermione labelled separate folders for OWL subjects. Sirius moved between rooms contributing items no one had requested: a packet of emergency signal flares, a sheathed utility knife, a Muggle torch and a sealed bottle whose label began FOR AN UNCONSCIOUS PATIENT.

Hermione held it up. "Unconscious people can't drink."

"Which is why the next line says 'for a qualified healer to administer'."

She turned the bottle. Sirius pointed out the dosage sheet fixed beneath the seal.

"What is it?"

"The stabilising draught Pomfrey sent. You don't open it yourself."

Hermione put it in the medical pouch, then caught the utility knife before Sirius could add it to a sock.

"It has a sheath," he said.

"It also has a pocket. Use that."

Harry sat upon the bedroom floor amid folded jumpers, broom-servicing equipment and stacks of books. The room had changed over the summer. Photographs moved upon the mantel. Neville's weather plant occupied the windowsill, its leaves now blue in anticipation of rain. Hermione's birthday journal rested beside Remus's Defence manuscript. His Gryffindor scarf hung from the bedpost. The old curtains no longer smelled of storage. It looked inhabited. His Hogwarts trunk waited open.

Harry had learnt to pack at Privet Drive, counting the hours until he could leave and putting every object he valued inside the trunk. Later, even during the summers when the Malfoys or Sirius had given him somewhere safe to stay, the habit had persisted. Leaving something behind required a kind of trust that packing it did not. Now each thing he packed removed evidence from the room. He picked up the birthday journal.

Hermione looked over from the desk. "Take that one," she said. "Unless you'd rather leave it here. I hoped you'd use it at school too."

"Umbridge can inspect student belongings."

"She's supposed to have a reason," Hermione said. Then she saw his expression and sighed. "I know. That isn't much reassurance."

"She writes the cause."

The words belonged to Lucius. Hermione recognised them.

"I can place a concealment charm upon it."

"What if she breaks it?"

"Then she'll find entries concerning Sirius's cake and your inability to identify straight badges."

"There might be other things later."

"Then we change the charm, or send it home," Hermione said. "You don't have to decide what you'll write in December before you've packed for September."

Harry turned the journal over.

"I could leave it here."

Hermione set down her labels.

"Do you want to?"

He looked around the room. Leaving the book felt safer. Taking it felt like assuming Hogwarts still had good memories to offer.

"No," he admitted.

Hermione held out her hand. Harry passed it to her. She drew her wand and began layering spells over the cover: concealment, misdirection and a small charm which made the book appear to contain Arithmancy exercises when opened by anyone other than Harry. With a tap of her wand, she showed him the disguised pages so they could test the effect.

"Cruel," Harry said when rows of equations appeared. "I might never want to open it again."

"You'll still see your own writing," Hermione told him. "I'm trying to discourage everyone else."

Draconia entered carrying Harry's new school robes.

She watched the equations move across the page. "Too interesting," Draconia said. "Someone might borrow it. Make it History of Magic."

Hermione looked offended for half a second, then glanced at Harry. "All right. But only because the point is to make people put it down."

The numbers became a detailed account of seventeenth-century cauldron taxation. Harry yawned before reading the second line.

"Perfect."

Draconia hung the robes inside his wardrobe instead of packing them. Harry noticed.

"Those are for school."

"You have three other sets."

"Madam Malkin made those to fit."

"Which means they will still fit at Christmas."

The word settled gently. Hermione ended the demonstration, checked that Harry could see his drawing of the birthday cake again, and placed the enchanted journal inside the trunk. Draconia smoothed the hanging sleeve.

"Your room should contain clothes," she said. "Otherwise it looks temporary."

Harry looked at her.

"Is your room temporary?"

Draconia's hand stopped. Hawthorn's bedrooms had never quite kept pace with the people staying in them. Draconia had given Remus his old room back while he recovered, and Dobby's extra bed remained in Hermione's temporary room. Her correspondence box and silver hair clasps now shared a shelf with Hermione's books. Downstairs, her parents' things had found places too: Narcissa's sewing basket beside a chair, Lucius's papers in the study, Winky's carefully folded linen in the cupboard. What Lucius had once called an arrangement had begun to look like people living there.

"The Ministry is still examining the Manor," she said. "And the people who want Father dead know it better than we'd like. We're safer here."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Hermione became busy with the trunk straps. Draconia looked towards the window.

"My room is here," she said at last.

It was not the same as calling Hawthorn home. It was close enough to make Harry smile.

"Then leave clothes in it."

"I intend to."

"More than three-quarters of the wardrobe?"

"Do not be absurd."

"Winky packed twelve green jumpers."

"They are different greens."

Hermione tightened a strap. "I counted five."

"You counted badly," Draconia said. "And you're not borrowing the soft one again if you keep calling it the same as all the others."

Harry laughed. They left his new robes hanging. He settled the enchanted journal between two jumpers, then deliberately returned one Defence book to the bedside table. His Gryffindor scarf remained upon the bedpost. The weather plant kept the window. When they finished, the room looked emptier. It did not look abandoned.

Sirius found Harry awake at two in the morning on the thirty-first of August. Harry sat against the headboard with the bedside lamp burning and Remus's compass in his hand. The home needle pointed down and slightly left, towards Hawthorn's heartstone beneath the kitchen. He had been watching it for nearly an hour. Sirius knocked upon the open door.

"Nightmare?"

"No. I just can't get to sleep."

"Anything in particular keeping you up?"

Harry turned the compass between his fingers. "You want the whole list?"

"Only if it isn't alphabetised. Hermione's had enough of an influence."

"You're being a bit dramatic."

"You're sitting in the dark holding a magical compass."

Harry glanced at the lamp. "Not dark."

"Then a poorly staged existential crisis."

Sirius entered and sat upon the end of the bed. He wore loose trousers and an old shirt printed with a Muggle band Harry had never heard of. His hair was tied badly behind his head.

"Worried about the train?" he asked.

Harry turned the compass. The needles continued pointing towards the familiar places marked for their different purposes. Turning the casing changed nothing; the little charm did not care which way he faced.

"A bit."

"Umbridge?"

"A lot."

"Leaving?"

Harry looked up. Sirius knew him too quickly sometimes. It was pleasant and frightening in equal measure.

"Hogwarts is Hogwarts," Harry said.

"Insightful."

"You know what I mean."

"I do."

Sirius waited. Harry rested the compass upon his knee.

"I used to think going back was the thing that made summer bearable. Even when something was trying to kill me."

"High standards."

"Privet Drive helped."

The joke did not survive long. Harry looked at the hanging robes, the photographs and the book left beside the lamp.

"I know I left here last year," he said. "And the Manor before that. I was glad to be there. I just kept packing as though I wouldn't get any of it back."

Sirius looked towards the book Harry had left beside the lamp.

"This time," Harry said, "I think I'm going to miss my room."

Sirius's face changed. He looked around the room as though seeing it through Harry's eyes: a space built in hope before Harry trusted it enough to arrive, then altered by every ordinary day.

"Yes," Sirius said.

Harry swallowed. "What if it changes while I'm gone?"

"It will."

Harry had been hoping for a different answer. Sirius shifted closer. "The roof will leak. Dobby will move your socks because he believes drawers are a moral issue. Lucius will acquire another chair no one is allowed to sit upon. Remus will leave books in here and claim he mistook the door. I'll probably damage something expensive."

"Probably?"

"Certainly. I was building suspense."

Harry smiled faintly.

"Houses change while people are away," Sirius said. "I'm not going to keep it exactly as it is," Sirius said. "But I'm not going to turn your room into somewhere else's, either. You'll come back, complain about whatever I've done to the workshop, and put your things where you want them."

Harry looked down at the key against his chest.

"My room stays?"

"Harry."

"I know."

"No, you don't. Not yet. So I'll keep telling you." Sirius touched the key through Harry's shirt. "This room stays yours if you spend every holiday at Hogwarts, decide you prefer Scotland, become a professional Quidditch player and buy a mansion with fourteen towers, or marry someone who refuses to live within fifty miles of me."

Harry felt heat enter his face.

"Why would they refuse?"

"Excellent question. Ask Draconia."

"I didn't say Draconia."

"I said ask her, not marry her."

Sirius's expression was unbearably pleased. Harry threw a pillow at him. Sirius caught it.

"Violence confirms guilt."

"You invented that."

"Guardian authority."

Harry reached for the second pillow. Sirius placed the first over his own face before impact. The brief fight ended when the compass fell between the mattress and wall. They spent five minutes retrieving it with a broom handle because neither wanted to wake Dobby and admit what had happened. When the room quietened again, Sirius sat upon the floor beside the bed.

"You'll mirror me every Sunday," he said. "And whenever you want. If the mirror fails its check, the ward alerts Hawthorn. If you use the extraction phrase, I come."

"Even if you're on a mission?"

"Then the duty person gets the alert and sends help. I'll come as soon as I can." Sirius leaned forward. "You won't be waiting for somebody to decide you've made enough fuss."

"So you aren't abandoning whatever you're doing halfway through?"

"I'm trying to learn the difference between a rescue plan and a very determined entrance."

Harry ran his thumb across the compass glass.

"What if Hogwarts doesn't feel safe anymore?"

Sirius looked up at him.

"Then you do not owe it loyalty because it saved you once."

The answer entered quietly. Harry had thought of Hogwarts as rescue for so long that fear of losing it resembled ingratitude. But castles were not people. Home was not a debt paid through endurance.

"You can come back," Sirius said. "Not only at Christmas. Any time. We'll argue with Dumbledore, the Ministry and every educational statute Hermione can carry."

"That's a lot of statutes."

"We'll bring a trolley."

Harry smiled. Sirius stood and bent to kiss the top of his head. Harry had not expected it. Sirius paused above him, his hand hovering for a moment before he began to draw away. Harry caught his wrist before he could move away.

"Night," he said.

Sirius's hand settled briefly against the back of Harry's head.

"Goodnight, kid."

He left the door open. Harry lay down. The compass rested upon his chest beside the key. The home needle pointed through him towards the floor. For the first time, leaving for Hogwarts hurt for the right reason.

The first of September began at five forty-three with Dobby accusing Sirius of sabotage.

"Master Sirius has moved the sandwiches!"

The shout travelled through Hawthorn's pipes, chimney and open bedroom doors with magical clarity. Harry woke reaching for his wand. By the time he recognised the ceiling, footsteps thundered along the corridor. A door struck a wall. Winky shouted that the sandwiches had been moved because Dobby had packed them beside ink. Sirius denied all involvement from somewhere downstairs.

"I was asleep!"

"Master Sirius was in the kitchen at two!"

"Different sandwiches!"

Harry lay back against the pillow. Hawthorn. Nineteen ninety-five. Departure morning. He listened. Hermione crossed the corridor outside, already awake. Draconia's door opened more slowly. A pipe knocked in the wall. From the floor below came the sound of Lucius objecting to someone placing pumpkin pasties inside his briefcase. The house was alive around him. Harry smiled into the dim room. Then the wardrobe opened by itself and threw his school trousers onto the bed.

"Dobby says Harry Potter must dress immediately," Dobby called through the wall.

"You could use the door."

"The door is too slow!"

Harry dressed. His trunk had vanished from the foot of the bed overnight. Dobby and Winky were conducting final inspections in the entrance hall. Hedwig's cage stood open beside the window; she had left before dawn, apparently unwilling to travel inside when the sky remained available. Harry pulled on the black jacket.

The hidden holster accepted his wand against his forearm. The mirror occupied the inside pocket nearest his chest. One emergency Portkey—a brass button—sat inside a sealed pouch at his waist. The second, a bent Knut, had been stitched into the lining of his school bag. Cedric's chain secured his glasses. Remus's compass and the Hawthorn key rested together beneath his shirt.

He bent to tie his new trainers and caught sight of himself in the wardrobe mirror: an ordinary schoolboy, provided nobody asked why he had so many pockets. He checked the sealed Portkey pouch once more, remembering that the train escort had to release the transport wards before it could be used. Then he shut the wardrobe and went to find breakfast. The smell of toast reached him halfway down the stairs, followed by Sirius asking where anyone had put the plates.

The entrance hall contained three school trunks, two covered owl cages, two broom cases, a stack of evidence folders disguised as Transfiguration notes and enough food to withstand a siege. Dobby stood upon Harry's trunk checking items from a scroll which unrolled across the floor and into the drawing room.

"Fourteen pairs of socks," Harry said.

"Sixteen pairs," Dobby replied.

"Why?"

"Danger does not wait for laundry."

"That's Moody's influence."

"Mr Moody says twenty. Winky says this is deranged."

Winky nodded firmly from inside Draconia's open case.

"Sixteen pairs is a compromise," Winky said firmly. "And Harry Potter must write if he needs more."

Harry accepted this because arguing with house-elves before six o'clock required strength he hoped to need later. Breakfast occupied every surface in the kitchen. Sirius had made eggs, which explained one scorched pan and the suspicious quantity of shell upon his plate. Remus sat near the stove with a cup of coffee and his injured side supported by a cushion he pretended had appeared accidentally. Mr and Mrs Granger arrived through the Floo wearing Muggle travelling clothes. Narcissa carried a list. Lucius carried the expression of someone who had lost an argument with the list. Hermione entered in full uniform. Her prefect badge shone upon her chest. She stopped when she saw Harry looking.

"Too early?"

"For the badge?"

"I thought it might be useful at the station."

"It is useful. People will know whom to ask about sock quantities."

"Sixteen pairs," Dobby supplied.

"Settled."

Hermione smiled and took the chair beside him. Draconia came down several minutes later wearing Muggle clothes beneath a dark travelling cloak. Her badge remained in its velvet box upon the table. Harry looked at it.

"I will put it on at the platform," she said.

"Ashamed of your authority?"

"Protecting it from breakfast."

Sirius dropped a piece of egg upon his own sleeve.

"Sensibly," Draconia added.

They ate between instructions. Hermione's mother reminded her to write even if school became busy. Narcissa reminded Draconia that authority did not require correcting every person who used it badly. Lucius advised precisely the opposite in different language. Remus reviewed the verification procedure. Sirius reviewed it again, then added that any teacher confiscating Harry's mirror should be informed the object was linked to active guardianship wards and removing it would trigger an immediate response.

"What sort of response?" Harry asked.

"Me."

"That sounds more like a threat."

"Excellent."

At six thirty, Kingsley's Patronus arrived in the shape of a lynx and announced that the route to King's Cross had been checked and the vehicles were ready. At six thirty-five, Moody's Patronus announced that no route was clear and anyone claiming otherwise was advertising incompetence. At six forty, Tonks arrived through the Floo and explained that this meant the transport was ready.

She wore a dark Auror cloak over a bright red shirt. Her hair was short and brown today, rendering her almost ordinary until she tripped upon the hearth and caught herself against Lucius. Lucius looked down at her hand upon his chest.

"Good morning," Tonks said.

"Is it?"

"Depends how attached you are to dignity."

Narcissa hid a smile. Tonks was her niece, though Narcissa's long estrangement from Andromeda had taught both women to approach the connection carefully. Their relationship had begun with careful surnames and progressed to Tonks stealing toast from Narcissa's plate.

"Two vehicles," Tonks said. "Ordinary Muggle vans outside the east boundary. Route changes twice. No magic unless signalled."

Arthur Weasley had selected the vans. This became obvious when Harry saw them. One was white, dented and bore the fading name of a plumbing company. The other advertised FRESH FISH across both sides in blue letters large enough to challenge discretion.

"Ordinary," Sirius said.

Tonks looked at the second van. "He was very excited."

Lucius stopped upon the drive. "I am not travelling inside a fish."

"It's a van," Harry said.

"Its stated identity disagrees."

"You take the plumbing one," Tonks offered.

"That is not better."

Narcissa entered the fish van. Lucius followed. Harry climbed into the plumbing van with Sirius, Remus, Hermione and the Grangers. Draconia had intended to travel with her parents. She looked from one vehicle to the other, saw Harry beside the open door and changed direction without explanation. Hermione moved across the bench to make room. Draconia sat between her and Harry.

"The fish van has more space," Harry said.

"It smells of herring."

"It has never carried fish," Tonks called from the front.

"Then the smell is more concerning."

Once everyone was seated, Tonks checked the belts. Harry had to pull his bag out from beneath his feet so Draconia could reach hers; she blamed Sirius's sandwiches for the lack of room, and Sirius defended them from the seat opposite. The doors closed, and the van began to move.

There was no dramatic final view. One moment, Harry saw the house beyond the boundary, its crooked roof dark against the morning and the lemon tree visible through the drawing-room glass. Then the van turned into the lane and hedges swallowed it. Harry pressed his palm against the key beneath his shirt. Home continued existing where he could not see it. That was another thing he had to learn.

King's Cross smelled of diesel, hot metal and too many breakfasts eaten from paper bags. Muggle commuters moved beneath the high glass roof in determined currents. Announcements echoed across the concourse. Wheels rattled over tile. Nobody looked twice at the unusual number of owls concealed beneath travel covers or the children pushing trunks towards a solid barrier between platforms nine and ten.

The two vans unloaded in a service lane behind the station after a slow drive through the waking city. The wait ahead would be long, but Sirius had insisted that leaving time to spare was preferable to running for a train with three trunks and a Ministry escort. Tonks entered first. Kingsley waited near the doors in a dark Muggle suit. He appeared to be reading a newspaper, though he looked up at every person approaching the doors. Three more members of the escort occupied the positions Kingsley had described at breakfast. Harry recognised one from the orchard briefing; the other two returned Kingsley's signal before turning back to the crowd.

"How many?" Draconia murmured.

"Seven," Harry replied.

Hermione adjusted the handle of her trolley. "Possibly nine. Two more by the ticket barrier," Hermione said.

They looked at her.

"They haven't checked a ticket since we came in. Though I suppose they could just be waiting for somebody."

Draconia studied them. "One more we actually know about," Draconia said. "The woman at the flower kiosk."

Harry followed her gaze. The elderly flower seller from Diagon Alley arranged roses near the entrance.

"She really is an Auror."

"I told you."

"She looks seventy."

"You remain strangely committed to the disguise."

Sirius pushed Harry's trunk beside them. He wore Muggle jeans, a white shirt and the leather jacket Harry now understood his own had been chosen to resemble. No one who passed recognised Sirius Black. Several people looked at him anyway.

"Ready?" he asked.

Harry looked towards the barrier. Last September, Hawthorn had still been new enough that Harry struggled to trust the promise of a place after he left. Today, the house remained less than an hour away. His book stood beside the lamp because he had chosen to leave it there. Sirius would return and find it, and Harry did not have to take it along to keep it safe.

"Nearly."

They waited for the Weasleys. The early start left time for another cup of tea while the escort checked their luggage. By the time the family appeared, the concourse had filled with children pushing trunks and parents trying to keep the owl cages covered.

Molly arrived with Ginny and Ron in a cluster of trunks. Arthur followed carrying three broom cases and arguing with a station employee about whether they required separate tickets. Fred and George appeared from behind a newsstand despite no one seeing them enter the station.

"Prefect!" Fred called.

Ron went red. "Shut up."

"He's protecting his anonymity," George explained.

Hermione stepped forward. "Ron, we have to report to the prefects' carriage once we board."

"Already?"

"The instructions say before departure."

"I thought we'd have until the train moved."

"Why?"

"No reason."

Hermione looked at him. "You didn't read the second page."

"There were two?"

Fred placed a hand over his heart. "Leadership."

Harry caught Ron's eye.

"Natural," he said.

Ron laughed despite himself. Draconia opened the velvet box and removed her badge. Harry held out his hand before she could fasten it. She raised one eyebrow.

"Installation service," he said.

"We are in public."

"Nervous?"

"Never."

She passed him the badge. Harry pinned it to her cloak. He was faster this time, though his fingers still brushed the fabric above her heart. Draconia watched his face rather than his hands.

"Straight?" he asked.

"Acceptable."

Hermione touched her own badge. "Mine may have shifted in the van."

"You checked it in the window twice," Harry said.

"Reflections distort."

Draconia folded her arms.

"Granger."

"Precision improves conversation."

Harry laughed and adjusted Hermione's badge by a degree.

Ron looked from one girl to the other. "Do I have to do that?"

"No," Harry said. "Apparently I have specialist qualifications."

"His remains crooked," Draconia observed.

Ron's hand flew to his chest. It was not crooked. Hermione smiled. Mr and Mrs Diggory arrived next. Cedric pushed his own trolley and carried the same battered school trunk he had taken to Hogwarts for years. Seeing it made his return real in a way the conversation in Diagon Alley had not. Cho Chang entered from the northern concourse a few minutes later with her mother. She stopped when she saw Cedric. For a moment, neither moved. Then Cho crossed the station and embraced him. Cedric's arms closed around her carefully, his eyes shutting. Harry looked away.

The reunion belonged to them, not to the version of Cedric the public had begun using as evidence.

"They're still together?" Ron asked quietly.

Hermione nudged his arm. "Ron, they're right there."

"I wasn't going to ask them."

"Then don't stare while you ask us."

Ron looked hastily towards the barrier. "I wasn't staring."

"Natural prefect judgement," Harry said.

Ron glared at him. The families approached the barrier in small groups. Harry went through with Sirius. For one instant, cold stone seemed to surround him. Then steam rolled across Platform Nine and Three-Quarters.

The scarlet engine waited beside the crowded platform, larger than memory and loud with restrained pressure. White vapour gathered around its wheels. Owls called overhead. Doors slammed. Parents leaned through windows issuing last instructions to children who pretended not to listen. Security had altered the familiar scene.

Two railway workers examined the underside of the engine with wands hidden inside spanners. A witch selling sweets near the rear van wore Auror boots beneath her apron. New silver lines had been painted around every carriage window and door, almost invisible until steam crossed them and the ward runes shone blue. Above the platform, the enchanted clock ticked towards eleven. Harry felt watched. Not by students. By the station itself. Kingsley appeared beyond the barrier and gave Sirius one small nod. Route clear. For now.

Goodbyes began twenty minutes before departure and refused to remain orderly. Mrs Granger hugged Hermione, inspected the prefect badge and reminded her that responsibility did not mean solving every problem alone. Mr Granger handed her a fountain pen and explained that it could write without inkpots if Hogwarts rules became inconvenient.

"Is it allowed?" Hermione asked.

"I was hoping you would teach the school something."

Molly embraced Ron until his ears matched his hair. Fred and George saluted. Ginny escaped her mother's final inspection by climbing aboard early with Luna, who had arrived wearing earrings shaped like tiny silver umbrellas. Narcissa stood before Draconia and adjusted the collar Harry had already straightened.

"Write after the feast."

"I will. You'll have a letter before breakfast."

"Write tonight, please." Narcissa smoothed the same fold in her collar again. "I know you'll be busy, but I'd rather not wait until morning."

Draconia's expression softened. "Tonight, then. And we'll do the arrival check before the feast."

Lucius waited until Narcissa had finished.

He placed one hand upon Draconia's shoulder. "People will tell you things because you're wearing that badge," Lucius said. "Some will want help; others will want you to take their side. Listen carefully before you promise either."

Draconia looked at him. "That is terrible advice for a prefect."

"It is excellent advice for my daughter."

She hugged him. Lucius's surprise lasted less time than it once would have. His arms closed around her without checking who watched. Harry turned towards Sirius and Remus. Neither had prepared a speech. Sirius looked as though he had prepared three and could not decide which to begin.

"Eat," Sirius began.

"Narcissa already said that."

"She was right."

"Entered into the record."

"Mirror every Sunday."

"And after the feast."

"Any teacher interferes—"

"I tell them removing it will summon you."

"Good."

Sirius looked at the train, the Aurors and the students moving around them. His jaw tightened.

"If something happens—"

"I don't act alone. I verify messages. I use the Portkeys when the escort confirms the transport wards have released. I do not attempt to duel an army from the roof."

Sirius narrowed his eyes. "Who added the roof?"

"Moody."

"Sensible."

Harry smiled. Remus held out his hand. Harry took it, but Remus pulled him into an embrace instead. He still moved carefully because of his ribs. Harry adjusted without being asked.

"The delay," Remus said near his ear. "Remember it."

"Half a second."

"Longer when you can afford it."

"That seems excessive."

Remus laughed softly.

He released Harry and touched the compass through his shirt. "Remember what it actually shows you," Remus said. "Places you know, not where people are or whether they're safe. If you're worried about us, use the mirror. Don't try to get an answer out of that needle."

Harry nodded. Sirius remained. They looked at one another amid steam, shouting and every goodbye happening around them. Harry had spent the night imagining this moment and found he possessed nothing clever enough for it.

"I'm coming home for Christmas," he said.

Sirius smiled, but his eyes brightened.

"I heard."

"The stag had trouble."

"Got there eventually."

Harry hugged him. Sirius held him hard. Not carefully this time. One hand pressed between Harry's shoulder blades, keeping him close while the train released a warning hiss beside them.

"Come home sooner if you need to," Sirius said into his hair.

"I will."

"Promise."

Harry closed his eyes. Promises frightened him when they depended upon survival. This one depended only upon asking.

"I promise."

Sirius released him slowly. Narcissa touched Harry's shoulder before he turned away. As always, she corrected something which did not require correcting, smoothing the lapel of his jacket over the hidden mirror.

"You heard him," she said.

"Yes."

"And you will eat."

"There it is."

Her smile appeared. Lucius approached last.

"Mr Potter."

"Mr Malfoy."

"Observe before reacting."

"Remus already said that."

"Then the advice has survived independent verification."

"Did you plan that joke?"

"Get on the train."

Harry grinned. The first whistle sounded. Hermione took one of his hands. Draconia took the other. Together, they boarded.

Their compartment stood halfway down the train. Hedwig had claimed the luggage rack before Harry arrived and regarded the other cages as intrusions. Hermione placed her school bag beneath the seat facing forward. Draconia inspected the window latch, the door and the silver ward line before sitting beside Harry.

"Prefects' carriage," Hermione said reluctantly.

"We know," Harry replied.

"The meeting should take twenty minutes."

"Thirty," Draconia said. "There are twenty-four prefects, plus the Head Boy and Girl. Somebody is bound to tell us their entire school history."

Ron appeared at the door wearing his badge and an expression of impending punishment.

"We're meant to go now."

"Second page?" Harry asked.

"Mum found it."

Hermione gathered her notes. "You'll be here?"

"Unless the train leaves without itself."

"Harry."

He smiled. "I'll be here. Cedric said he'd come by, and Tonks is right outside."

Draconia stood and looked towards the platform, where Sirius remained visible through the steam, then back at Harry.

"Mirror?"

He tapped the inside of his jacket.

"Portkeys?"

"Both."

"Wand?"

Harry drew it from the holster and returned it in one smooth movement.

"Show-off," Hermione said.

"Draconia built it. Blame her."

"I accept responsibility," Draconia replied.

She caught the front of his jacket and pulled him close enough to check the fastening. Her face was inches from his.

"We will come back," she said quietly.

Harry knew she meant the compartment. The sentence carried more.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He looked at her.

"I'm learning."

Draconia released the jacket. Hermione touched Harry's cheek as she passed, an affectionate gesture so brief Ron pretended to examine the ceiling.

"Twenty minutes," she said.

"Thirty," Draconia repeated.

"Twenty-five," Harry decided.

"You don't get to shorten the meeting just because you'd rather we stayed," Hermione told him.

"That's recently been established."

Her expression fell. Harry caught her wrist before she could apologise.

"I'm joking, Hermione. You haven't done anything wrong."

Hermione searched his face, then nodded. Tonks appeared at the doorway as the three prefects left together. She was wearing the younger face she had shown them during the briefing, her Auror cloak exchanged for a student's travelling coat.

"I'll walk them to the meeting and come straight back," she told Harry. "Kingsley's covering this section."

Harry nodded. Further down the corridor, Kingsley looked up from a folded timetable. The arrangement was less reassuring than having the girls beside him, but it was an arrangement, not an empty promise.

Ron looked back once from the corridor. "Save us seats."

"Natural authority," Harry said.

Ron gave him a rude gesture behind Hermione's back and hurried after them. Harry sat beside the window. Outside, families moved through steam. Mrs Granger stood with one arm through her husband's. Narcissa and Lucius remained close together. Remus leaned upon his cane. Sirius stood nearest the carriage, both hands inside his jacket pockets and his attention fixed upon Harry's window. The second whistle sounded. Doors slammed along the platform.

The train shuddered. Harry caught the edge of his school bag before it slid from the opposite seat and set it beside him. Out in the corridor, somebody was trying to persuade a cat to leave an empty compartment; farther away came the familiar rattle of a trolley wheel over the joins in the floor.

For one irrational moment, Harry wanted to leave it. He could cross the corridor, jump to the platform and return to Hawthorn before lunch. Hogwarts contained Umbridge, surveillance and a year already arranged to test whether his anger could be used against him. Home waited less than an hour away.

Harry pressed the key through his shirt. The brass was warm from resting against his skin. He could still get off, and he could still return another day; nothing about boarding the train had taken those choices away. The engine pulled forward. Sirius walked beside the window for the first few feet. Then faster. Harry raised one hand against the glass. Sirius did the same. The platform began to slide.

Remus, the Grangers and the Malfoys moved with it. Faces passed behind steam. Lucius inclined his head. Narcissa lifted her hand. Remus smiled. Sirius remained visible longest, black hair caught by the wind as the train gathered speed. Then King's Cross curved away. Harry kept his hand upon the window until brick replaced the platform and daylight opened beyond the station roof.

Harry lowered his hand from the window. The seat opposite was still warm where Hermione had sat, and Draconia's bag remained tucked beneath it. He moved his own things out of the way so there would be room when they returned. He set his bag on the floor instead of leaving it on the opposite cushion. Cedric would need somewhere to sit, and Ron had asked him to save a place too.

Outside, the last rooftops slipped away. Harry leaned back and listened to the voices in the corridor, waiting for one he knew.
