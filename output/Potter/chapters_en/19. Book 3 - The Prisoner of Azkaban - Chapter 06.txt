6

Harry sat outside Professor Lupin's office fidgeting with his shirt. He had arrived eleven minutes early. That had not been intentional. At least, he had not admitted it was intentional until the corridor had cleared and the clock opposite him confirmed how long he had been waiting. His schoolbag rested between his shoes. Inside it lay an unfinished History of Magic essay, two crushed biscuits Hermione had forced upon him at lunch and a note from Draconia containing seven questions she believed he ought to ask before allowing Lupin to expose him to anything resembling a Dementor. The final question had been underlined twice.

What safeguards has he established if you lose consciousness?

Hermione had added a line beneath it in smaller handwriting.

Do not antagonise the person responsible for catching you.

Harry had written his answer across the bottom.

This advice has arrived two years too late.

Neither girl had found that reassuring. He unfolded the parchment again, though he already knew every word upon it. The paper bore signs of the disagreement which had produced it: one corner was torn, three sentences had been crossed out and Draconia had spilled green ink over Hermione's proposed list of emergency procedures. At the sound of approaching footsteps, Harry folded it quickly and pushed it into his bag.

Professor Lupin appeared around the corner carrying a battered wooden case beneath one arm. He wore robes which had been repaired at both elbows and looked more tired than he had during their conversation several days earlier, though the smile he offered Harry was genuine.

"I hope you haven't been waiting long."

"Not really."

Lupin glanced at the clock. Harry glanced at the opposite wall.

"Eleven minutes," Lupin said.

"The staircases were unusually cooperative."

"That must have been alarming."

"I'm trying not to dwell on it."

Lupin shifted the case and unlocked his office. "Come in."

The room had changed since Harry's last visit. Several desks had been moved against the walls, leaving an open stretch of floor in the centre. Lamps burned in each corner, filling the office with warm amber light despite the grey afternoon beyond the windows. A broad-backed chair stood near the fireplace, and the tea tray upon Lupin's desk had been joined by a plate of sandwiches. Harry noticed them immediately. He wished he had not.

Lupin placed the wooden case beside a tall cupboard and removed his outer robe. Beneath it, his jumper had been darned at the shoulder with thread several shades darker than the wool.

"Before we begin," he said, "there are a few things you need to understand."

Harry sat in the chair nearest the desk. "Draconia has prepared questions."

"That sounds ominous."

"She's very thorough when she thinks someone might kill me."

Lupin's hands paused over the teapot.

"Does she often think that?"

"More often than you'd expect."

"Given your history, I'm not sure I would."

Harry took out the parchment and passed it across the desk. Lupin read the first question. One corner of his mouth moved. He continued down the list, his expression becoming steadily more entertained until he reached Draconia's final, heavily underlined demand.

"Miss Malfoy asks questions rather like her father."

"Is that good?"

"It depends whether you are the risk."

Lupin set the parchment down. "You may tell her that the Dementor in this room will not be real. The case contains a Boggart. When released, it should assume the form it used during our lesson."

"Should?"

"Magic involving fear is not always predictable."

"That word is going to bother her."

"I thought it might."

"And Hermione."

"I assumed she helped construct the list."

"She wanted twelve questions. Draconia said eight looked less panicked."

"That was the less panicked version?"

Harry nodded. Lupin regarded him for a few seconds. His amusement softened, though it did not disappear entirely.

"They care about you."

"I know."

"You say that as if it presents a difficulty."

"It occasionally produces paperwork."

Lupin smiled and pushed the plate of sandwiches towards him. Harry looked at it.

"I ate lunch."

"I didn't ask."

"I did."

"I believe you."

The plate remained between them. Harry waited for Lupin to insist. Adults usually did one of two things once they noticed he had not eaten enough: they ignored it because feeding him was inconvenient, or they watched every mouthful with such concentration that swallowing became impossible. Lupin poured two cups of tea.

"The Boggart will react to you as a Dementor would," he said. "It will bring cold into the room. It may affect your thoughts and your memories. It cannot perform a Dementor's Kiss, nor can it truly consume happiness, but your body may not recognise the difference."

Harry stared at the cupboard.

"Helpful body."

"It was designed for survival, not convenience."

"Mine appears to have misunderstood both."

Lupin's gaze returned to him, quiet and attentive. He did not smile this time. Harry reached for half a sandwich. The bread was soft, the cheese sharp and slightly warm from having sat near the fire. He managed three bites before setting it on his plate. Lupin drank his tea without commenting.

"A Patronus," he continued, "is not merely a shield made from happiness. That is the description most textbooks use because it is easier than the truth."

"What's the truth?"

"That depends upon the witch or wizard casting it. The charm takes what a Dementor tries to remove—hope, affection, purpose, the conviction that life contains more than the worst thing which has happened to you—and gives it form."

Harry considered this.

"So I think of a happy memory."

"Yes, but recalling happiness is only the beginning. You must inhabit it. You have to believe, while you cast, that the memory represents something real and lasting."

"That sounds harder."

"It is."

"Textbooks leave that part out."

"Textbooks are often written by people who have forgotten what it is like not to understand them."

Hermione would either have liked that sentence very much or regarded it as a personal attack. Lupin rose and crossed to the open space in the centre of the office. He drew his wand.

"The incantation is Expecto Patronum. The movement begins here."

He demonstrated slowly. The wand swept upwards from his right side before turning in a clean, decisive curve. Harry copied it.

"Again," Lupin said.

Harry repeated the movement.

"Keep your wrist loose."

"I am."

"You are holding your wand as if you expect it to escape."

"It has tried before."

"I'm beginning to understand Miss Malfoy's paperwork."

They practised the movement until Harry's shoulder loosened and the incantation no longer caught against his teeth. Lupin corrected his pronunciation twice and the angle of his wand four times. There was no Boggart yet. No cold. Only the quiet repetition of movement, the hiss of the fire and rain tapping against the windows. Harry began to relax. That, he suspected, was why Lupin had started this way.

"Now choose a memory," Lupin said.

Harry lowered his wand. "Any happy one?"

"Preferably one which belongs entirely to you."

The answer should have been easy.

He had more choices now than he would have possessed three years earlier. Hogwarts had given him flying, feasts, presents and friends. He could remember the first time he had stepped into the Great Hall and seen candles suspended beneath a ceiling filled with stars. He could remember Hagrid placing a birthday cake into his hands. Hermione asleep over a stack of library books. Draconia discovering that treacle tart stuck permanently to expensive gloves. Christmas mornings. Quidditch victories. Summer evenings at Malfoy Manor when Narcissa had pretended not to notice three children stealing strawberries before dinner.

Every memory arrived with another attached to it. The Great Hall became Quirrell burning beneath his hands. Hagrid's cake became iron bars across his bedroom window. Hermione sleeping became Hermione lying rigid and sightless behind a hospital curtain. Draconia laughing became her body on the Chamber floor, bloodless beneath the stone face of Salazar Slytherin.

Harry's fingers tightened around his wand.

"Take your time," Lupin said.

Harry chose flying. Not the match against Slytherin, nor the broom which had tried to throw him into the stands. He chose his first lesson instead: the school grounds green beneath him, the handle of the broom steady beneath his hands, the astonishing instant when gravity had ceased to be an instruction he was required to obey. He drew the memory close. Wind against his face. Robes snapping around his legs. The castle below. The sky open in every direction.

"Ready?" Lupin asked.

Harry nodded. Lupin moved towards the cupboard.

"The boggart is in the cupboard. I'll stand between you and it to begin with. Raise your left hand if you want me to stop. If you drop your wand or stop answering, I'll stop anyway. All right?"

"Yes."

"If you believe you can continue but I tell you to stop, what do you do?"

"Argue?"

"You stop."

"Then argue?"

"If you must."

Harry lifted his wand. Lupin opened the cupboard. Darkness unfolded from within it. The lamps dimmed first. Their amber flames contracted into pinpoints, and the cheerful movement of the fire slowed until each ember seemed fixed beneath a skin of grey ash. Cold crept over Harry's shoes and beneath his robes, searching for every place warmth had collected. A tall, hooded figure rose from the cupboard.

Its feet never touched the floor. Rotten hands emerged from its sleeves, grey flesh stretched over bone. The Dementor-shaped Boggart bent beneath the ceiling and turned its hidden face towards Harry. He held on to the broom. The sky. The freedom.

The Boggart glided forward. A woman screamed. The memory of flight broke apart. Harry was no longer above the Hogwarts grounds. He was beneath them, sinking through black water while stone pressed down from every direction. Draconia lay before him in the Chamber. Hermione's empty hospital bed waited beyond her. The scream came again, close enough to tear through his skull.

"Harry."

Lupin's voice reached him from a long distance. The Boggart moved closer.

"Harry, cast."

He raised his wand.

"Expecto Patronum!"

A thread of silver emerged from the tip. It trembled in the air, no brighter than moonlight caught upon a spider's web. The Boggart did not stop. Cold closed around Harry's lungs.

"Not Harry," Voldemort said.

His mother begged.

"Please—please—"

"Expecto Patronum!"

The silver thread widened into a cloud. It drifted forwards and vanished against the Boggart's robes. The hood lifted. Harry did not see a face. He saw a doorway, a flash of green and his mother's hands reaching towards a child she could not save. His wand lowered. Lupin stepped in front of him.

"Riddikulus."

The Dementor collapsed inwards. Its robes crumpled, its height vanished and an enormous black balloon rolled across the carpet. A silver pin struck it. The balloon burst with a noise like a champagne cork. The cold released the room. Harry staggered. Lupin caught his upper arm, guided him into the nearest chair and placed a square of chocolate in his hand. Harry stared at it.

"Eat."

"I'm not—"

"That was not a question."

The chocolate cracked between Harry's teeth. Sweetness spread across his tongue, followed by warmth which seemed to move directly into his blood. Lupin shut the Boggart inside the cupboard and reinforced the lock with three separate spells. Harry's hands began to shake. He folded them beneath his arms.

"That went badly."

"You produced light on your first attempt."

"It didn't do anything."

"It resisted."

"It disappeared."

"After resisting."

Harry looked towards the dark cupboard. The woman's scream remained within his head, thinner now but no less recognisable.

"I thought of flying."

"A good choice."

"It changed."

Lupin returned to the desk but did not sit behind it. He lowered himself into the chair opposite Harry, leaving little distance between them.

"What did it become?"

Harry ran his thumb along the edge of the chocolate wrapper.

"The Chamber first."

Lupin waited.

"Draconia was on the floor. Hermione was still Petrified. Then I heard my mother."

The colour shifted in Lupin's face. Harry noticed because he had become very good at noticing what adults wished he would not.

"You knew her," he said.

"Yes."

"What was she like?"

Lupin's eyes moved towards the rain-streaked window. The answer took long enough that Harry wondered whether he had asked the wrong question.

"She was fierce," Lupin said at last.

Harry had expected kind. People always called dead mothers kind, even if they had never known them. Kind and beautiful, reduced to two harmless words upon a grave.

"Fierce?"

"Not in the way James was. Your father announced his anger to an entire room and usually three corridors beyond it. Lily could become very quiet." Lupin's mouth curved faintly. "It was much more frightening."

"What made her angry?"

"Cruelty. Carelessness. People deciding that another person's pain was acceptable because acknowledging it would be inconvenient."

Harry looked down at the chocolate.

"She would have hated the Dursleys."

The words left him before he could stop them. Lupin did not offer an immediate reassurance. His jaw tightened, and the weariness around his eyes altered into an expression Harry could not quite name.

"Yes," he said. "She would have."

"Did she laugh?"

"Often."

"What at?"

"James, usually. Sometimes with him. The distinction mattered."

Harry imagined a red-haired girl laughing while his father pretended not to mind being the cause of it. It was not a memory. It never could be. Still, the image warmed a small place inside him.

"Do you think she knew?" he asked.

"Knew what?"

"That she was going to die."

Lupin drew a slow breath.

"No. I don't."

"She sounds frightened."

The rain continued against the glass. Neither of them pretended Harry had not heard what he had heard.

"The first time the Dementor came near me, I only heard her scream," Harry said. "On the train, I was her. I could see my room through her eyes. I could feel her holding me."

Lupin's hand closed around the arm of his chair.

"A Dementor doesn't bring back memories in any particular order," he said. Some of the colour had gone from his face. "You were too young to remember that night as you would remember yesterday. But something of it stayed with you. I'm afraid the Dementor found it."

"Was Sirius there?"

"No."

The answer was immediate. Harry looked up.

Lupin continued more carefully. "Not when Voldemort entered the house."

"But he helped Voldemort find them."

"That is what I was told."

"You don't sound certain."

For the first time, Lupin looked trapped. The expression vanished quickly, but not before Harry saw it.

"There was a war," Lupin said. "Certainty became a luxury. People lied. People disappeared. Friends suspected one another because trusting the wrong person could kill everyone they loved."

"Did you trust Sirius?"

"Yes."

"Did my parents?"

"Yes."

"Then why would he betray them?"

Lupin's gaze dropped to his hands.

"I have asked myself that question for twelve years."

Harry waited for more. None came.

"Was he my parents' friend?"

"He was James's closest friend."

The cold seemed to return, though the Boggart remained locked away.

"Closer than you?"

"Yes."

"And now he wants to kill me."

Lupin's face tightened.

"The Ministry believes he intends to reach you."

"That isn't the same answer."

"No," Lupin admitted. "It isn't."

Harry studied him. Most adults filled uncertainty with authority until it sounded like fact. Lupin left the empty spaces visible. It should have made Harry trust him less. Instead, it felt like the first honest answer anyone had given him about Sirius Black. Lupin pushed the sandwiches towards him again.

"Your spell failed because the memory became tangled with fear," he said. "Not because you are incapable of casting it."

"Can we try again?"

"Not today."

"I'm all right."

"You nearly collapsed."

"I didn't."

"You were halfway there."

"I could do better now."

"I believe you could." Lupin's voice remained mild. "We still aren't trying again."

Harry's frustration flared hard enough to surprise him.

"You said you'd teach me."

"I am teaching you. Part of that process will involve stopping before your determination becomes another method of hurting yourself."

"I wasn't hurting myself."

"You heard your mother die."

"That happens whenever a Dementor gets close. It'll happen whether I practise or not."

"Yes. Which is why we will practise slowly enough that you can learn from the experience rather than merely endure it."

Harry looked away. The office felt too warm now. The fire had recovered, the lamps had brightened and sweat cooled beneath his collar. His body could never seem to agree with the room it occupied.

"How long will that take?"

"I don't know."

"I need to know how to stop them."

"You will."

"You can't promise that."

"No." Lupin picked up his cup. "But I can promise to keep teaching you for as long as you are willing to learn."

Harry had no answer ready for that. Promises from adults usually came with hidden endings. I'll be back shortly. You'll be perfectly safe. This is for your own good. Lupin's promise was smaller. It did not claim success or safety. It only offered presence. Harry ate the rest of the sandwich. When he left twenty minutes later, Hermione and Draconia were waiting at the end of the corridor. They had attempted to disguise this by standing on opposite sides of a suit of armour and pretending to conduct an unrelated argument.

"You followed me," Harry said.

"We walked in the same direction," Hermione replied.

"For four floors?"

"The library staircase moved."

"It moved you outside Lupin's office?"

Draconia inspected one of the armour's gauntlets. "Hogwarts is architecturally unreliable."

Harry looked from one to the other. Hermione's bag bulged with enough books to damage her spine. Draconia still wore her Potions robes, and a faint green stain marked one cuff. Neither asked why his face felt cold or why a chocolate wrapper protruded from his pocket.

"How did it go?" Hermione asked instead.

"I made a silver string."

"That sounds promising."

"It vanished."

"Less promising," Draconia said.

"Lupin says it resisted first."

Hermione brightened. "Then it worked partially."

"So I only partly failed."

"You made something happen. That counts."

"Granger has diagrams for most of them."

"I do not make diagrams for everything."

Draconia and Harry looked at her.

Hermione adjusted the strap of her bag. "Only when they're useful."

They began walking towards dinner. Harry expected questions. He had prepared answers which would reveal nothing important and several jokes designed to change the subject. Neither girl used the opportunity. Draconia complained about an essay Snape had assigned upon the legal distinction between poisons and improperly labelled cosmetic potions. Hermione explained that Professor Vector had recommended a book which appeared to have been written by a person who believed numbers were more emotionally stable than people. By the time they reached the Great Hall, Harry's hands had stopped trembling.

He still told them about hearing his mother. Not everything. He left out the green light and the sensation of Lily's arms around him. He did not repeat Lupin's uncertainty concerning Sirius. That conversation felt too fragile to expose before he had decided what it meant. But he told them enough. Hermione stopped beside the Gryffindor table. Draconia went completely still.

Harry waited for the horror in their expressions, for the immediate demand that he stop the lessons or allow them to attend. Hermione reached into her bag and withdrew a slightly squashed chocolate frog.

"Professor Lupin said chocolate helps, didn't he?"

"Yes."

"This is all I have."

Harry accepted it.

Draconia looked offended. "I have better chocolate."

"You didn't offer it."

"I was about to."

"You were staring."

"I can stare and offer confectionery simultaneously."

"Prove it."

Draconia opened her satchel. Harry laughed before he meant to. Several students turned towards them. Draconia produced a silver-wrapped bar and pressed it into his hand with great dignity.

"You are both becoming strange about food," Harry said.

"We have always been strange about food," Hermione replied. "You've only recently begun noticing."

They separated for dinner. Harry sat between Neville and one of the Weasley twins. Across the hall, Draconia took her usual place at the Slytherin table. Hermione opened a book beside her plate until Percy confiscated it and told her that reading while eating was bad for digestion. She began arguing with him. Harry unwrapped Draconia's chocolate beneath the table. For the first time that evening, the scream inside his head became quiet.

The Patronus Charm entered his life slowly. It occupied Thursday afternoons first, then spread into the rest of the week. Harry practised the wand movement while walking between classes, beneath the desk during History of Magic and once in the changing room before Quidditch practice, where Wood accused him of developing an entirely new fault in his Seeker technique.

"You keep turning your wrist," Wood said, seizing Harry's forearm and demonstrating the correct angle for diving. "You'll lose speed."

"I'm practising a spell."

"On your broom?"

"Not deliberately."

"Whatever it is, stop."

"It might save my soul."

Wood released him. "After the match."

Harry decided not to argue. Oliver Wood ranked Quidditch somewhere above oxygen and considerably above the continued spiritual wellbeing of his players. During the second lesson, Lupin did not open the cupboard at all. He made Harry sit beside the fire and describe five things he could see, four he could feel, three he could hear, two he could smell and one he could taste.

"This is Defence Against the Dark Arts?" Harry asked.

"Yes."

"I thought there would be more magic."

"There will be."

"I can already see the table."

"The exercise is meant to anchor you in the room."

"I'm fairly sure the chair is doing that."

Lupin threw a cushion at him. Harry caught it and nearly dropped his tea.

"Your father was exactly like this," Lupin said.

"Good at catching cushions?"

"Determined to mock any lesson which required patience."

"Was he good at it?"

"Unfortunately."

Harry smiled despite himself. They built a memory without using the charm. Lupin made him choose an ordinary one rather than the greatest happiness he could imagine. Harry selected an evening at the Leaky Cauldron near the end of summer. Nothing remarkable had happened. Hermione had tried to teach Draconia a Muggle card game. Draconia had accused the cards of lacking strategic dignity. Harry had cheated badly enough that both girls noticed but disagreed over which rule he had broken. Crookshanks had stolen a slice of ham from Lucius Malfoy's plate and carried it beneath a cabinet while Lucius watched in appalled silence.

Harry could remember the exact sound of Hermione laughing. He could remember Draconia's hair falling loose from its ribbon when she lunged across the table to seize his cards. The room had smelled of woodsmoke, roast potatoes and the rain which had followed shoppers into the pub.

"What happened afterwards?" Lupin asked.

"We went upstairs."

"And then?"

"I slept."

"Did anyone get hurt?"

"No."

"Was there a threat?"

"No."

"Did you have to save anyone that evening?"

Harry understood what Lupin was doing.

"It still ended."

"All days end. That does not make what happened inside them less real."

Harry watched the fire shift around a blackened log.

"They could still leave."

"Yes."

"They could still get hurt."

"Yes."

"That isn't very reassuring."

"I'm not trying to reassure you. I'm asking whether the possibility of losing a person makes loving them a mistake."

Harry's immediate answer was no. The answer his body gave was more complicated. He thought of Hermione's empty stare and Draconia's trembling hand around his wrist. He remembered the enormous, silent space of the summer after their letters stopped. Happiness had become dangerous precisely because it could be removed. Before Hogwarts, he had possessed very little worth fearing for. Life had been easier in that one narrow respect.

"No," Harry said at last. "It doesn't make it a mistake."

"Hold on to the memory."

Harry closed his eyes. The Leaky Cauldron returned. Hermione slapped a card onto the table. Draconia objected. Crookshanks vanished beneath Lucius's chair trailing ham and triumph. Harry raised his wand.

"Expecto Patronum."

Silver mist breathed from the tip. It did not form a shield. It hovered above Lupin's carpet, pale and uncertain, before dissolving into the firelight. Harry opened his eyes.

"That was better," Lupin said.

"It lasted three seconds."

"Four."

"Are you rounding up?"

"I'm being generous."

"Is that part of the instruction?"

"It is when the pupil looks likely to throw his wand into the fire."

Harry lowered it. Lupin handed him a piece of chocolate. The lessons continued. Between them, October settled over Hogwarts. The hills surrounding the castle turned from green to rust. Leaves collected in the courtyards and chased one another through open archways whenever the wind rose. Rain pressed against the windows for days at a time, blurring the grounds until Hagrid's hut became a dark smudge beside the forest.

Inside, the castle grew warmer and louder. Fires appeared in every grate. Damp cloaks crowded the entrance hall, filling it with the smell of wet wool. Students began measuring the term not by lessons but by the approach of the first Hogsmeade weekend. Notices appeared one Monday morning. They were pinned inside every common room, beside the doors of the Great Hall and upon the board outside Filch's office, though nobody could explain why any student would voluntarily approach the last of these. Harry found the Gryffindor notice surrounded by half the third years.

FIRST HOGSMEADE WEEKEND SATURDAY, 16 OCTOBER THIRD YEARS AND ABOVE SIGNED PERMISSION FORMS REQUIRED The parchment might as well have added his name at the bottom. Dean began listing everything he intended to buy at Zonko's. Seamus insisted the village contained a sweetshop capable of making Honeydukes look ordinary, although nobody believed him once he admitted his cousin had provided this information after consuming six bottles of Butterbeer. Neville turned towards Harry.

"Did McGonagall sign yours?"

"No."

The conversation around them faltered. Harry wished he had lied.

"She couldn't," he added. "School rules."

"That's rubbish," Seamus said.

"It's parchment, actually."

"No, I mean—"

"I know."

Neville looked genuinely distressed. "Maybe Professor Dumbledore could do it."

"If he wanted to, he would have done it when McGonagall asked."

"You could ask again."

Harry stepped away from the board.

"I could also spend Saturday enjoying the rare experience of a quiet castle."

The idea followed him to breakfast. Hermione had already seen the notice. She attacked her porridge with the concentration of someone preparing an argument. Across the hall, Draconia held up a folded square of parchment.

We are not going.

Harry found a clean portion of his napkin and wrote back.

Yes, you are.

The paper bird crossed the Hall. Draconia read the answer and frowned. Hermione leaned towards her from the Gryffindor table, attempting to decipher the message from thirty feet away. Draconia wrote again.

You cannot order us to abandon you more politely.

Harry replied beneath the table.

I'm not being polite. Go.

Hermione intercepted the bird before it reached Draconia. She read both messages, produced a quill from her hair and added her own sentence.

We haven't decided.

Harry wrote:

You spent all week discussing Honeydukes.

Hermione returned:

That is unrelated.

Draconia added:

Her list is fourteen inches long.

Hermione crossed out the sentence. Draconia rewrote it below. Harry looked between them. Hermione was trying to glare at Draconia without attracting Professor McGonagall's attention. Draconia calmly buttered a piece of toast. The normality of it eased the pressure in Harry's chest. He wrote one final response.

If you both stay, I'll spend the entire day telling you how much fun everyone else is having.

Draconia read it.

That is emotional blackmail.

Harry nodded across the Hall. Hermione took the parchment.

We taught him too well.

They agreed to go. The decision did not prevent them from trying to arrange his Saturday in advance. Hermione proposed a library schedule, a list of teachers who might be available and several indoor locations from which Harry could observe the weather without technically leaving the castle. Draconia suggested he spend the afternoon with Lupin, visit Hagrid before dusk and carry the emergency mirror Narcissa had sent after the summer. Harry refused the schedule. He accepted the mirror only after Draconia threatened to place it in his bag without telling him.

"That defeats the purpose of asking," he said.

"We have moved beyond asking."

"You know, when Lucius does that, you complain."

"When Father does it, he is controlling."

"And when you do it?"

"Efficient."

Hermione looked up from correcting her Arithmancy calculation. "It is controlling when both of you do it."

Draconia appeared offended by the betrayal. Harry smiled down at his Charms essay, though the feeling beneath it was less simple than amusement. Their concern had become a presence of its own. He encountered it at meals when Hermione pushed food towards him before serving herself. It followed him into corridors when Draconia demanded to know where he was going and when he expected to return. If he rubbed his scar, failed to answer quickly or spent too long staring at nothing, both girls became very still. Harry understood why.

He had watched Hermione breathe without moving for weeks. He had carried Draconia out of the Chamber while poison worked through his blood. He knew what fear did to the mind after danger had passed. Understanding did not make the attention easier to bear. Sometimes their eyes felt too much like locks. Saturday arrived beneath a pale, clean sky. The rain had stopped during the night, leaving the grounds silvered with frost. Boots rang against the entrance-hall flagstones while students queued beside Filch's desk and presented their permission forms for inspection.

Harry stood near the marble staircase with Hermione and Draconia. Hermione wore a Gryffindor scarf wrapped twice around her neck. Draconia's dark green coat fitted too neatly to have been bought with school robes in mind, and silver fastenings flashed at the cuffs. Each carried an empty bag destined to return considerably heavier.

"You are certain?" Hermione asked.

"Yes."

"We could stay until the next weekend," Draconia said.

"You'll say the same thing next time."

"Possibly."

"Go."

Hermione adjusted her scarf. "You'll be in the library?"

"Some of the time."

"And then Lupin?"

"If he's free."

"Hagrid knows you may visit," Draconia said. "I sent word."

"You sent Hagrid my schedule?"

"The schedule you refused."

"That seems to weaken the refusal."

Filch shouted for the next three students.

Harry reached into his pocket and removed a small leather pouch. "Here."

Hermione took it. Coins shifted inside.

"What is this?"

"My contribution to the irresponsible purchase of sweets."

"We don't need your money."

"I know. I want things."

Draconia examined him. "Specific things?"

"Surprise me."

"That is a dangerous amount of trust."

"You bought me a green jumper for Christmas."

"It was tasteful."

"It had silver snakes around the wrists."

"You continued wearing it."

"Only beneath my robes."

Hermione smiled. "We'll find something equally subtle."

"Please don't."

The line moved forward. Harry meant to tell them to have fun. The words should have been easy. Instead, he imagined the gates closing behind them. He saw the road curving beyond the grounds and the two people he trusted most disappearing into a place he could not follow. His mind supplied a series of possibilities without being invited: Sirius in the village, Dementors along the road, an attack no one had predicted. Draconia saw the change in his face.

"We will be back before supper," she said.

"I know."

"Hermione has the mirror."

Hermione touched the pocket of her coat. Narcissa had charmed a pair of silver mirrors so that speaking the other person's name would call their reflection into the glass. Hogwarts wards interfered with the enchantment at long distances, but they ought to function between the castle and the village.

"We tested it," Hermione said.

"Seven times," Harry replied.

"Eight," Draconia corrected. "The first test was inconclusive."

Harry breathed through the pressure beneath his ribs.

"Go before Filch decides I'm attempting to smuggle myself out in Hermione's bag."

"It would not work," Hermione said. "There are too many books."

Draconia leaned closer to Harry. For an instant, he expected a hug. Instead, she straightened his scarf with a quick movement that reminded him painfully of Narcissa at King's Cross.

"If you leave the grounds," she said, "I will know."

"How?"

"I have methods."

"You don't."

"I have Granger."

Hermione lifted one shoulder. "She has me."

Harry surrendered. They joined the queue. He watched Filch inspect their forms, watched the oak doors open and watched the crowd pour down the steps into the cold. Hermione turned at the foot of the path and waved. Draconia did not wave, but she looked back twice before the students passed through the gates. Then they were gone. The entrance hall fell silent.

Harry remained beside the staircase until the cold from the open doors had disappeared.

"Quiet, isn't it?"

Neville stood several steps above him. Harry had forgotten that Neville's grandmother had not signed his form either.

"Brilliantly," Harry said.

Neville glanced towards the doors. "It's awful."

"Completely."

They looked at each other.

"Chess?" Neville suggested.

Harry considered the empty day waiting ahead.

"Only if you're prepared to lose with dignity."

"I've seen you play Ron."

"Then you know dignity won't be involved."

They spent the first hour in the common room. Neville was better at chess than Harry expected and worse at remembering that the pieces could object to his decisions. After his second bishop refused to advance, Neville attempted to reason with it. The argument ended when the bishop accused him of tactical cowardice and lay flat upon its square. Harry laughed hard enough to frighten Crookshanks from the rug. The sound felt unfamiliar in the empty room. Later, Neville went to the greenhouses to help Professor Sprout repot a collection of plants which had begun eating their labels. Harry took his bag to the library.

The castle without most of its older students felt larger. His footsteps followed him through corridors. Portraits slept openly in their frames, no longer concerned with being seen. Sunlight crossed the library tables in long golden bands, revealing dust which turned slowly above the shelves. Harry opened his Defence book. For twenty minutes, he read the same paragraph about non-corporeal Patronuses. Then someone placed a mug beside his elbow. He looked up.

Lupin stood over him carrying another cup and a roll of parchment.

"I was told you might be here."

"Draconia?"

"Hagrid."

"Draconia told Hagrid."

"So I gathered."

Harry wrapped his hands around the mug. It contained hot chocolate rather than tea.

"Are you checking on me too?"

The question came out sharper than intended.

Lupin sat opposite him. "I had an essay to return. Then Hagrid told me you were in here on your own."

"I don't."

"I see."

Lupin set the essay upon the table. A red E had been written at the top. Harry waited for him to leave. Lupin opened the book he had brought and began reading.

"You're staying," Harry said.

"I have some reading to do."

"There are other tables."

"This one has hot chocolate."

Harry looked down at the second mug. Lupin turned a page. They sat without speaking. The silence differed from the silence of Privet Drive. That silence had always been a punishment or a warning, stretched carefully around Harry until any sound he made became proof of wrongdoing. Here, pages turned. Quills moved. Somewhere among the shelves, Madam Pince sneezed and muttered angrily at the dust. Lupin did not watch him drink.

After several minutes, Harry returned to his paragraph.

"Did my father ever have to stay behind?" he asked.

Lupin lowered his book.

"From Hogsmeade?"

"Yes."

"Once."

"Why?"

"He and Sirius charmed every suit of armour on the third floor to sing the school song whenever Professor McGonagall passed."

Harry tried to imagine it. "Why?"

"They were fourteen."

"That doesn't explain anything."

"It explained most of their decisions at the time."

"What did my mum do?"

"Went to Hogsmeade."

Harry smiled. "She left him?"

"She said he might learn something if she didn't get him out of it."

"That sounds like Hermione."

"Lily would have liked her."

The words settled warmly and painfully at once. Harry traced the rim of his mug.

"Would she have liked Draconia?"

Lupin considered with enough seriousness to make Harry glad he had asked.

"I think Lily would have been wary at first," he said. "She knew what the Malfoy family represented during the war. But she judged people by what they chose when choosing became difficult."

"She would have liked her, then."

"Yes," Lupin said. "I think she would."

Harry returned to his book. The paragraph made sense on the next attempt. Near midday, Lupin accompanied him to Hagrid's hut. Frost crackled beneath their shoes, and their breath smoked in the air. Hagrid received them with tea, rock cakes and three young Nifflers which immediately escaped their pen and attempted to steal the buttons from Lupin's coat. Harry spent half an hour on the floor luring one of them from beneath Hagrid's bed with a brass buckle. Another climbed into his jumper and refused to emerge until Fang barked at it. It was an ordinary afternoon.

That should not have felt like an achievement. It did. Hogsmeade smelled of chimney smoke, wet stone and sugar. Hermione decided this within three minutes of passing beneath the painted village sign. The High Street curved between rows of crooked shops, their roofs steep enough to hold snow later in the year. Bright awnings snapped in the breeze. Cauldrons swung outside an apothecary, and a wooden sign above the Three Broomsticks creaked each time the painted mugs collided.

Students scattered in every direction. Draconia stopped in the middle of the road. Hermione nearly walked into her.

"What?"

"I am deciding where to begin."

"You said my list was excessive."

"Your list arranged leisure by estimated duration."

"That prevents wasted time."

"Leisure is the time being wasted."

Hermione frowned. "That makes no sense."

"It made perfect sense."

"Only grammatically."

Draconia turned towards Honeydukes.

Hermione caught her sleeve. "Zonko's is first."

"Honeydukes is closer."

"Only by thirty-seven feet."

"You measured?"

"I estimated."

"You calculated walking times last night."

"It was raining."

Draconia looked at her with reluctant fascination. "What does that have to do with anything?"

"I had time."

They began with Zonko's. Inside, shelves reached towards a ceiling crowded with enchanted objects. Dungbombs rattled inside locked glass jars. A display of Hiccough Sweets interrupted customers whenever they attempted to ask the price. Draconia discovered a silver whistle which released an insult in the voice of the nearest authority figure. When she blew it, the whistle declared in Lucius Malfoy's measured tones that Hermione's scarf had been tied without proper regard for symmetry. Draconia laughed so suddenly that she dropped it. Hermione purchased the whistle.

"For Harry?" Draconia asked.

"No."

"You cannot possibly intend to keep that."

Hermione blew it again. This time it used Professor McGonagall's voice to inform Draconia that smirking was not a substitute for personality. Hermione bought two. At Honeydukes they filled Harry's pouch with sugar quills, peppermint toads, fudge and a packet of sweets labelled Unpredictable Fruit, which changed flavour according to the eater's most recent thought. Draconia considered a display of expensive dark chocolates.

"He likes those," Hermione said.

"I know."

"You bought them after his Patronus lesson."

"I was present."

"He ate the whole bar."

"I noticed."

Hermione placed two bars into the basket. Draconia added a third. Neither commented. They carried their purchases to the Three Broomsticks and found a table beside the window. Madam Rosmerta set two foaming mugs of Butterbeer before them. Hermione took one sip.

Warmth travelled into her chest, rich with butterscotch and spice. Draconia watched her.

"What?"

"You like it."

"It's pleasant."

"You look surprised."

"I didn't know whether it would contain alcohol."

"It does."

Hermione put the mug down.

"A very small amount."

"How small?"

"You are not going to become intoxicated."

"That isn't an amount."

Draconia took a long drink.

Hermione narrowed her eyes. "You don't know, do you?"

"No."

Hermione reached for her bag.

"If you take out a book concerning beverage regulation, I will leave."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

Hermione removed the silver mirror instead. Draconia looked towards the clock.

"We contacted him twenty-three minutes ago."

"We agreed once every half-hour."

"We agreed no more than once every half-hour."

"There is a difference?"

"One is a limit. The other is a schedule."

Hermione turned the mirror between her hands. The surface reflected the crowded pub and the worry she had tried to hide since passing beyond Hogwarts' gates.

"What if he needs us?"

"He would call."

"What if the mirror fails?"

"We tested it."

"What if Sirius Black is inside the school?"

"Then the castle contains dozens of professors, hundreds of witnesses and Albus Dumbledore."

"Hogwarts contained all of those last year."

Draconia's expression changed. The noise of the pub receded beneath the memory neither of them needed to name. Hermione saw again the inside of a hospital ward from eyes which could not move. She remembered Harry's voice beside her bed and the terror beneath his attempt to sound normal. She remembered waking to learn that he had entered the Chamber alone. Draconia's thumb rubbed repeatedly over the silver fastening of her coat.

"Hogwarts also contained us," she said.

"We weren't enough."

"We were twelve."

"We still are."

"For another week in my case."

"That is not the defence you think it is."

Draconia looked through the window. "If we had stayed, he would have spent the entire day watching us pretend we weren't disappointed."

"I wouldn't have been disappointed."

"Yes, you would."

Hermione did not answer. Draconia leaned back in her chair.

"He asked us to go."

"He says what he thinks people need to hear."

"So do you."

"That's different."

"Because you believe you are usually correct?"

"Because I usually am."

Draconia smiled into her Butterbeer. Hermione pushed the mirror back into her bag.

"We'll call him in seven minutes."

"Ten."

"Eight."

"Nine."

"Fine."

At precisely nine minutes, the mirror filled with Harry's face. A Niffler clung to his hair. Draconia laughed so hard Butterbeer came out of her nose. Hermione remembered that moment for a very long time. Harry was in the entrance hall when the Hogsmeade party returned.

He had not been waiting for them. He had merely finished dinner unusually early, taken the most direct route towards Gryffindor Tower and stopped beside the doors for reasons which did not require examination. The oak panels opened upon a rush of cold air and noise. Students poured inside carrying striped Honeydukes bags and contraband from Zonko's. Their cheeks were pink from the wind. Mud marked their shoes. Every conversation appeared to occur at twice the necessary volume. Hermione came through the doors first.

Draconia followed with four bags suspended from one hand and a paper parcel held beneath her opposite arm. She saw Harry at once. Relief crossed her face before she replaced it with composure.

"You survived," Harry said.

"We faced considerable danger," Draconia replied. "Granger attempted to organise recreation."

"It was a suggested route."

"It had calculated toilet intervals."

"They were not calculated. They were marked as optional."

Harry took two of the bags. "Any casualties?"

"Only dignity," Draconia said.

Hermione's face went pinker.

Harry looked at her. "What happened?"

"Nothing."

"Draconia?"

"She discovered Butterbeer contains alcohol."

"I asked a reasonable question."

"She then attempted to determine whether she was drunk by reciting prime numbers."

"That is a recognised cognitive test."

"You forgot twenty-nine."

"I was distracted."

Harry grinned. "Scandalous."

"I was not drunk."

"You sound defensive."

Hermione shoved a Honeydukes bag into his chest.

"This is yours."

Inside lay enough sugar to endanger several organs. Harry recognised the chocolates Draconia had given him after his first lesson, two packets of fudge, three sugar quills, peppermint toads and a glass jar filled with pale sweets which rearranged themselves whenever he tilted it.

"You bought all this?"

"You provided money," Hermione said.

"Not this much money."

Draconia handed him the paper parcel. "Open it."

Harry peeled back the wrapping. A thick scarlet scarf fell across his hands. Tiny golden Snitches moved along its length, darting between the fibres whenever he touched them. For one irrational instant, the gift hurt. They had spent the day together in a place he could not enter. They had seen shops and streets he knew only from their descriptions. Now they stood before him full of stories which belonged to the two of them, offering proof that they had thought of him while living without him. The loneliness inside Harry twisted itself into resentment before he could stop it.

"You didn't have to buy my half of the village."

Hermione's smile faded.

"We wanted to."

"I already said I was fine."

Draconia's fingers tightened around one of the bag handles. "You also asked for surprises."

"I meant sweets."

"The scarf was my idea," Hermione said. "The one you have is too thin."

"It works."

"Your neck was red this morning."

"It was cold."

"That is generally what scarves prevent."

Harry heard the concern closing around him again. The entrance hall seemed smaller than it had a minute earlier.

"I spent one day alone," he said. "You don't need to compensate for it."

"We aren't compensating," Draconia replied.

"Aren't you?"

"No."

"Then stop looking at me like you expected to find a body."

The words silenced all three of them. Students moved around them towards the staircases, laughing and calling to friends. A parcel from Zonko's exploded near the doors, covering two Ravenclaws in blue feathers. Hermione looked down. Draconia's face became unreadable. Harry wanted to pull the sentence back. Instead, pride held his mouth closed.

"We should go," Draconia said.

She took the bags from him.

"You don't have to—"

"We need to reach the dungeons before curfew."

"It's six o'clock."

"We walk slowly."

Hermione remained beside Harry for half a second longer.

"He missed us," Draconia said without turning. "He is expressing it badly."

"I know," Hermione answered.

"I can hear both of you."

"That was the intention," Draconia said.

Harry watched them cross the hall. Hermione stopped halfway to the dungeon staircase, turned and came back. She seized the scarlet scarf from his hands, looped it around his neck and pulled one end hard enough to drag him forward.

"There," she said. "Now you can be ungrateful without developing a cold."

Then she followed Draconia. Harry stood beneath the floating candles wearing the scarf. One of the golden Snitches landed against his collarbone. He kept it on. By the end of the following week, the Dementor-shaped Boggart could cross half the room before Harry lost the memory supporting his spell.

This represented progress. Harry disliked progress which felt indistinguishable from failure.

"Again," he said.

Lupin stood beside the cupboard, one hand braced against it. He looked unusually pale. Shadows lay beneath his eyes, and he had paused twice during the lesson to catch his breath.

"That will be enough."

"It didn't reach the desk."

"It held for six seconds."

"It needed seven."

"Why?"

"Because seven would be more."

Lupin closed his eyes briefly. "An irrefutable argument."

Harry rolled his shoulders and lifted his wand. The Boggart floated before him, cold radiating across the carpet. Silver mist hung between them, thinning at the edges.

"Harry."

"I can do it."

"Your spell is weakening."

"Then let me strengthen it."

The hood turned towards him. His mother screamed. Harry caught the memory of the Leaky Cauldron before it could disappear. Hermione's cards struck the table. Draconia lunged. Crookshanks fled with Lucius's dinner.

"Expecto Patronum!"

Light erupted from his wand. The mist thickened into a curved barrier. It had no fixed shape, but it pushed the Boggart backwards by several feet. Triumph surged through Harry. The emotion shattered his concentration. The silver shield broke.

The Boggart rushed towards him. Lupin's wand cut through the air.

"Riddikulus!"

The black balloon burst against the cupboard. Harry dropped into a chair, breathing hard. Lupin locked the door and remained beside it for several seconds.

"You should sit down," Harry said.

"I'm perfectly capable of standing."

"You don't look it."

"An encouraging observation from a pupil."

Lupin returned to his desk. The movement lacked its usual ease. Harry accepted the chocolate offered to him but continued watching.

"Are you ill?"

"Only tired."

"You look ill."

"Thank you."

"Madam Pomfrey could—"

"I have seen Madam Pomfrey."

Harry stopped. Lupin unwrapped his own square of chocolate. The refusal had been polite and absolute. Harry recognised it because he used the same kind.

"You ask me questions," he said.

"I do."

"But I'm not allowed to ask you."

"You can ask. I may not answer everything."

"That seems unfair."

"It probably is."

Harry considered pushing. Hermione would have. Draconia would have approached from another direction and pretended the question belonged to someone else. He let it go. Lupin's shoulders loosened slightly.

"When does your next Quidditch match take place?" he asked.

"Saturday."

"Against Hufflepuff?"

"Yes."

"Cedric Diggory is their Seeker."

"I know."

"He is good."

"I know."

"James would have spent this conversation insisting the opposing Seeker had no talent."

"My dad was a Chaser."

"That never stopped him having an opinion."

Harry smiled. Lupin looked towards the Nimbus propped beside Harry's bag.

"You fly like him."

"People keep saying I look like him."

"You do. The resemblance becomes less important once you move."

"What does that mean?"

"Still photographs make inheritance appear simple. James's hair. Lily's eyes. A person becomes more complicated when they speak, choose and act."

Harry looked at his hands.

"So I'm not like either of them?"

"I didn't say that."

"What did I inherit?"

Lupin studied him.

"James always wanted to see what would happen if he tried," he said. "Lily noticed when someone was hurting, even when they said they weren't. You remind me of both of them."

Harry laughed. Lupin smiled, but fatigue dulled it quickly.

"You should go," he said. "Sleep before the match."

"Wood scheduled practice at six tomorrow morning."

"Then mutiny."

"He'd replace me."

"With whom?"

Harry considered the Gryffindor alternatives. "He'd play Seeker himself and Chaser at the same time."

"That sounds like James."

"Did Dad play through storms?"

"Your father once continued a match after lightning set his sleeve on fire."

"Did they win?"

"No."

Harry stared at him.

Lupin's smile returned. "That fact troubled him more than the burns."

Saturday morning arrived in a storm. Wind struck the towers hard enough to make the old windows complain. Rain swept almost horizontally across the grounds, turning the path towards the Quidditch pitch into brown water. The sky hung low and iron-grey above the stands. Harry could barely see the goalposts from the changing-room door.

"Excellent conditions," Wood announced.

The Gryffindor team stared at him. A gust of wind drove rain through the doorway and extinguished two lamps. Fred Weasley closed the door.

"Oliver," he said carefully, "what conditions would you describe as poor?"

"Fog."

"There is fog."

"Not enough to matter."

George pressed his face to the narrow window. "I can no longer see Fred."

"I'm behind you."

"Then it's worse than I thought."

Wood turned towards Harry. "You'll need to stay above the cloud line when possible. Diggory is heavier, so the wind should affect him less."

"That sounds like an advantage for him," Harry said.

"Only if you let it."

"Brilliant. I'll ask the weather to show favouritism."

Alicia tightened the straps of her gloves. "It already supports Hufflepuff. Everything is yellow."

"Lightning isn't."

"Give it time," Angelina said.

Harry tried to eat half an energy bar Hermione had placed in his hand at breakfast. It had the texture of compressed sawdust and tasted faintly of raisins. He pushed the remainder into his pocket. Wood began his final instructions. His voice faded beneath the wind. Harry's Nimbus stood against the bench, polished and ready. He placed one hand around the handle.

Flying had always reduced the world. On the ground, there were corridors behind him, dangers ahead and people he might fail in every direction. In the air, there was only balance, speed and the next choice. Fear became useful. It sharpened his sight and steadied his hands. That simplicity had begun to change. Now the sky contained Dementors. Harry tightened his grip.

He had seen them only once since the train, dark figures positioned beyond the school gates. Even at that distance, their presence had turned the air bitter. Dumbledore had forbidden them from entering the grounds, and the Headmaster's order had held. Harry had still begun searching the horizon whenever he mounted his broom. The changing-room door opened. Hermione slipped inside beneath Wood's arm.

"You can't be in here," Wood said.

"I need Harry."

"Everyone needs Harry. We have a match."

Draconia appeared behind her wearing a green cloak and a Slytherin scarf. Wood's expression darkened.

"You definitely can't be in here."

"My father sponsors your equipment."

"He sponsors Slytherin's equipment."

"Not exclusively."

"Out."

Hermione ignored him and approached Harry. Her hair had doubled in size in the damp, and raindrops clung to her eyelashes.

"Professor Lupin isn't here," she said quietly.

Harry looked towards the open door.

"He doesn't attend every match."

"He said he would come."

"Maybe he's ill."

Hermione's mouth tightened.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've realised something and decided no one else is sensible enough to hear it."

Draconia joined them.

"Granger has been doing that face since breakfast."

"I have not."

"You looked at the lunar chart during toast."

Hermione elbowed her.

"We are leaving," Hermione said.

"Are we?"

"Yes."

Draconia reached into her cloak and handed Harry a small square of silver-wrapped chocolate.

"For after," she said.

"I have six pieces in my bag."

"This one is better."

"Is expensive chocolate more effective against soul damage?"

"It is more effective against poverty."

"That didn't answer the question."

Wood seized the door.

"Malfoy. Granger. Leave before I have you removed."

Hermione looked at Harry.

"Remember the charm."

"I know."

"If they come near the grounds—"

"They won't."

"You cannot know that."

Dread tightened briefly around Harry's lungs. Draconia saw it and glared at Hermione.

"Excellent preparation," she said. "Perhaps describe every possible method of falling next."

Hermione's face fell.

"I didn't mean—"

"I'll remember," Harry said.

The teams were called onto the pitch. Noise struck them first. Hundreds of voices rose from the stands, thinned and distorted by the storm. Gryffindor banners snapped in the wind. Students huddled beneath umbrellas which repeatedly turned inside out. The commentator's voice crackled through the magical megaphone, losing every fourth word to thunder. The rain soaked Harry before he had crossed half the grass. At the centre of the pitch, Madam Hooch waited beside the crate of balls with her robes plastered to her body. Cedric Diggory stood opposite Harry, broad-shouldered and steady despite the wind.

He offered Harry his hand.

"Good luck."

"You too."

Cedric glanced towards the clouds. "If either of us sees the Snitch, shout."

"So the other person can catch it?"

"So we know it still exists."

Harry grinned despite himself. The whistle blew. Fourteen brooms rose. Wind seized Harry immediately and pushed him sideways. He leaned hard against it, forcing the Nimbus upwards while the pitch dropped beneath him. Rain struck his glasses. The world blurred into green, gold and grey.

Below, Katie Bell took possession of the Quaffle. A Hufflepuff Chaser cut across her path. Fred drove a Bludger between them, and the ball vanished into the storm with a metallic crack. Harry wiped his glasses against his sleeve. It made no difference. He climbed higher. The air grew colder. His wet robes dragged against his shoulders, and each breath tasted of rain. Cedric circled near the opposite goalposts, a dark shape among the clouds.

Ten points to Hufflepuff. Then ten to Gryffindor. The score emerged in fragments from the commentary. Harry searched. A flash of gold appeared near the base of the Hufflepuff stands.

He dived. The wind changed. His broom dropped twenty feet in a single sickening plunge. Harry flattened himself against the handle, corrected and saw Cedric descending from the opposite direction. The gold vanished. A Bludger tore past Harry's ear.

George shouted an apology which the storm carried away. Harry climbed again. From the Slytherin stand, Draconia followed his movement until her neck hurt. She disliked watching Harry play. This opinion had developed over two years and strengthened every time he regarded the broom beneath him as a suggestion rather than an object governed by physical law.

Beside her, Hermione gripped the railing with both hands.

"You're bending it," Draconia said.

"What?"

"The metal."

Hermione released one hand. The railing remained slightly curved.

"Did you see the Snitch?"

"No."

"Could Harry?"

"He is closer."

"That doesn't mean he can see."

"He has glasses."

"Which are covered in rain."

Draconia drew her wand.

Hermione caught her wrist. "You cannot charm them during a match."

"I was going to clear the rain."

"That counts as outside assistance."

"No one would notice."

"I would."

"That is consistently the problem."

Thunder rolled above the pitch. Hermione looked towards the staff stand. Professor Dumbledore sat in the centre, silver beard bright against his dark robes. McGonagall leaned forwards beside him. Snape occupied the end of the row and appeared to be watching Draconia rather than the players. The seat reserved for Lupin remained empty. Hermione's fingers found the chain beneath her scarf.

Draconia noticed.

"You are not using that."

"I wasn't going to."

"You touched it."

"It's a necklace. People touch necklaces."

"Not when they are calculating whether an extra hour could improve a Quidditch match."

Hermione lowered her hand. Draconia returned her attention to the sky. Harry disappeared into cloud. Her body responded before thought. Her wand came free. Her pulse struck painfully beneath her jaw. Then he emerged, banking sharply around a goalpost.

Hermione breathed out. Neither commented upon the other's reaction. On the pitch, the score reached sixty-fifty to Hufflepuff. Harry lost feeling in his fingers. The Snitch appeared twice more. Both times the wind carried it out of sight before he could close the distance. Cedric remained near him now, each Seeker using the other's attention as a guide.

Alicia scored. The Gryffindor stand erupted. Lightning split the sky beyond the forest. For half a second, the entire world became white. In that white light, Harry saw movement beyond the pitch.

Not above it.

Below.

Dark figures glided from the direction of the gates. His stomach dropped. The light vanished. Harry turned his broom. At first he counted three.

Then six. Then more emerged from the rain, black cloaks streaming behind them. They crossed the boundary without sound and gathered beneath the goalposts. The storm swallowed the spectators' reaction. Cold reached Harry even at this height. His wet robes stiffened against his skin. The broom handle became slick beneath fingers which no longer seemed connected to him.

The Snitch flashed near Cedric's shoulder. Cedric dived. Harry did not follow. A scream rose through the wind. Not from the stands.

From inside him. The pitch disappeared behind a narrow staircase and a bedroom door which would not open. His mother ran. Harry felt her bare feet strike the floor. He felt the child clutched to her chest and the impossible weight of knowing there was nowhere left to go.

"Not Harry."

Harry's wand was inside his robes. He tried to reach it. His hand would not move. Far below, the Dementors lifted their faces.

"Please. Not Harry."

Draconia saw his broom stop. Every other player continued moving around him. Cedric plunged after a speck of gold. The Chasers crossed the centre line. Harry remained suspended high above them, one hand hanging uselessly at his side. Then his body folded.

"Harry!"

Draconia climbed over the railing. Hermione seized the back of her cloak. Harry slipped from the broom. For one terrible instant, he remained beside it in the air. Then he fell.

The sound left the stadium. Draconia tore free and jumped from the stand. She did not remember drawing her broom from beneath the bench. She did not remember mounting it or kicking away from the wooden platform. One second she stood beside Hermione; the next the wind struck her face and the ground dropped beneath her. Harry fell through the rain. His arms did not move.

Draconia drove the broom down. She would not reach him. The knowledge arrived with perfect clarity. She leaned forwards until the handle shook beneath her.

"Harry!"

His name broke uselessly against the storm. Above the pitch, Dumbledore rose. His wand cut through the air.

"Arresto Momentum!"

The spell struck Harry thirty feet above the ground. His fall slowed violently. His body turned, robes snapping around him, and drifted the final distance into the mud. Draconia landed badly enough to tear both knees of her trousers. She abandoned the broom and ran. Hermione reached the pitch from the opposite side, having taken the stairs quickly enough to send two students sprawling. She slipped in the mud, recovered and continued. Players descended around Harry.

Madam Hooch shouted for everyone to stand back. Draconia dropped beside him. His glasses had been thrown several feet away. Rain ran over his closed face, catching in his eyelashes and filling the shallow hollow beneath his cheek. She pressed two fingers to his neck. A pulse answered.

"Harry."

He did not move. Hermione fell to her knees opposite her.

"He's breathing," Draconia said.

"I can see that."

Hermione's hands hovered over him, unable to decide where touching would help rather than harm.

"Why isn't he waking?"

"I don't know."

"Harry."

Dumbledore crossed the pitch towards them. He did not run. He did not need to. The fury upon his face seemed to drive the storm away from his path. He lifted his wand towards the Dementors. Silver light exploded across the grounds. It did not resemble mist. It struck the darkness with the force of a living thing, enormous and winged, driving the creatures backwards. Cloaks twisted beneath the impact. One by one, they retreated beyond the boundary and vanished into the rain.

Warmth returned too slowly. Professor McGonagall reached Harry and knelt in the mud without regard for her robes.

"Stand back," she ordered.

Draconia did not move.

"Miss Malfoy."

"No."

McGonagall looked at her.

"He is alive," Draconia said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. "I am not in your way."

Hermione took Harry's hand. His fingers were colder than the rain. The whistle sounded above them. Somewhere near the Hufflepuff goalposts, Cedric held the Snitch. He stared down at the scene below, victory forgotten in his face. No one cheered.

A groan of splintering wood rolled across the grounds. Harry's Nimbus, abandoned to the wind, struck the Whomping Willow. The tree's branches descended. Wood snapped. By the time Madam Pomfrey levitated Harry towards the castle, the broom had been reduced to pieces.

Harry woke to white curtains and the taste of chocolate. For several seconds, he did not know where he was. Rain struck tall windows. Firelight moved across the hospital-wing ceiling. Every muscle in his body ached, but none with the sharpness of injury. Someone gripped his right hand. Someone else had fallen asleep against his left shoulder.

Harry moved his fingers. Hermione's head lifted immediately.

"You're awake."

Draconia jerked upright beside the bed. Her hair had dried badly, leaving pale strands tangled around her face. Mud streaked one cheek. A dark bruise spread beneath the skin of her jaw. Harry looked from one girl to the other.

"Did we win?"

Hermione stared at him.

"You fell nearly fifty feet."

"That wasn't the question."

"No," Draconia said. Her voice was rough. "Diggory caught the Snitch."

Harry considered this.

"Then the fall was badly timed."

Hermione made a sound which might have been a laugh if it had not broken halfway through. She struck his arm.

"Ow."

"That did not hurt."

"It might have."

"You are impossible."

"I'm conscious."

"That is not the standard we're using anymore."

Madam Pomfrey swept through the curtains before Harry could ask what the new standard involved. She examined his eyes, his pulse, his ribs and the back of his skull with increasingly disapproving movements.

"No fractures," she announced. "No concussion. Professor Dumbledore slowed your fall before impact."

"Can I leave?"

"No."

"When?"

"When I am satisfied that your experience with the Dementors has produced no lasting physical consequences."

"How long does that take?"

"Longer each time you ask."

Harry closed his mouth. Madam Pomfrey looked towards Hermione and Draconia.

"Five minutes. Then out."

Neither argued. This worried Harry more than anything else. Pomfrey closed the curtains behind her. Silence filled the space. Harry shifted against the pillows.

"My broom?"

Hermione looked down. The answer reached him before she spoke.

"The wind carried it into the Whomping Willow."

"How bad?"

Draconia reached beside the bed and lifted a cloth-wrapped bundle no longer than Harry's forearm. She placed it across his knees. Harry unfolded one corner. Splintered mahogany lay inside. The tail twigs had been snapped into pieces, and the once-smooth handle ended in a jagged break beneath the fading gold letters. NIMBUS TWO THOU—

Harry covered it again. The broom had been his first real birthday present. McGonagall had arranged it when he knew her only as a severe professor who transformed into cats and disapproved of reckless flying. It had carried him above the castle when the ground felt unbearable. It had obeyed him through rain, wind and enchanted Bludgers. Now it fitted inside a parcel.

"I'm sorry," Hermione whispered.

Harry swallowed.

"It's a broom."

Neither girl believed him. He pushed the bundle aside.

"What happened to the Dementors?"

"Dumbledore drove them away," Hermione said. "The Ministry claimed they entered because someone reported Sirius Black near the edge of Hogsmeade."

Harry thought of the enormous black dog beyond the gates during the summer.

"Was he there?"

"No one found him."

"So the Dementors broke into the grounds because someone might have seen him."

Draconia's mouth hardened. "Father will be contacting the Minister."

"That should terrify him."

"It will."

Harry tried to sit higher. Both girls moved at once. Hermione reached for the pillows. Draconia placed a hand against his shoulder. The reaction sent a flash of irritation through him.

"I can sit up."

"We know," Hermione said.

"Then let me."

Draconia removed her hand, but she remained close enough to catch him. Harry pushed himself upright. The effort made the room tilt briefly. Hermione noticed.

"You're dizzy."

"I moved too quickly."

"You need to lie down."

"I've been unconscious. That's enough lying down."

Draconia reached for the water jug. Harry's irritation sharpened.

"I can pour it."

"I was already standing."

"You weren't."

"I was about to."

"Stop."

Draconia froze.

Hermione looked at him. "Stop what?"

"Hovering."

"We are sitting."

"You know what I mean."

"You fell out of the sky," Draconia said.

"I remember."

"No, you don't. You were unconscious before you hit the ground."

"I'm awake now."

"That does not make what happened less serious."

Harry swung his legs towards the edge of the bed. Hermione stood in front of him.

"Where are you going?"

"Nowhere."

"Then put your feet back."

"I'm testing them."

"You can test them tomorrow."

"Why?"

"Because Madam Pomfrey told you to remain in bed."

"Madam Pomfrey thinks breathing is a supervised activity."

"This isn't funny."

"I didn't say it was."

Hermione's face had gone pale. Draconia still held the water jug, though her grip had turned her knuckles white. Harry saw their fear. He knew he should be gentle with it. Instead, every locked door of the summer seemed to close at once.

"You don't get to decide when I can stand."

"We are not deciding—" Hermione began.

"You're standing between me and the floor."

"Because you nearly died!"

"I didn't."

"We watched you fall," Draconia said.

Her voice cut through the room. Harry looked at her. The controlled mask had fractured. Terror remained beneath it, fresh and furious. Mud darkened the torn knees of her trousers. Harry realised she had not changed since the match.

"I watched you stop moving," she continued. "I called your name and you did not hear me. I flew after you and I could not reach you."

"You flew onto the pitch?"

"I wasn't going to remain in the stands."

"You could have been hurt."

The hypocrisy of the sentence reached all three of them. Draconia laughed once, without humour.

"Yes," she said. "How unbearable."

Harry looked away.

Hermione's voice softened. "We thought you were dead."

"I thought both of you were dead last year."

Hermione flinched. The anger should have stopped there. It did not.

"I thought Hermione was never going to wake up. I found Draco on the Chamber floor and didn't know whether I'd reached her in time. I know what it's like. I still didn't demand permission every time either of you wanted to cross a room."

"No," Draconia said. "You simply watched us whenever we did."

Harry's head snapped towards her. She put down the jug before her shaking hand spilled it.

"You count how long Hermione spends between classes," Draconia said. "You look at me every time ink touches parchment. You wake when either of us moves near your bed. You are no less frightened than we are, Harry. You are only quieter about it."

"That's different."

"It is happening to you, so you believe it is."

Hermione wiped at her face angrily.

"We aren't trying to lock you anywhere."

"It feels like it."

The words hung between them. Draconia went still. Harry saw the barred window reflected in her expression. He remembered her standing beneath it, pale with fury, refusing to leave until he had been brought outside.

"I opened those bars," she said.

Regret struck him immediately.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"Because you are speaking to me like I put them there."

"I didn't mean—"

Madam Pomfrey pulled back the curtain. Her gaze moved from Harry sitting at the edge of the bed to Hermione's wet face and Draconia's bloodless one.

"Out," she said.

Neither girl argued. Hermione gathered her bag. Draconia lifted the broken Nimbus from Harry's knees and placed it carefully upon the bedside table. At the edge of the curtain, Hermione stopped.

"We'll tell Professor McGonagall you're awake."

Harry nodded. Draconia did not look back. The curtains closed. Harry remained sitting with his feet above the floor. He no longer wanted to stand.

The hospital wing grew dark around him. Madam Pomfrey brought dinner. Harry moved the food across his plate and ate enough to prevent her from commenting. She removed the tray, lowered the lamps and told him to sleep. He did not. Whenever his eyes closed, he fell again. Sometimes he landed in the Chamber. Sometimes he struck the Privet Drive pavement while Vernon watched from the garden. Once, Draconia flew after him and disappeared beneath a sea of black robes before Harry could draw his wand.

He opened his eyes. Rain trailed down the windows. The broken Nimbus lay upon the table, catching narrow lines of firelight along its splintered handle. Near midnight, the hospital doors opened. Harry reached for his wand.

Professor Lupin entered. He walked slowly, one hand resting briefly against the doorframe. His face was grey with exhaustion, and a fresh cut crossed his left cheek. He looked worse than he had during their lesson. Harry lowered his wand.

"You missed the match."

"So I heard."

Lupin approached the bed and placed a bar of chocolate upon the table.

"I have enough chocolate."

"I have been reliably informed that this is impossible."

Harry looked towards the curtained beds around them.

"Madam Pomfrey said visitors had to leave."

"She makes an exception for staff members who know where she keeps the spare keys."

"That doesn't sound like an exception."

"No."

Lupin sat. For a while, neither spoke. Harry expected questions about the Dementors. Instead, Lupin examined the broken broom.

"Your father broke one in almost the same place," he said.

"Against the Whomping Willow?"

"Against the Astronomy Tower."

"How?"

"He attempted to fly through an open window."

"Was it open?"

"Not enough."

Harry almost smiled. Lupin leaned back, though the movement appeared to hurt.

"I spoke with Professor Dumbledore," he said. "The Dementors have been returned to the outer boundary. He has made it clear to the Ministry that another intrusion will not be tolerated."

"They were already forbidden."

"Yes."

"That worked well."

"No," Lupin said. "It did not."

The agreement left Harry with nowhere to direct his anger.

"They heard her again," he said.

"Your mother?"

Harry nodded.

"I couldn't reach my wand."

"The Dementors affected you before you recognised their presence."

"I should have noticed."

"You were flying through a storm while searching for an object the size of a walnut."

"I've done harder things."

"That does not make this easy."

"If I'd learned the charm faster—"

"You have attended three lessons."

"They were enough to prove I can't do it."

Lupin's expression sharpened.

"No."

Harry looked at him.

"They were enough to prove that a thirteen-year-old cannot master exceptionally difficult defensive magic in several afternoons," Lupin said. "That is not a failure of character."

"Dumbledore did it."

"Dumbledore has been practising magic for longer than our combined lifetimes."

"He stopped all of them."

"Yes."

"I couldn't stop one that wasn't even real."

"You held it back."

"For six seconds."

"Fourteen, during your last attempt."

Harry had not known Lupin had counted.

"It still got through."

"And you stayed on your feet."

"Not today."

Lupin's voice gentled. "Today was not a lesson."

Harry looked towards the window.

"The girls were frightened."

"I saw them downstairs."

Guilt moved beneath Harry's ribs.

"Are they still there?"

"Yes."

"Pomfrey sent them away."

"They have interpreted 'away' broadly. Miss Granger is in the corridor. Miss Malfoy is attempting to convince a suit of armour to permit access through an unused service door."

"That won't work."

"I did not tell her."

Harry picked at a loose thread in the blanket.

"I said they made me feel locked in."

Lupin was quiet.

"I know they aren't the Dursleys," Harry said. "I know they're trying to help."

"Knowing why a person is frightened does not make their fear comfortable to carry."

"I hurt them."

"Yes."

Harry looked up, surprised by the directness. Lupin's expression remained kind.

"You were frightened too," he said. "That explains the cruelty. It does not erase it."

"I wasn't trying to be cruel."

"Most people aren't. They simply try to escape their own pain and fail to notice who they push into it."

Harry thought of Draconia's face when she said she had opened the bars.

"How do I fix it?"

"You apologise."

"That seems too easy."

"An apology is not meant to purchase immediate forgiveness. It is meant to tell the truth about what you did."

Harry sank back against the pillows.

"They keep watching me."

"Yes."

"They keep deciding what I need."

"Yes."

"I hate it."

"You are allowed to."

"Even if they're right?"

"Especially then. Being correct does not grant anyone ownership of you."

Harry turned this over.

"But they watched me fall."

"And you watched both of them nearly die. The three of you have begun treating vigilance as proof of love."

"That sounds bad when you say it."

"It is not entirely bad. You kept one another alive."

Lupin leaned forwards, forearms resting upon his knees.

"But a friendship cannot survive forever in a state of rescue. Sooner or later, you must learn to sit beside someone's fear without obeying it."

"How?"

"Badly, at first."

Harry gave him a doubtful look.

"I'm not sure there's a neat answer," Lupin said. "Tell them when it gets too much. Give them a chance to listen. But you'll have to listen too, Harry. They can't know what you need if you tell them nothing."

Harry looked away.

"That part seems unrelated."

"It does not."

"Did they complain to you?"

"No. Miss Malfoy threatened me."

"About what?"

"Allowing the Dementor exercise to continue before you were ready."

"She doesn't know whether I was ready."

"Neither do you."

Harry pulled the blanket higher.

"Whose side are you on?"

"Tonight? Madam Pomfrey's. She has stronger potions than any of us."

That earned a brief laugh. Lupin stood. His knees nearly failed beneath him. Harry reached instinctively towards him. Lupin caught the bedframe and steadied himself.

"You really are ill."

"I will recover."

"You say that like me."

"I was afraid you might notice."

Harry studied the fresh cut on his face. There were questions gathering around Lupin: his monthly absences, the lunar chart Hermione had touched at breakfast, the exhaustion which no ordinary cold could explain. Harry did not ask. Lupin had left him one uncertainty without forcing it open. Harry could do the same.

"Will we continue the lessons?" he asked.

"When Madam Pomfrey releases you and not one hour sooner."

"She'll keep me here forever if she hears that."

"I have faith in your ability to irritate her into discharging you."

At the door, Lupin paused.

"Harry."

"Yeah?"

"You fell today because creatures designed to consume hope entered a place in which you had every right to feel safe. You did not fall because you are weak."

The door closed behind him. Harry listened to the quiet ward. A few minutes later, metal crashed in the corridor. Draconia swore. Madam Pomfrey shouted.

Harry covered his face with both hands and began to laugh. Madam Pomfrey released him after breakfast. Hermione and Draconia waited outside. Neither approached when he emerged. Hermione held Harry's schoolbag. Draconia carried the broken Nimbus beneath one arm. Between them remained a careful distance, wide enough for Harry to decide whether he wished to enter it.

He did.

"Can we go somewhere private?" he asked.

They found an unused classroom on the third floor. Dust softened the desks. A faded diagram of a Grindylow remained upon the blackboard, its many-fingered hands reaching towards an explanation no one had bothered to erase. Morning light fell through the windows and divided the floor into pale rectangles. Harry closed the door. Hermione placed his bag upon a desk. Draconia set down the broom.

"I'm sorry," Harry said.

He had practised several versions during the night. All of them disappeared now. Hermione folded her arms, but not angrily. It looked more like she needed to hold herself together.

"I shouldn't have compared you to them," Harry continued. "Neither of you put bars on anything. You came to take them off."

Draconia watched him.

"And I shouldn't have said you were treating me like I was already dead."

"No," Hermione said. "You shouldn't."

"I know."

"We watched you fall."

"I know."

"I keep seeing it."

Harry moved closer.

Hermione's voice wavered. "One second you were there, and then you weren't holding the broom anymore."

"I know."

"You cannot keep saying that."

"I don't know what else to say."

"You could say you'll be careful."

Draconia looked at her. "He cannot promise never to fall."

"I know that."

"He cannot promise never to be hurt."

"I know."

Hermione's anger collapsed.

"That is the problem," she whispered.

Harry understood. He had known since the Chamber. Perhaps earlier. Loving people did not grant him the ability to keep them alive. It only taught his mind new ways to imagine losing them. He stopped in front of Hermione.

"I'll tell you when I'm frightened," he said. "I'll try, anyway."

Hermione looked up at him.

"And if I need help, I'll ask."

"You never ask," Draconia said.

"That's why it'll be impressive."

She did not smile. Harry turned towards her.

"But you can't decide where I'm allowed to go, or whether I'm allowed to fly, or when I can stand up."

"We can object," she said.

"Of course."

"Repeatedly."

"Within reason."

"Whose reason?"

"Hermione's."

Both Harry and Draconia looked at Hermione.

Hermione blinked. "Why mine?"

"You enjoy rules," Harry said.

"That does not make me an impartial authority."

"No," Draconia agreed. "But it ensures written records."

A reluctant smile pulled at Hermione's mouth. Harry looked down at the broken broom.

"I was frightened too," he admitted. "Before the match. I kept looking for Dementors."

"Why didn't you tell us?" Hermione asked.

"Because you were already frightened."

"That was not helpful."

"I realise that now."

Draconia stepped closer.

"You do the same thing we do," she said.

"Probably."

"You watch."

"Yes."

"You count."

Harry thought of the minutes Hermione spent between lessons. The number of times Draconia touched her satchel during meals. The way he searched every room for both of them before allowing himself to relax.

"Yes."

"Then we all stop," Draconia said.

Hermione shook her head. "We won't."

Draconia frowned.

"We should," Hermione continued, "but we won't simply decide to and succeed immediately."

"That is very pessimistic."

"It is accurate."

"So what do we do?" Harry asked.

Hermione looked at each of them.

"We notice when we're doing it."

"And?" Draconia asked.

"We tell one another."

"That sounds irritating."

"Then it should suit us."

Harry held out his hand.

Hermione stared at it. "What is that?"

"I don't know. Agreement?"

"This is not a business transaction," she said.

Draconia placed her hand over his.

"Everything is improved by ceremony."

Hermione rolled her eyes and added hers. For several seconds they stood amid the dusty desks, hands stacked awkwardly between them.

"This feels ridiculous," Harry said.

"It was your idea," Hermione replied.

"I know."

Draconia's fingers tightened briefly against his. Then they separated. Hermione lifted the scarlet scarf from Harry's bag.

"You left this in the hospital wing."

"I was unconscious."

"You were wearing it before the match."

"It was raining."

"It is still cold."

Harry bent his head and permitted her to wrap it around his neck. Draconia adjusted the knot.

"Too tight," Harry said.

"It prevents complaints."

"I can still speak."

"An unfortunate design flaw."

They left the classroom together. Professor Lupin cancelled the following Thursday's lesson. A note arrived at breakfast in handwriting shakier than usual.

Harry,

I am afraid I am not well enough to teach this afternoon. We will continue next week. In the meantime, practise the incantation and memory without attempting to produce the charm. The distinction is important.

R. J. Lupin

Hermione read it over Harry's shoulder.

"Madam Pomfrey is taking him potions."

Harry folded the note. "How do you know?"

"I saw her carrying them."

"When?"

Hermione reached for her tea too quickly. Draconia looked between them.

"Which time?" she asked.

Hermione kicked her beneath the table. Draconia's spoon struck her bowl. Several Gryffindors turned.

Harry lowered his voice. "You said you weren't using the Time Turner outside lessons."

"I'm not."

"You've missed breakfast twice this week."

"I attended both times."

"Exactly," Draconia said.

Hermione glared at her. Harry watched the shadows beneath Hermione's eyes.

"We're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Counting."

The three of them fell silent. Hermione buttered a piece of toast.

"I am attending the subjects Professor McGonagall approved," she said. "I am sleeping."

"How much?" Draconia asked.

Hermione's knife stopped.

"Objection," Harry said.

Draconia looked at him.

"You were counting."

"I was asking."

"With numbers."

"All questions concerning sleep eventually involve numbers."

Hermione's mouth twitched. The tension loosened but did not vanish. Harry folded Lupin's note and slipped it into his pocket. Across the Great Hall, Snape entered through the staff door carrying a goblet which steamed despite the cool room. He placed it before Lupin's empty chair, spoke quietly to Dumbledore and left without taking breakfast. Draconia followed every movement.

She said nothing. Neither did Harry. Some mysteries could remain unopened for one more week. When the next Thursday arrived, Lupin looked recovered. Not well, exactly. His face remained lined and pale, but his movements had regained their usual steadiness. The cut upon his cheek had faded into a thin mark.

He had moved the lesson to an unused classroom on the fifth floor. The room was larger than his office and nearly empty. Moonlight entered through high windows. A single desk stood beside the wall bearing water, chocolate and the familiar wooden case. Harry stopped inside the doorway.

"You said we would continue slowly."

"We will."

"That case suggests otherwise."

"The Boggart is not inside."

"What is?"

"A precaution."

Harry approached it carefully. Lupin opened the lid. The case contained a folded black cloak. Harry waited.

"That's it?"

"What were you expecting?"

"Teeth."

"Another week, perhaps."

Lupin lifted the cloak. "Tonight, you will practise the charm while I introduce distractions. Cold. Darkness. Movement. Nothing capable of reaching into your memories."

"That seems almost sensible."

"I have been told it happens occasionally."

Harry moved to the centre of the room. Lupin extinguished half the lamps. He opened the windows enough for October air to enter and charmed the cloak to rise. It floated at the far end of the classroom. Harry's body tensed despite knowing it was empty.

"Choose your memory," Lupin said.

Harry returned to the evening at the Leaky Cauldron. Cards. Rain. Stolen ham. Hermione laughing. The cloak drifted forwards.

"Expecto Patronum."

Silver mist emerged. It spread into a narrow wall. Lupin sent a blast of cold across the floor. Harry's feet went numb, but the memory remained. The cloak moved closer. Harry strengthened his grip.

The mist wavered. Hermione's laughter began to fade beneath the scream his mind expected. There was no scream. Only rain against the Leaky Cauldron windows.

"Again," Lupin said.

Harry drew breath.

"Expecto Patronum!"

Light filled the room. The mist thickened, bright enough to cast shadows behind the desks. It moved forwards, met the floating cloak and forced it back. Harry's heart leapt. The light held. One breath.

Two.

Three.

Within the silver vapour, lines began to gather. They rose higher than Harry's head, branching briefly through the air before losing definition. Lupin stopped moving. Harry heard his breath catch. The shape dissolved. Mist scattered across the room and vanished against the windows.

Harry lowered his wand.

"Did you see that?"

"Yes."

"What was it?"

Lupin looked towards the place the silver branches had disappeared.

"Not yet."

"That looked like part of something."

"It was."

"What?"

"A Patronus becomes fully corporeal only when the caster can sustain both the magic and the memory. Guessing its form before then will not help."

"You know what it was."

"I suspect."

"That is an irritating answer."

"I learned from your mother."

Harry smiled despite his frustration. Lupin relit the lamps. Warmth returned to the room, gradual and golden.

"You did very well."

"It wasn't a Dementor."

"No."

"It wasn't even a Boggart."

"No."

"So it doesn't count."

Lupin faced him.

"Why are you so determined to make every success smaller?"

Harry opened his mouth. No answer arrived. Lupin folded the black cloak.

"You expect your happiness to be challenged," he said. "The moment it appears, you search for the thing which will take it from you."

"That's not—"

"It is not unreasonable. Your life has taught you to do it."

Harry looked at the cooling chocolate upon the desk.

"Hogwarts was supposed to be different."

"From Privet Drive?"

"Yes."

"And now it is not?"

Harry thought of Quirrell beneath the school. The basilisk in the pipes. Hermione behind a curtain. Draconia unconscious beside the diary. Dementors gliding beneath the Quidditch stands.

"It still is," he said. "But not in the way I thought."

Lupin waited.

"I thought I'd left all the bad things there," Harry continued. "At the Dursleys. Then every year there's another thing here waiting to kill someone. Every time it gets better, I start wondering how long it has."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is."

"Do Hermione and Draconia know?"

"Some of it."

"Which part do they not know?"

Harry rubbed his thumb against his wand.

"That sometimes I wish I'd never found any of it."

Lupin's expression did not change. Harry forced himself to continue.

"Not Hogwarts. I don't mean that. I just—before, I didn't know what I was missing. I didn't have anyone for Voldemort or a monster or Sirius Black to take away."

"You believe loving them created the danger."

"No. I know it didn't."

"But part of you believes that caring less would hurt less."

Harry stared at the floor.

"Would it?"

"Yes," Lupin said.

Harry looked up. Lupin did not soften the answer.

"It would hurt less," he continued. "You would also live less. The mind often offers numbness as if it were safety. It is not. It is only a quieter form of suffering."

Harry thought of summer days passing without shape. Meals he had not tasted. Letters which had stopped arriving and the awful ease with which he had begun believing that no more would come.

"What if I can't stop expecting it?" he asked.

"The loss?"

Harry nodded.

"Then do not build the Patronus from a promise that loss will never come," Lupin said. "Build it from the knowledge that fear has not yet taken everything."

He gestured towards Harry's wand.

"Choose another memory."

"I already used the best one."

"No. You used the safest."

Harry frowned.

"What memory arrived first when you began learning the charm?"

"Flying."

"Before you discarded it."

Harry thought back to the crowded, painful rush of images. The Great Hall. Hagrid's cake. Hermione sleeping in the library. Draconia laughing.

All of them had sharpened into fear almost immediately. One remained. It was not a grand memory. No monster had been defeated. No adult had offered him a home. Nobody had promised forever. It had happened during the previous winter, before Hermione was Petrified and before Draconia disappeared. They had been in the library preparing for exams which none of them truly believed mattered.

Hermione had attempted a page-turning charm upon three open books. The spell had caught every book on the surrounding shelves instead. Thousands of pages began flipping at once. The resulting wind scattered their parchment, blew out three lamps and sent Madam Pince charging towards them with murder in her eyes. Draconia had tried to preserve her dignity until a dictionary opened and closed repeatedly around the end of her plait. Then she had snorted. The sound was tiny and entirely undignified. Harry had laughed.

Hermione had stared at them both in betrayal before she began laughing too. They fled the library with Madam Pince shouting behind them, papers streaming from their bags and no idea where they were going. Nothing terrible had followed. They had simply been thirteen floors from where they meant to be, breathless and together. Harry closed his eyes. He let the memory fill in around him.

Dust. Paper. Warm lamplight. Draconia trying to free her hair from an offended dictionary. Hermione's horrified face when the first shelf began turning its own pages. Their laughter rose. It did not need to promise that they would never be hurt. It only needed to have happened. Harry raised his wand.

"Expecto Patronum."

Silver light poured into the room. Not mist this time. It surged from the wand in a broad, luminous wave, curving around Harry before driving forwards. The floating cloak snapped away and struck the far wall. Every shadow vanished. Moonlight disappeared beneath a brighter, living silver. Harry held the memory. The light gathered.

Four slender legs touched the floor. A narrow body began to form between him and the darkness. Above it, silver lines rose and branched like winter trees. The shape lasted only an instant. Then it broke into sparks. They drifted slowly through the classroom, reflecting in the windows and catching upon Lupin's astonished face.

Harry lowered his wand. His hand shook with effort. Neither spoke until the final spark went out.

"That," Harry said breathlessly, "was more than six seconds."

Lupin looked at him. His eyes shone in the fading light, grief and wonder held so closely together that Harry could not separate them.

"Yes," Lupin said. "It was."

"What was it?"

Lupin glanced towards the empty floor.

"A beginning."

Harry groaned. "You really are irritating."

"So I've been told."
