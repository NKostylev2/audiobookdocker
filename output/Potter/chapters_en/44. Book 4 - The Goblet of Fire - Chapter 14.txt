Gillyweed died quickly, which became their first problem. The second was that living samples were controlled by the Ministry, cultivated by only three licensed growers in Britain and apparently more expensive than several respectable brooms. All three licensed growers had already allocated their winter cuttings to medical contracts; their replies offered nothing they could spare before March. Money could make an unlicensed seller answer faster, but Narcissa would not let Lucius buy from the people who offered. The third problem was Harry.

By the final week of January, the abandoned Charms classroom had ceased resembling a place intended for lessons. Steam clouded the upper panes. A portable cauldron occupied Professor Flitwick's old demonstration platform, surrounded by jars of stabilising agents, scraps of bladderwrack and two thermometers which disagreed by four degrees. Wet towels hung from the backs of chairs. Reference books formed unstable towers along the wall, their pages swollen by damp, while soap bubbles drifted beneath the ceiling and burst soundlessly against the beams. Outside, sleet moved sideways across the windows. Inside, the room smelled of lavender soap, hot copper and the bitter warming draught Draconia had been reducing since supper.

Harry stood at the centre of it all, damp to the elbows, and attempted to breathe inside a spell determined to become a bath product.

"Again," Hermione said.

Harry kept his wand raised while a soap bubble trembled before his face. For eleven seconds, it held. At twelve, one side thinned to a trembling, almost invisible film; at thirteen, it burst and covered his glasses in lavender foam. He closed his eyes a moment too late.

Draconia looked up from the portable cauldron. "Improvement."

"The last one lasted fourteen."

"Then deterioration."

Harry removed his glasses and wiped them against his sleeve, which was scarcely drier than the lenses. "Could you pick a side?"

"Could you give me one worth picking?"

Hermione levitated another bubble from the basin. Hundreds floated around them, catching the lamplight in fragile skins of pink and gold. Several had attached themselves to the ceiling. One drifted through the open door and frightened a passing second-year.

"The membrane isn't the difficult part," she said, watching his wand rather than his face. "You're forcing air into it before the outer structure has settled."

"I thought air was rather the point."

"Not yet."

"I prefer spells that do things in the order they're needed."

"Then you can invent one after February. For now, try waiting."

Harry aimed again. The Bubble-Head Charm remained their most reliable alternative to gillyweed. Hermione could cast it around herself for nineteen minutes. Draconia had reached twenty-three, though she claimed her version possessed a weak seal behind the left ear. Harry's record was twelve minutes and six seconds—far better than the first attempt, nowhere near the hour required beneath the lake. They practised every evening, staying until the corridors outside fell quiet and even Hermione began to misread her notes. Sometimes, with the three of them arguing over the same page, it almost felt like before.

The work gave them familiar positions. Hermione designed tests and recorded results, her handwriting becoming smaller and more severe whenever the numbers refused to improve. Draconia identified weaknesses, prepared whatever stabilising solution Hermione had dismissed as unnecessary and usually proved it necessary. Harry stood where they placed him, cast until his wrist ached and attempted not to suffocate. Their arguments returned before their ease did, slipping into the gaps where conversation was still difficult. Hermione objected to Draconia altering the test sequence without notation. Draconia objected to Hermione calling an obvious improvement statistically insignificant. Harry objected whenever both agreed that the next trial required him to remain inside an unstable charm for longer. It was not peace, exactly.

It sounded enough like friendship to make him hopeful. Between experiments, the difference returned. Hermione no longer leaned across Harry to reach a book if she could walk around the table. Draconia thought before touching his arm. When their conversations wandered towards the Ball, all three became suddenly interested in the task. They ate together again and saved one another places without discussing it, but they did not name what they were rebuilding. Harry found the uncertainty easier to survive when it had a timetable: tomorrow, after supper, they would be here again.

"Ready?" Hermione asked.

He raised his wand. "No."

"Good."

"How is that good?"

"You usually say yes when you aren't."

Draconia's stirring hand paused. Harry looked at Hermione through foam-streaked glasses, waiting for the criticism that usually followed a remark like that. She did not look away, but there was no accusation in her face. Somehow, that made answering harder.

"I'm ready enough," he said.

"All right. Ready enough."

Hermione released the bubble. Harry met it with his wand. This time, he waited. Magic settled across the surface before he fed air into the centre. The bubble expanded, clearer and stronger, until it surrounded his head from collarbone to hair. Sound dulled, leaving his own breathing uncomfortably loud inside the membrane. Draconia turned the hourglass, and Harry sat on the floor with a book propped against his knees. He tried to read the same paragraph three times before accepting that watching the sand was going to win. At eight minutes, pressure gathered behind his ears. At fifteen, the membrane rippled. At twenty-one, Hermione moved close enough to inspect the join near his shoulder. Her hand settled against his shoulder as she steadied herself, warm through his jumper. Harry forgot the pressure behind his ears. For a second, neither of them moved, and the touch felt familiar enough to hurt.

The bubble collapsed at twenty-two minutes and four seconds. Air rushed cold across Harry's damp face. Hermione stepped back.

"Twenty-two," Draconia said.

"I noticed."

"You seemed rather distracted."

Harry looked at her. Draconia returned her attention to the cauldron, but the pale line of her jaw had tightened. Some repairs remained too delicate to survive pretending jealousy had vanished. Hermione turned the hourglass upright.

"Again?"

Harry glanced between them. "Five minutes."

"Are you tired?" Hermione asked.

"Yes."

Both girls went still. Harry understood their surprise a moment too late: he had answered directly, without a joke or an assurance that he could manage one more attempt. Draconia lowered the flame beneath the cauldron, and Hermione set down her quill.

Hermione nodded once. "Five minutes."

No one praised him. The absence felt like kindness.

Narcissa obtained their first gillyweed sample without using the Malfoy name. It arrived inside a Muggle thermos carried by an owl which appeared deeply offended by the string around its feet. Draconia removed the parcel at breakfast, soothed the owl with a piece of toast and read her mother's note beneath the table.

"Well?" Harry asked.

"An authenticated cutting. It belonged to a private healer in Marseille, apparently."

Hermione lowered her porridge spoon. "Belonged?"

"It died during transport."

"How long ago?"

Draconia read further, and a small crease appeared between her brows. "Six years."

Harry looked at the thermos, then back at her. "Efficient."

"She also says Father would like it recorded that he could procure a living sample within forty-eight hours."

"But?" Hermione asked.

"Mother has forbidden him from contacting the person he described as 'perfectly discreet.'"

"Sensible."

Draconia folded the note. "Father added a separate message."

She passed it to Harry. Two words occupied the centre of an expensive sheet of parchment.

Eat more.

Harry turned it over. "Is there task advice on the back?"

"No."

"Threats?"

"The message is the threat."

Hermione smiled into her bowl. The dead gillyweed resembled a handful of grey rats' tails. Its stalks had collapsed into leathery strips, and two bulbous nodes remained shrivelled near the base. Nothing about it suggested the ability to transform lungs. They carried it to Greenhouse Three after Herbology, with Hermione holding the thermos upright as if six years of death might still be made worse by careless handling. Professor Sprout listened to Hermione ask a hypothetical question for less than a minute before holding out her hand.

"Sample."

Hermione stopped. "Professor?"

"You've given me four different explanations for this independent project, Miss Granger. None of them accounts for the lake-temperature charts sticking out of Mr Potter's sleeve."

Harry looked down. The edge of a graph protruded from his cuff, where he had tucked it when they ran out of room in Hermione's bag.

"Subtle," Draconia murmured.

Harry handed over the thermos. Sprout tipped the cutting onto a potting bench. Winter light entered through steamed glass overhead, warming rows of sleeping plants. The air smelled of wet soil, fertiliser and the sharp peppermint tang of shrubs which exhaled whenever anyone passed. Professor Sprout touched her wand to one withered node. A green pulse moved through it and failed.

"Gillyweed," she said. "True strain, properly harvested and entirely useless."

Hermione opened her notebook. "Could it be revived?"

"Not by Merlin with a watering can."

"Could its magical signature be preserved?" Draconia asked.

Sprout's eyes moved between them, lingering on Harry before returning to the shrivelled cutting. Tournament rules prevented staff from advising a champion; nothing prevented her from knowing exactly why they asked, or from looking thoroughly unhappy about it.

"A Revealing Solution will identify a living sample against this one," she said. "Preparation instructions are in Magical Mediterranean Flora, third edition. The second edition mistranslates the ratio and produces false positives with ordinary pondweed."

Hermione wrote every word. "Thank you, Professor."

"I have not helped you."

"Of course not."

Sprout wrapped the sample again. Harry hesitated.

"Does Hogwarts have any?"

The professor's kind face tightened. "My last living culture succumbed to silver blight in July."

"Snape?" Draconia asked.

"Professor Snape," Sprout corrected. "Keeps preserved gillyweed among his restricted ingredients. Preserved samples retain their potion properties, not their transformative ones."

"Could we ask to inspect it?" Hermione said.

"You may ask."

Sprout's tone made the likely outcome clear. Draconia repacked the thermos with rather less hope than she had brought into the greenhouse.

"If a fresh sample appeared," Harry said, "how long would it remain usable?"

"Out of salt water? Four hours. Six if wrapped in bladderwrack and held below ten degrees."

"So whoever supplies it has to know when I need it."

Sprout met his eyes.

"Yes."

Harry became aware of a draught at the back of his neck, although the greenhouse door was closed. Moody knew the task date, knew Harry had found the water clue and had been the one to point him towards plants. If fresh gillyweed arrived at precisely the right moment, they would have a good idea who wanted him to eat it. They still would not know why.

Professor Snape refused their request in six words.

"No, and ten points from Gryffindor."

Harry stood before the Potions desk with Hermione on one side and Draconia on the other, a formation which had apparently done nothing to improve their chances.

"Why Gryffindor?" he asked.

"Miss Malfoy possessed the sense to remain silent."

"She wrote the request."

"Your decision to deliver it indicates a leadership failure."

Draconia's expression remained innocent. Snape looked at her.

"Five points from Slytherin for abusing delegation."

"Yes, Professor."

"We only want to examine the preserved sample," Hermione said. "We're asking to compare two plants, Professor. That isn't the same as asking you to solve the task."

"You have entered my classroom outside lesson hours as a group, carrying a comparative botanical specimen and wearing the expression you reserve for facts you intend to extract through exhaustion."

Hermione drew breath.

"Do not demonstrate," Snape said.

Harry placed both hands upon the desk. "Someone may give me fresh gillyweed before the task."

Snape's attention sharpened. "Someone?"

"Moody pointed me towards aquatic plants."

"Professor Moody."

"Do you correct him when he calls you Snivellus?"

Hermione closed her eyes as if she might still prevent the question by refusing to witness it. Draconia found something of sudden interest on the dungeon ceiling. Snape became perfectly still.

"Twenty points from Gryffindor."

"Worth it," Harry said.

"No, it wasn't," Hermione whispered.

Snape rose, his black robes settling around him as he stepped away from the desk. The fires beneath the classroom cauldrons had been extinguished, leaving the dungeon cold enough for Harry's breath to show faintly. Whatever punishment Harry expected next, it did not come.

"What precisely did Moody say?"

Harry repeated the conversation. He omitted nothing—not the underwater books, not Moody's observation about mineral residue and not the exact number of days remaining. Snape listened without interrupting.

"And Black knows?" he asked when Harry finished.

"Everything except the gillyweed."

"Tell him."

The instruction carried none of Snape's usual contempt for Sirius. There was no sneer, no suggestion that Harry's godfather would make matters worse. That frightened Harry more than an insult would have. Snape crossed to the locked storeroom. Wards rippled when he touched the handle. He disappeared inside and returned with a narrow jar sealed in black wax. The material within was grey-green, thicker than Narcissa's cutting and suspended in brine.

"You said it was useless," Harry said.

"For your intended purpose. It remains adequate for identification."

Hermione took out the prepared Revealing Solution. Snape did not hand her the jar.

"You will conduct the comparison here. You will not remove the sample, touch the sample or breathe unnecessarily near the sample. If Mr Potter consumes it through some act of inspired incompetence, I will permit the lake to keep him."

"You'd miss me," Harry said.

"Begin."

The dead cutting produced a pale blue light beneath the Revealing Solution. Snape's preserved sample answered with the same colour, brighter and threaded with gold. Hermione recorded the response. Draconia adjusted the formula twice. Harry sketched the exact shape of the nodes and the serrated edge of each narrow leaf until he could have drawn them from memory. They knew how to recognise gillyweed and how its magic answered the solution. They still had none he could use. Snape resealed the jar while Hermione waited for her final line of notes to dry.

"Professor," Draconia said. "Who can enter your stores?"

"The headmaster. Myself. Any sufficiently skilled thief willing to discover why the wards are described as punitive rather than preventative."

"Moody?" Harry asked.

Snape's gaze rested upon him. "Alastor Moody could enter most rooms in this castle."

"Could he do it without you knowing?"

"No."

Harry had expected an answer that gave them something solid to hold on to. Instead, Snape's certainty left him wondering what, exactly, the professor would do if he found Moody in his stores.

As February wore on, the remaining days seemed to narrow around them. Harry learned to hold a Bubble-Head Charm for thirty-one minutes, though by the end his temples ached and keeping the membrane steady took nearly all his attention. Hermione reached forty-two. Draconia abandoned the spell at thirty-eight and concentrated upon a warming draught which kept Harry's hands functional after half an hour in lake water. They practised swimming in the prefects' bathroom beneath the Disillusionment Charm. The first evening, Harry discovered robes became determined murderers once wet. The second, Hermione brought close-fitting exercise clothes Mr Granger used for swimming and reduced them to Harry's size with a tailoring charm. The black fabric fitted Harry so closely that he refused to emerge from behind the towel screen until Draconia threatened to vanish it.

"It looks ridiculous."

"It looks Muggle," Draconia corrected.

"That is not an improvement in your vocabulary."

Hermione folded her arms. "Loose fabric creates resistance. There's a reason competitive swimmers use them."

"Do competitive swimmers have to walk past everyone they know first?"

"Most wear less," Hermione said.

Harry looked at her, and Hermione's face became pink as she seemed to hear her own answer. Draconia turned one sapphire tap so hard that it produced a wave, slopping water over the nearest steps. Harry laughed before he could stop himself. The sound came more easily than it had in the library, though it faltered when he realised both girls were watching him. He entered the pool before anyone could mention it, grateful for something to do with his hands.

They made a rescue dummy from bundled towels and a weighted swimming cloak. Harry practised bringing it to the steps without trapping his wand hand, then tying a temporary fastening between its belt and his harness. The first knot tightened beyond his ability to undo it with cold fingers; the second loosened under a sudden pull. Hermione made him start again, checking that the knife remained reachable every time. Draconia cast currents against him until he learnt how quickly loose cloth could turn a straightforward carry into a struggle. His speed improved. His turns sharpened. He learned to cast through water, where spells moved slower and bent in directions the wand had not indicated. Hermione charmed lengths of rope to catch his ankles. Draconia transfigured towels into pale imitations of grindylows and sent them after him in groups.

One attached itself to Harry's head. He surfaced wearing it like an angry hat.

"Your creature lacks anatomical accuracy," Hermione told Draconia.

"It achieved its purpose."

"Its purpose appears to be humiliation."

"Then it exceeded expectations."

Harry removed the towel from his face. "I preferred you when you weren't speaking."

Both girls went silent, and Harry heard the words again without the joke he had meant to put around them. Hermione's mouth closed; Draconia's wand lowered an inch. The easy warmth of a moment earlier vanished.

"I didn't mean—"

"We know," Hermione said too quickly.

Draconia vanished the towel creatures nearest him. Harry's first instinct was to climb from the pool, dry himself and give them room until the hurt became small enough for everyone to pretend it had healed alone. He caught the marble edge instead, making himself stay.

"No."

Hermione had already turned towards the stack of towels. She stopped.

"No what?" Draconia asked.

"We aren't doing that again." Water ran from Harry's hair into his eyes. He pushed it back. "I meant I preferred you before you became united in trying to drown me with household linen. It was a stupid joke. It sounded worse than I meant it, and I'm sorry."

Neither girl spoke immediately. The taps whispered into the pool. One of the surviving towel creatures drifted near the bottom, slowly becoming ordinary white cotton again. Hermione's shoulders lowered first.

"It still hurts, even when you don't mean it," she said.

"I know."

Her eyebrows rose.

"The good kind," Harry added.

Draconia regarded him for another moment. "Your apology could use some work."

"Which bit?"

"The bit where you explain how badly we misunderstood your brilliance."

"I did say the joke was stupid."

"A promising foundation."

Hermione picked up the towel she had intended to throw at him and placed it within reach instead.

"Accepted," she said. "Provisionally."

Harry looked at Draconia. She flicked her wand. Three fresh towel creatures unfolded along the tiles, each now possessing a recognisable grindylow shape and twice as many claws.

"Survive the next exercise," she said, "and I will consider the matter resolved."

"That doesn't sound much like forgiveness."

"It is Slytherin forgiveness."

Hermione sent the first creature into the water. Practice did not end early. Hermione began counting as Harry ducked beneath the water, and Draconia sent the creatures after him with renewed enthusiasm. He still felt the sting of what he had said, but when he surfaced, both girls were waiting at the edge. This evening felt different from the ones that almost resembled before. They had stayed, even after something went wrong.

Hagrid knew what lived in the lake. He also knew why they wanted to know and demonstrated his respect for Tournament rules by becoming incapable of looking directly at Harry.

"Ordinary school interest," Harry said.

"Course."

Hagrid poured boiling water into four mugs and missed one by several inches.

"We thought we ought to learn more about the local wildlife," Hermione added, rather too earnestly.

"Educational."

Water spread across the table. Draconia rescued the plate of rock cakes before it became an island. Outside the hut, sleet scratched against the windows. Wind came down from the mountains and worried at the roof, making the rafters creak above them. Fang occupied most of the hearthrug with his paws extended towards the fire. Every few minutes, he sighed as though the weather had been arranged to inconvenience him personally. Harry held both hands around his tea.

The hut smelled of woodsmoke, damp wool and whatever Hagrid had boiled in the largest copper pot. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the ceiling. A cracked oar leaned beside the door. Upon the mantel sat a moving photograph from the First Task: Harry diving beneath the Horntail while Hagrid stood at the edge of the frame with both hands over his mouth. Harry had not known anyone took it. He watched the tiny figure of himself dive again and tried to remember that, from where Hagrid had stood, there would have been no way to see what happened next.

"We need to know about grindylows," Hermione said.

Hagrid sat heavily. "Nasty fingers. Stronger'n they look. Break the grip at the wrist, not the fingers."

"How many live in the lake?" Harry asked.

"Few colonies. Mostly keep ter the weed beds on the western side."

Hermione unfolded a hand-drawn map. Hagrid stared at it.

"That's very detailed fer ordinary school interest."

"We are committed students," Draconia said.

"You drew the merpeople's village," Hagrid said.

Hermione put one hand over it. "Theoretical."

Hagrid's beard moved around a smile he attempted to conceal. He marked three regions of the map with a thick finger. Grindylow beds near the shallows. A kelpie's hunting ground along the eastern bank. The merpeople's village in the deepest part of the lake, beyond a steep shelf where sunlight failed even in summer.

"Giant squid?" Harry asked.

"Friendly."

"To whom?" Draconia said.

"Most people."

"That doesn't tell us how to avoid being one of the exceptions," Hermione said.

"Likes toast."

Draconia looked at Harry. "At last, a defensive skill you actually possess."

"Carrying emergency bread?"

"Attracting creatures with terrible dietary choices."

Harry reached for a rock cake and discovered Fang's head beneath his hand. The boarhound had crossed the room without anyone noticing. He seized the cake, Harry's glove and a corner of the map in one movement. Hermione caught the parchment. Harry kept hold of the glove. Fang pulled.

"Release," Harry told him.

Fang pulled harder.

"He respects authority," Draconia observed.

"Help."

"This is valuable practical training."

Harry braced one foot against the table leg. Fang shook his enormous head. The wet glove slapped Harry across the face, the stitching failed and he fell backwards into Hagrid's basket of kindling. Hagrid roared with laughter. Hermione managed a startled movement towards him and nearly two seconds of concern before covering her mouth. Draconia looked down at Harry sitting among split logs with half a glove still clenched in one hand.

"Potter," she said, perfectly solemn, "the lake may be beyond you."

Harry tried to answer and laughed instead, helplessly enough that he had to brace one hand against the basket. It was nothing like the startled half-sound in the library or the careful laugh in the prefects' bathroom. This one bent him forward and made his bruised back hurt against the wood, which only made stopping harder. Hagrid laughed harder because Harry did. Hermione surrendered next. Even Draconia's composure broke when Fang proudly deposited the other half of the glove in her lap. For several minutes, the task disappeared. The Ball disappeared. There was only the hot, crowded hut, sleet against the windows and Fang chewing leather while four people failed to stop laughing. Harry wiped his eyes. Hagrid looked at him across the table. Pride and relief lived openly upon his face. Harry's laughter quietened.

Hagrid cleared his throat and reached for the map.

"Merpeople won' hurt yeh if yeh respect their waters," he said. "They've got rules older'n the school. Don' point a wand unless they threaten yeh first, an' don' mistake warnin' fer attack."

"What if something is being held in the village?" Harry asked.

Hagrid's fingers stopped upon the parchment.

"Then they'll keep it safe."

"Would they stop me taking it?"

"Depends whether it belongs ter you."

The warmth of the hut suddenly seemed unable to reach him. Harry pictured Hermione beneath dark water, then Draconia bound in the same place, and could not make himself choose between the images. Somewhere, magic would select one person and the Tournament would treat that choice as an answer.

"And if something doesn't?" he asked.

Hagrid met his eyes at last.

"Harry."

"If someone is left behind."

"The judges won' let anyone get hurt."

Hermione's hand tightened on the map, and Draconia looked down at her untouched tea. Harry wanted to believe him; wanting was not quite enough. Hagrid glanced towards the First Task photograph, where the little dragon was turning for another strike.

"Bring what's yours back," he said quietly. "Then listen ter the merpeople."

It was the only advice he could give without breaking rules. Harry folded the map carefully when they left, keeping the advice with it.

Luna found Harry in the Owlery two evenings before the task. He had climbed there to send Sirius a copy of Moody's remarks, Snape's assessment and every property of gillyweed they had confirmed. The circular tower was full of winter wind and irritated owls. Feathers moved across the stone floor. Far below, the grounds lay white beneath a late frost, the lake a black wound through the landscape. Luna stood beside the western window with a barn owl upon her shoulder.

"Hello, Harry."

"Hello."

The owl held one strand of her hair in its beak. Luna did not appear to notice. Harry tied his letter to a school owl and watched it launch into the dark. Hedwig remained at Hawthorn Cottage through the coldest weeks, where Sirius claimed she had established territorial control over his bedroom. Harry missed her whenever another owl looked at him without judgement.

"You're worried about the water," Luna said.

"Is something attached to me?"

"Several feathers."

Harry brushed one from his sleeve. "The task is in the lake."

"I know."

He looked at her. "How?"

"You keep borrowing books with titles like How Not to Drown."

"That one was useful."

"I liked the illustrations."

The barn owl released her hair and nipped gently at her ear. Harry leaned against the wall.

"The magic is going to take someone I care about."

Luna's pale eyes moved towards the lake. "Magic often confuses possession with importance."

"That is unusually relevant."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't—never mind."

Luna smiled. The easy silence she carried settled between them while the owl rearranged its feet on her shoulder. Harry had taken her to the Ball because being beside Luna required no performance. He could stand here with nothing useful to say, and she would not mistake it for a wish to be alone. She noticed everything and demanded explanations for very little.

"Are Hermione and Draconia still sad?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Are you?"

Harry watched an owl circle above the rafters. "Yes."

"You're speaking again."

"We never stopped entirely."

"That isn't what I meant."

Of course it wasn't. Harry put both hands into his pockets.

"I don't know what we are now."

"You didn't know before."

"It felt less important before."

"Things are often easier when we haven't named why they matter."

The wind shifted. Wings rustled throughout the tower.

"You don't have to know what you are to know where you want to be," Luna said.

Harry thought of two saved places at breakfast. Hermione's shoulder against his in the library. Draconia's fingers wrapping his thumb while insisting she remained angry.

"That sounds suspiciously sensible."

"Daddy says sense is a story ordinary people tell after something works."

"There it is."

Luna took a small paper parcel from the windowsill. "I brought you this."

Inside lay a pair of yellow woollen socks decorated with blue fish. Each fish changed direction when Harry touched it.

"For after the lake," she explained. "Feet become cold."

Harry held them with both hands, feeling the thick wool catch against the rough skin beside his thumb. There was no hidden purpose to the gift, no advice he had to remember or promise he was expected to make. They were ridiculous socks given by someone who believed he would return to wear them.

"Thank you."

Luna lifted the owl from her shoulder and set it upon the sill.

"You should tell them when you're frightened."

"Hermione and Draconia?"

"They already know. It would make them feel less alone in it."

She left before Harry could answer, her footsteps light on the spiral stairs. He stayed by the window a little longer, letting the blue fish swim around his hands before folding the socks back into their paper.

On the night before the task, Harry told them. They sat upon the floor of the unused Charms classroom surrounded by their preparations. A flask of warming draught stood beside the egg. Hermione had packed towels, blankets, dry robes, dittany, chocolate and three methods of signalling underwater. Draconia inspected Harry's wand for the fourth time. The Bubble-Head Charm had reached thirty-seven minutes that afternoon. There was still no living gillyweed.

"I'm frightened," Harry said.

Hermione's hands stopped inside the medical bag, and Draconia looked up from his wand. Harry had intended to begin with something practical about contingency plans, or perhaps a joke about the squid. Instead, the admission came out bare and embarrassingly small. He looked at the flask beside his knee, resisting the urge to add something that would let them all laugh it away.

"Of the task?" Hermione asked.

"Of who they take."

Neither girl moved.

"I keep thinking it will be one of you," he continued. "Then I think it might be Sirius, or Remus, or Hagrid. Every time I decide who the magic means, someone else appears. And if it chooses one person, it feels like it's saying everyone else matters less."

Draconia laid his wand across her knees.

"The Goblet thought you belonged to a school that doesn't exist," she said. "I wouldn't give its judgement much authority."

Harry looked at her. The words echoed Cedric so closely that affection hurt beneath his ribs. Hermione shifted nearer.

"Whoever is taken will be protected," she said.

Harry's expression answered.

"I know," she added. "We aren't relying on that."

"If I don't come back in an hour—"

"You will," Draconia said.

"If I don't, you tell Sirius not to go into the lake."

"No."

"Draco."

"I am not helping you arrange things in case you die. Don't ask me to."

"It isn't—"

"Then do not speak as though it is."

Draconia's words were sharp enough to leave Harry silent. She gripped his wand until her knuckles whitened, staring at him as if he had stepped towards an edge she could not reach. Hermione touched her wrist, then left her hand there. Draconia's fingers slowly eased around the wood. Harry watched. Their bond no longer depended upon him. Once, that would have frightened him. Tonight, it made the room feel stronger.

"All right," he said.

Draconia returned the wand.

"What do you do if the gillyweed appears?" Hermione asked.

"Bring it to both of you."

"Then?"

"Test it with the solution, check for poison and curses, and compare it with the cutting. I remember."

"If it passes?"

"I tell McGonagall and Dumbledore where it came from."

"And if they permit it?"

"Eat something that looks like dead worms and hope Professor Sprout knows her plants."

Hermione nodded.

"If it doesn't appear?" Draconia asked.

"Use the warming draught and the Bubble-Head Charm. Come up after thirty minutes, whether I've found them or not."

The last condition had taken three days of argument to get into the plan. Harry said it because there was no sensible alternative, although he could not imagine obeying it with someone still below. From Hermione's expression, she knew that too. Draconia looked at the flask and said nothing. They finished packing without reopening an argument none of them had truly won. At the door, Hermione hesitated.

"Do you want us to stay?"

Harry looked around the classroom where he had spent the morning after the Ball listening to the egg scream. It had seemed an enormous room then, with far too many empty chairs.

"Yes," he said, before he could persuade himself they needed proper beds more than he needed them here.

Draconia closed the door but did not bolt it. They slept badly upon transfigured cushions beneath three separate blankets. Harry woke repeatedly. Each time, one of them remained visible: Hermione curled towards the wall with one hand beneath her cheek, Draconia flat upon her back with her wand still held against her chest. By five, Harry had stopped trying to sleep and was listening to the small sounds the room made around them. At six, Hermione woke to find him watching the first snow gather beyond the windows. She sat up, drawing her blanket around her shoulders, and neither of them suggested another attempt at sleep. Ten minutes later, something scratched at the door. All three drew their wands. The scratching came again. Harry approached while Hermione covered the hinges and Draconia stood to one side. He opened the door with a Shield Charm held before him.

No one waited outside. A narrow parcel lay upon the stone. Cold mist escaped from its seams. Upon the brown paper, in block letters, someone had written:

FOR THE LAKE.

Harry did not touch it.

Behind him, Draconia whispered, "He knew where we were."

Hermione looked along the empty corridor. "Or watched us enter."

The castle settled around them with a low movement of distant stairs. A faint tap came from inside the package, followed by a soft, unmistakable scraping.

Hermione levitated the parcel onto a cleared desk. Draconia cast the first detection charm. Harry cast the second. No curse answered, no poison darkened the paper and no hidden writing appeared beneath the block letters.

"Open it remotely," Hermione said.

Harry directed the tip of his wand towards the string. "Diffindo."

The knot separated. Brown paper unfolded to reveal a glass tube packed with ice and bladderwrack. Something grey-green twisted inside, thick stems knotting against one another with slow, unpleasant purpose. Harry had expected a living sample to look healthier than the dead one. Somehow, it looked considerably worse.

"Charming," Harry said.

Hermione used tongs to transfer one narrow leaf onto a porcelain dish. The Revealing Solution touched it. Pale blue light filled the room. Gold threaded through the centre, brighter than Snape's preserved sample and moving with each contraction of the plant. Draconia compared the nodes to Harry's sketch.

"Shape matches."

"Leaf margin matches," Hermione said.

"Magical signature matches."

Harry looked at the tube. "And it appeared exactly when Moody would need it to."

"We take it to Dumbledore," Hermione said.

They arrived at the gargoyle before seven. The Headmaster's office contained Dumbledore, Professor McGonagall, Snape and Moody. That fact disturbed Harry almost as much as the gillyweed. Moody stood beside the window in his heavy travelling cloak, magical eye fixed upon the glass tube the moment Harry entered. Snape occupied the edge of Dumbledore's desk with both arms folded. McGonagall wore outdoor robes and an expression sharpened by insufficient tea. Dumbledore looked from the trio to the parcel.

"I had wondered whether our early visitor reached the correct door."

Harry stopped. "You knew?"

"The castle informed me that someone carrying magically chilled vegetation crossed the fifth floor shortly before six."

"Who?" Draconia asked.

"The castle is excellent at identifying objects," Dumbledore said. "Less cooperative regarding people who understand its passages."

Moody's scarred face revealed nothing. Harry set the tube upon the desk.

"Did you send it?"

The magical eye rotated towards him.

"No."

Harry waited for more: outrage, perhaps, or a demand to know why he suspected a professor. Moody offered neither, leaving the word between them as though it needed no explanation.

"Did you arrange for someone else to send it?" Hermione asked.

Moody's ordinary eye moved to her.

"No."

Snape's gaze remained upon him.

"The plant is genuine," Draconia said. "The magical signature matches Professor Snape's preserved sample."

"Its packaging follows Sprout's instructions," Harry added. "Whoever brought it knew when we would be in that classroom."

"And how to obtain a fresh cutting in time," Hermione said. "That isn't something you can leave lying about until it's convenient."

Dumbledore examined the gillyweed through half-moon spectacles. "Severus?"

Snape removed the stopper. He performed three silent spells over the tube, each causing the plant to recoil. The last produced a narrow band of white light.

"Unaltered," he said. "Unpoisoned. No compulsion, tracking charm or curse detectable."

"Safe?" Harry asked.

"I did not say safe. I said no additional threat has been attached to a plant whose intended function is forcibly restructuring your respiratory system."

"Closer than anything else we've got."

"That is not the same thing."

McGonagall approached the desk. "Tournament champions are permitted outside assistance," she said. "Use of the sample would not violate the contract."

"That isn't what concerns us," Hermione replied.

McGonagall's expression softened by a degree.

"No."

Harry looked at Dumbledore. "If I refuse it, I have a charm that lasts thirty-seven minutes."

"The task allows one hour."

"I know."

"And if you accept it?"

"I survive long enough to find out why someone wanted me prepared."

Moody's mouth shifted. Harry saw.

"Do you find something amusing?"

"You're learning."

"From people I trust."

The distinction landed. Moody's magical eye stopped moving. Dumbledore placed one hand beside the tube.

"Sometimes a person offering help is relying on us to refuse it," he said. "Accepting it carefully may serve us better. You have identified the substance and told us how it arrived. There are witnesses to both. That does not answer every question, Harry, but the decision remains yours."

Harry glanced towards Hermione and Draconia. Hermione's hands were clasped tightly around her notebook; Draconia had not taken her eyes off the tube. Both were frightened, and he hated that they had lost so much sleep helping him prepare for this. Neither tried to choose for him.

"I'll use it."

Snape resealed the tube. "Then consume the complete cutting no earlier than two minutes before entering the water. The transformation will begin at once."

"How will I know?"

"Breathing will become difficult."

"You do know how to make this sound appealing."

Snape handed the tube to Draconia rather than Harry. "Keep it cold."

She accepted it with both hands. As they turned to leave, Moody spoke.

"Potter."

Harry looked back.

"One hour."

Harry had heard the same words in the clue often enough to know them in his sleep. Coming from Moody, they sounded less like a limit and more like a warning.

Sirius's letter was waiting beside Harry's breakfast plate. It had been folded without an envelope, the corner weighted by a spoon. Harry recognised the handwriting before he noticed Remus standing at the end of the table: the impatient downward stroke of the capital H, a word that had run out of room and continued beneath itself. His hand went automatically to the mirror inside his robes. Whatever Sirius had written, hearing him say it would be better.

"He isn't at Hawthorn," Remus said.

Harry looked up. Hermione stopped arranging the medical bag beneath the bench. Draconia's fingers closed around the chilled tube.

"Where is he?"

Remus sat opposite him, moving the bowl of porridge aside before Harry could put an elbow in it.

"With the merpeople. He agreed to be your hostage last night, Harry."

For a moment, the Hall lost its ordinary sounds. Harry could still see an owl struggling with a parcel above the Ravenclaw table, could see Luna trying to persuade a younger student that the seeds inside her breakfast fruit were not watching him. The Hall went on around him, busy and bright and full of people who had not yet heard what Remus had said. Harry could not make any of it fit with the image of Sirius beneath the lake.

"They've taken him."

"He went willingly," Remus said, keeping his voice quiet. "Please read what he wrote."

Harry unfolded the page, catching the corner before it could tear beneath his thumb. An inky print marked one edge. Sirius had been careless with the ink again, and the familiar little mistake made Harry want to hear his voice all the more.

Harry,

Before you decide to murder anyone in an official hat: nobody compelled me. They asked whether I would be your hostage. I asked considerably more questions than they enjoyed, spoke to the chieftain and watched Flitwick check the breathing enchantments. I agreed after that.

I have a separate safeguard against the sleep spell failing. Minerva and Remus have the terms in writing. The merpeople know exactly who I am, despite my inability to introduce myself underwater without sounding like a blocked drain.

If you do not reach me, they bring me back. If your hour ends, they bring me back. Your marks are the part which runs out of time. I do not.

I wish I could sit in the stands and shout ridiculous encouragement. Apparently loving you has finally become an activity the organisers can score. You are allowed to object to that; I certainly intend to afterwards.

Come back safely. There are paint decisions waiting at Hawthorn for Easter, and I refuse to be blamed for making them without supervision.

Sirius.

Below the signature he had added:

The mirror is with Remus until I am awake. You may call him. Do not stand in the middle of the lake attempting it.

Harry read the page again, more slowly this time. The joke about paint reached him late, carrying with it the smell of the cottage kitchen and Hedwig on the garden wall. There would be an ordinary argument at Easter, in a house where his opinion mattered and somebody expected him to arrive. He tried to stay with that thought, but his eyes caught on they bring me back and refused to move.

"Why did he agree?"

Remus did not bristle. He waited until Harry looked up before answering.

"He checked the protections and trusted what he saw. And he would rather go himself than have them ask another child."

Harry glanced at Hermione, then Draconia. Both were still beside him. He had spent weeks seeing one or the other beneath dark water, but the absence he needed to look for now lay outside the castle, in a sitting room where nobody would answer his own name through the glass.

"They could have let him tell me."

"They approached him after eleven last night. He asked to leave you a message, and this is the earliest the regulations allowed us to deliver it." Remus touched the edge of the page, his expression tightening. "I wish they'd let the two of you speak first. I would have preferred that too."

Harry was grateful he said it. Knowing that somebody else disliked the arrangement did more than another assurance about ancient safeguards would have done.

"You saw him go?"

"Yes. Minerva accompanied us. He was awake when he entered the water."

"And you checked the spells?"

"Flitwick did. I watched, and I checked the written conditions against what the chieftain agreed. Sirius made her repeat the arrangements twice."

"She must have loved that."

"She informed him that she had successfully kept fish alive for more years than he had been irritating."

Harry's mouth twitched despite himself. He looked down before it could become a proper smile, still frightened enough to resent that Sirius was able to make him laugh from beneath a lake.

Hermione slid the porridge towards him. "You need to eat."

"I've lost the enthusiasm."

"Then eat without it."

Draconia took his spoon from the letter and put it into his hand. Her other hand settled briefly against the back of his neck, warm beneath the collar. He looked at her, then at the paper and the familiar thumbprint. Sirius had chosen this, after asking questions and checking the things Harry would have wanted checked. Harry could hate the lake, and the rules that had kept them from speaking, without making his godfather's decision into another disappearance somebody had forced upon them.

"You'll stay where they can find you?" he asked Remus.

"Beside the judges' platform. Minerva knows. Your mirror will reach me whenever you are above water."

Harry folded the letter along its original crease and slipped it into the pocket that held his brass key. The small, solid shape pressed against the paper as he settled back onto the bench. He took one spoonful, then another, while Hermione explained why the warming draught needed more than nerves to work on and Draconia inspected the seals on his equipment for the fifth time. Remus remained until Harry finished. When they stood, he squeezed Harry's shoulder once, firmly enough to be felt through his robes.

"He is waiting for you," he said. "And so are we."

The Second Task began beneath a white sky. Temporary stands curved around the western edge of the lake. Their wooden supports stood in the frozen shallows, groaning whenever spectators stamped their feet for warmth. Four sets of flags moved above the highest tier: Hogwarts red and gold, Beauxbatons blue, Durmstrang crimson and a second Hogwarts banner added after the Goblet produced Harry's name. Harry could not look at the extra flag without remembering that nobody had ever explained which school it was meant to represent. The snow had stopped, but cold silvered the reeds, hardened the mud and turned every breath into smoke. Beyond the stands, the lake stretched smooth and dark to the far bank. Even the giant squid had abandoned the surface.

Harry stood inside the champions' enclosure wearing Mr Granger's black swimming clothes beneath an open winter cloak. He had stopped objecting to the fit the moment the air touched his legs. It was difficult to be embarrassed and this cold at the same time. Cedric waited beside him in dark yellow robes charmed to shed water. His Bubble-Head Charm had improved enough that he planned to cast it as the whistle sounded. Fleur wore silver-blue swimming robes and carried three pearl phials along her belt. Krum had removed his outer cloak to reveal something resembling a sleeveless duelling uniform.

"You solved it," Cedric said.

Harry showed him the tube. "Someone solved it for me."

Cedric's eyes narrowed. "Who?"

"Not knowing is becoming a bit of a theme."

Cedric looked towards the stands. His attention moved quickly over the Hufflepuff section, then the rows of blue and bronze where the Ravenclaws had gathered.

"Cho isn't there," he said.

"Sirius is down there."

Cedric turned towards him. "Your godfather?"

"He left a letter. He checked the protections himself."

Cedric looked back at the water, the surprise leaving his face. "Then we make sure you can tell him what you think of the idea afterwards."

The boys looked at one another, and Harry found none of the rivalry he had braced for in Cedric's face. There was worry there, plainly enough, but it made room for Harry's instead of competing with it. Scores, applause and school loyalties all seemed very small beside the black water waiting beyond the enclosure.

"If one of us reaches the village and something has gone wrong—" Cedric began.

"We bring everyone we can."

Cedric nodded once. "Even if it costs time."

"I thought Hufflepuffs cared about rules."

"People first," Cedric said. "We can argue about the rules when everyone's out."

Harry held out his hand. Cedric clasped it, grip firm and cold from the morning air.

"Both back," he said.

"All back," Harry corrected.

Cedric's mouth moved. "All back."

Across the enclosure, Fleur paced before Madame Maxime. Her usual composure had sharpened into something close to panic. She asked a question in rapid French. Maxime took both her hands and answered quietly.

"Gabrielle," Harry said.

Cedric followed his gaze. "Her sister?"

"She arrived with the Beauxbatons families yesterday."

Fleur turned towards the lake as though she could see through it, searching the empty surface with a helplessness Harry recognised. Krum stood alone, his face set and unreadable. Harry could not tell whom he was thinking about, or whether he was simply better at keeping it to himself. Bagman called the champions forward. The roar of the crowd crossed the ice-stiff air. Harry found Hermione and Draconia in the front row immediately. They stood beside Remus, with Narcissa and Lucius in the seats behind them, both dressed in winter robes too elegant for the wooden stand. Hagrid towered near the judges' platform. Professor Sprout held binoculars she could not possibly use once Harry entered the water.

Remus's eyes went to the chilled tube in Draconia's hand. Harry had told him about its arrival at breakfast. He knew where it had appeared, whom they suspected and which professors had examined it. The answering mirror remained inside his coat; for this morning, he held the other end of the line Harry usually trusted Sirius to answer. Dumbledore met the champions at the edge of the lake.

"The task before you is simple to describe," he said, his voice carrying across the stands. "Someone each of you values waits within the merpeople's village. You have one hour to find and return with them. The lake's people have agreed to protect those entrusted to their care."

Fleur stepped forward. "And if we do not return within the hour?"

Silence travelled through the spectators. Dumbledore's gaze rested upon all four.

"The hostages will not be harmed."

Harry heard what he did not say. The champions might be.

"Sir." Harry waited until Dumbledore looked at him. "If I cannot reach Sirius, someone brings him back?"

"The chieftain will return him safely. Your godfather examined the protections before agreeing to participate. Professor Flitwick confirmed them independently."

"Then why does the clue sound as though you'll leave them there?"

Dumbledore's face grew grave. "The threat concerns a champion's score," he said. "The wording suggests something far more frightening, but it does not determine how the hostages are treated. They will be returned safely."

Harry looked past him at Fleur. She was clutching the edge of her cloak so tightly that the fabric had twisted between her fingers.

"Someone might have mentioned that before we spent a month hearing it."

"Yes," Dumbledore said quietly. "They should have."

The admission surprised Harry, although he wished it had come weeks earlier. He thought of Sirius's letter beneath his hands at breakfast and the brass key he had tucked beside it. Both were safely ashore now, waiting for him to come back and collect them. Bagman announced five minutes. Hermione and Draconia came down from the stands. Remus followed. No official stopped them. Lucius stood behind the nearest one with the expression of a man prepared to explain why that would be unwise. Draconia held out the gillyweed.

"With the ice and bladderwrack, it should last until about eleven-twenty," she said. "That gets you past the hour. Keep it in the tube until the last moment."

Hermione checked Harry's wand harness, then the flask of warming draught tied to his belt.

"The western route is shorter. Avoid the reed beds. Follow the slope after the first shelf."

"I remember."

"Wait until you lose feeling in two fingers before you use the warming draught. It could speed up the gillyweed as well as warming you."

"I remember."

"If the transformation begins to fail—"

"Surface."

Hermione held his gaze, looking for a promise he could not honestly give. Harry wanted to say he would do exactly what they had practised; he wanted, even more, to make it true. Draconia pushed the chilled tube into his hand before either of them spoke again.

"Come back."

Her voice caught on the second word, and the sound frightened him more than another warning would have. Harry closed his fingers around the glass, steadying it between them.

"I will."

Remus reached him last. He took Harry's face briefly between both hands, his thumbs brushing the cold from Harry's cheeks.

"I'll be here," he said. "Minerva will be beside me. You know how to reach us once you're above water."

Harry nodded. The place where Sirius should have been opened inside his chest again, and he let Remus hold him for a moment before stepping back. Remus's hand stayed upon his shoulder until Harry was ready to move. One minute remained. Harry removed his cloak. His school robes, the letter and the brass key were already in Hermione's medical bag; she had taken them when he changed before entering the enclosure. He would collect them when he was back. The cold struck the skin his cloak had covered, making his shoulders tighten. Hermione stepped forward, stopped and glanced at Draconia. Whatever question passed between them needed no words; a moment later, Hermione wrapped both arms around him.

He stood startled for half a second before holding her back. Her cheek pressed against his bare shoulder. Her grip contained every warning she had run out of time to give.

"This doesn't answer anything," she whispered.

"I know."

"Good kind?"

"Good kind."

She let him go. Draconia did not move. Harry looked at her.

"Am I allowed to hug you?"

Her eyes flashed. Then she crossed the distance and collided with him. Draconia held him differently—one arm around his back, the other braced behind his head as though expecting something to tear him away before the whistle. Her breath shook once near his ear.

"Don't make me regret this," she said, so quietly he almost missed it.

"Very affectionate."

"Potter."

"I'm coming back."

Bagman raised the whistle to his lips. Draconia released Harry, her hand catching briefly at his wrist before she stepped back. Harry opened the tube and pulled out the gillyweed. It writhed across his palm, cold and slippery against his skin.

"That is disgusting," Hermione said.

"Helpful."

He forced the entire plant into his mouth. The texture was rubbery. The taste combined rotten fish, lake mud and something bitter enough to make his jaw ache. Harry chewed twice, failed to swallow and forced himself to chew again as the starting whistle split the morning. Cedric cast his Bubble-Head Charm and entered the lake; Fleur dived cleanly beside him while Krum began a transfiguration spell. Harry finally swallowed. For one second, nothing happened. Then pain opened along both sides of his neck, sharp as knives, and he fell to his knees.

The crowd's roar became confusion. Harry clawed at his throat. Air entered his mouth and found nowhere to go. Cold skin split beneath his fingers—not blood, but two sets of narrow gills opening and closing uselessly against the wind. His toes pulled together as webbing stretched between them, and his fingers followed, skin drawing into translucent fans. He dragged in another useless mouthful of air. Somewhere behind him, Hermione shouted his name. Harry looked at the lake, understood with the desperate certainty of something that could not wait, and threw himself into the black water after three stumbling steps.

Cold swallowed him. It struck through the thin swimming clothes, closed around his skull and emptied thought from his body. Harry sank beneath a cloud of bubbles with both hands still locked against his throat. Water entered the gills, and the pain changed. The first pull felt so much like drowning that instinct forced his mouth open. Lake water flooded it, bitter with silt and iron, but his lungs remained empty. Along his neck, the gills opened wider, drawing oxygen from the current before he could understand why the next frantic struggle for breath no longer came. Slowly, Harry stopped fighting. He let the water pass through again.

The cold remained brutal, but he could move through it now. His webbed hands pushed against the water with startling force, and one kick carried him beyond the shallows. A second took him down the steepening floor, leaving the Tournament above as a blur of pale sky and broken shadows. He lit his wand and turned towards the darkness below.

Green light spread only several feet before the dark consumed it. Tall weeds moved upon either side, bending in a current he could not feel against his transformed skin. Small silver fish turned together and vanished. Stones became vague shapes beneath layers of silt. The silence was not true silence: water carried the scrape of his own movement, distant clicks, the slow groan of ice along the bank and a pulse too deep to identify. He followed the slope west, tracing Hermione's map in his head: past the first shelf, clear of the reeds, then north where two drowned trees formed an arch. The village waited beyond the second drop. He repeated the directions once, fixing them against the unfamiliar shapes his wandlight found.

Harry swam faster than he had in the prefects' pool. Gillyweed transformed effort into speed. His webbed feet caught the water like fins. Fingers that had looked fragile upon shore became broad paddles. He passed the first shelf in minutes and descended until pressure tightened inside his ears. The cold found the unprotected centre of him gradually. Draconia's warming draught knocked against his belt. He flexed two fingers to check; he could still feel both. Not yet. Beyond the next stretch of weed, the drowned trees came into view. Their trunks leaned together beneath a curtain of weed, bark softened by years underwater. Pale growths clustered upon the branches. Something long and dark moved behind them. Harry stopped. The shape passed through his wandlight.

A pike longer than his arm regarded him with flat, ancient offence and continued into the dark. Harry released a breath through his gills.

"Friendly lake," he attempted to say.

The words escaped as bubbles, taking the complaint with them. He crossed beneath the trees, and something closed around his ankle. Harry twisted, dragging his wandlight down through the hanging weed. A grindylow clung below him, green skin almost indistinguishable from the weed. Horns rose from its skull. Its face widened around sharp teeth while long fingers wrapped twice around Harry's leg. Hagrid's instruction came back to him: break the grip at the wrist. Harry struck down with the heel of his free foot, but the creature hissed bubbles and tightened its hold. A second hand caught his swimming clothes near the knee as three more grindylows emerged from the branches. He bent, caught the first creature's wrist with both webbed hands and wrenched sideways until its grip broke. Before he could kick clear, another launched itself onto his back.

Claws raked his shoulder. His wandlight spun through weed and darkness. Harry brought the wand against his chest.

"Relashio!"

Underwater, the spell became a burst of scalding bubbles that struck the grindylow on his back and spread through the weed. The creature shrieked and released him. Harry drove upwards, only for another to catch the flask at his belt and jerk him sideways. The buckle tore; the warming draught fell. He lunged after it, but the grindylow reached it first, closing its long green fingers around the glass. The bottle shattered, and gold potion streamed into the lake. Harry reached uselessly through it as the current carried it away.

"Oh, you horrible little—"

Water swallowed the insult. Harry cast again. Boiling bubbles scattered the creatures. He kicked through the gap before they recovered, crossed the drowned arch and did not stop until wandlight revealed only open water behind him. Four red lines burned across his shoulder. His hands were beginning to numb.

Upon shore, eight minutes had passed. Hermione knew because she had watched every second. The enchanted clock beside Bagman's table had one hand and sixty marks. Hermione had thought that would make the wait easier to measure. Instead, the hand crept whenever she stared at it and moved unforgivably fast whenever she looked towards the water. Fleur's entry point had stopped rippling. A trail of bubbles marked Cedric for several yards and disappeared; Krum had entered with the head of a shark and a human body, an incomplete transformation so alarming that she spent twenty seconds wondering whether it had gone wrong. Harry had left nothing. The lake closed over him, smoothed itself and continued reflecting the white sky.

Draconia stood beside her at the rail. One of her gloves lay upon the bench behind them. She had removed it to fasten Harry's wand harness and forgotten it when he ran towards the water.

"The gills worked," Hermione said.

"He didn't resurface."

"Exactly."

Draconia looked at the black water. "All that proves is he's still under there."

Hermione's fingers tightened on the rail. She could explain the transformation, its intended duration and the signs that showed it had begun correctly. None of that told her what was happening now. Remus stood on Draconia's other side with Harry's answering mirror inside his coat. He had pushed his stick beneath the bench, where it would not obstruct anyone if he needed to move. Lucius and Narcissa had descended from the seats and waited several steps behind them. The place Sirius would have occupied remained empty. Hermione kept almost turning towards it. There ought to have been somebody making an inappropriate joke, telling Harry he looked ridiculous in those swimming clothes, promising to come down if the judges forgot what mattered. Instead, the person who usually said those things was another body beneath the lake.

Remus glanced at the clock. His wand lay against the inside of his wrist, visible only when the wind lifted his sleeve. Nine minutes. Nobody spoke of marks.

Below them, Harry found the second shelf. The lake floor fell away so abruptly that Harry checked his movement as though he had reached the edge of a cliff. His wandlight stretched beyond it and found nothing to catch on; the water deepened from green to a black that seemed to press back against the light. Harry hovered above the drop, moving his feet just enough to remain level. Somewhere below, the song from the egg had begun again. Its separate notes trembled through his ribs before he could distinguish them with his ears.

He descended towards the song, angling his kicks to keep the current from turning him. Pressure tightened behind his eyes, and his gills worked faster. The cold had changed from pain to absence: he could still move his hands, but had to watch to be certain his fingers were obeying. At least they all remained attached. Draconia would regard that standard as inadequate; remembering her expression helped him steady the wand.

Shapes appeared below. At first he mistook them for rocks. Then one square of darkness resolved into a doorway and another into a rough window. Stone houses clustered across the lake bed, their walls built from boulders, their roofs woven from weed which streamed in the current. Gardens moved around them. Strings of pearls caught his wandlight and returned it in dim points, marking narrow passages between the buildings.

Merpeople emerged from the streets. Their grey skin shone like fish bellies, long green hair drifting around faces which turned towards him together. Yellow eyes followed his hands. Some carried spears; others held ropes attached to horned creatures which watched Harry with considerably less patience. Children stood in the doorways, small knives clenched in webbed fingers. None advanced upon him, but the space through which he swam grew crowded with witnesses. He lowered his wand enough to show respect without losing light. The song led towards the village centre, where a stone merperson stood with both arms extended. Four humans hung beneath them, held upright by ropes of braided waterweed. Harry recognised Sirius's boots first. One lace had come loose, trailing beside the ankle in a way he had seen a hundred times at Hawthorn. The boots hung above the floor, toes pointing slightly downward, while the long black coat between them and the statue moved in slow folds. Sirius's head rested against one shoulder. His hair had slipped from its tie and drifted across his face, hiding everything except a pale cheek and the corner of his mouth. Harry had spent the morning preparing himself for this sight. None of the images he had forced himself to consider had been as bad as finding a familiar face so still.

He kicked forward too hard and struck the statue with one knee. Silt rose around the boots. Sirius did not move. Harry caught his jaw, pushed the hair aside and put two fingers beneath it, searching for the pulse Remus had said he would find. His hand shook badly enough that at first he felt only his own blood beating through the transformed fingers. He adjusted them, made himself wait, and found it: slow, steady, entirely unconcerned by the terror gathering above it. A clear membrane covered Sirius's mouth and nose. It brightened with each breath, then faded to a thin shimmer against his skin. Beneath his left cuff, the separate safeguard glowed silver. Harry watched the membrane brighten twice more and checked the silver safeguard again before his grip began to loosen. The spells were working. Remus had told him they would, and Sirius had seen them checked. He made himself hold on to that.

Sirius looked asleep. He had looked like this in the cottage after falling asleep over a book, one hand still holding it open, expecting the room to remain his when he woke. Harry wanted that room around him now. He wanted lamplight upon shelves and the noise of Remus moving a cup. Instead, he pushed a strand of hair away from the membrane, then another, with the same careful gesture Sirius used when Harry woke from a nightmare. He had never thought about how often his godfather did it. Recognising the movement in his own hand made his throat ache.

He checked the watch Hermione had charmed to work underwater. Twenty-nine minutes. There was time to return safely, time to stop looking at Sirius as though the protective magic required Harry's fear to keep working. Only then did he examine the others. Cho hung beside Sirius, her dark hair moving around her Ravenclaw robes. On the statue's opposite side floated a broad-shouldered Durmstrang boy Harry recognised from Krum's table. The fourth hostage was a little girl, silver-blonde hair spread across the water behind her. Gabrielle Delacour.

Her bare feet hardly reached the statue's lowest carving. The same breathing membrane protected her face, and a warming flask marked with a pearl D hung from her hostage line. Fleur had prepared for her and would be on her way. Harry held those facts carefully, resisting the memories the small, motionless figure stirred. The child beneath the stairs had possessed neither certainty, but Gabrielle was not that child. He drew the knife from his calf sheath and turned back to Sirius.

The first rope resisted the blade. On the second stroke, silver sap clouded the water. The third separated it. Harry caught Sirius beneath the arm before cutting the lower binding, keeping his own shoulder against the statue while the freed body began to turn in the current. One alone. One returned. One debt answered.

"Gabrielle!"

The name crossed the stands. Hermione's eyes went to the clock and then to the place Harry had entered. Draconia's bare hand closed around her forearm.

"He doesn't know."

"He'll wait."

"Until the hour."

Below, the merpeople began counting in song. Harry checked the watch. Forty-seven minutes. No light had appeared above the houses since Cedric's, and the village entrance remained empty. He pointed towards it, then at Gabrielle. The merperson with the shark-tooth necklace shook her head. When Harry dragged one finger across his other wrist to indicate cutting the rope, her spear lowered between them. Warning. Hagrid had made him understand the difference before the task. At fifty minutes, the cold had become an obstacle inside his joints. Moving required a separate decision for each limb. Sirius's coat pulled against the harness knot, and the chill which had reached Harry's chest made every breath through his gills feel smaller. He could go now. He could carry his godfather back, tell the judges about Gabrielle and trust them to do what they had promised. Across the statue, Gabrielle's hand moved in the current. Her fingers opened, brushed the stone and closed upon nothing.

Gabrielle's face returned. Her own face, peaceful beneath the charm. This was a choice he was making; nobody had asked him to pretend it was sensible. Around him, the song changed again, warning that ten minutes remained.

The merperson's yellow eyes followed each movement. Her spear remained steady. Beneath it, a waterproof label identified the standard warming draught they had studied in the classroom. He checked the dosage before pulling the stopper free. A faint trace of cloves reached him through the water as a little of the draught escaped the neck of the flask.

Two fingers had no feeling. The third had begun to follow them. Harry pressed the flask beneath one gill and poured, letting the transformed tissue draw the potion in with the water. Heat spread through him in painful waves, reaching hands and feet he had nearly stopped considering part of himself. He flexed his fingers, feeling pins and needles bite where there had been nothing. Hermione's warning returned as clearly as her instructions about the knot: warming his circulation might make the transformation end sooner. He checked the watch again. Fifty-three minutes. He had used up the easy choices. Harry took Sirius's shoulder, settled him against his side and drew his wand. The spear levelled. He aimed past it, at the rope above Gabrielle's head.

"Diffindo."

The spell reached the waterweed as a thin line of white. It frayed the rope, leaving pale fibres trailing from a notch. Three merpeople surged forward. Harry twisted beneath the nearest spear and cast again; its shaft struck his wrist, knocking the wandlight towards the floor. He pushed the wand into its harness before it could leave his hand and reached for the knife.

He was frightening them now. He saw it in their sudden movements and in the way the leader's free hand reached towards Sirius's sleeve. A child retreated behind the nearest doorway. The merpeople were trying to stop him from turning a guarded village into a fight, and he was making it harder for them. He had come into their home, accepted their protection and decided his fear knew better than their rules. The thought remained with him. So did Gabrielle. Harry cut at the weakened rope. Silver sap clouded the blade. A hand caught his uninjured shoulder, and he drove his elbow back, breaking its grip without looking. Sirius turned in the current. The harness pulled sharply across Harry's ribs and forced him against the statue.

The knife slid along a carving. Harry caught it by the handle before the blade could reach his own leg. His heart hammered hard enough that the gills opened wide beneath the collar. He braced his wrist and made one more cut. The rope stretched, split, and released Gabrielle into the current. Harry caught her beneath the arm and brought her against his chest just as Sirius drifted outward on the fastening, pulling him the other way. He had a child who could not hold him, an adult who could not help him and hands which had begun to shake despite the draught. The merpeople closed around them.

Harry put away the knife. He made himself meet the leader's eyes and pointed to the watch, then to the open water. She looked at Gabrielle, at Sirius and at the scratches across Harry's shoulder. Her mouth tightened. For a moment she simply looked at him, and Harry became horribly aware of how little time he had left to argue. Whatever she decided, it took longer than he wanted and less time than his conduct deserved. She struck her spear against the stone, and the circle opened. Harry checked the harness knot once more, secured Sirius beneath his left arm and settled Gabrielle against his right. Then he kicked towards the surface before anybody changed their mind. The watch read fifty-six minutes.

The first upward movement told him what carrying both would cost. Water supported them while they were still, but moving turned every loose sleeve and trailing limb into resistance. Sirius's coat opened like a dark sail behind them, almost cancelling Harry's first kick. He dragged it closer, gathered Gabrielle higher against his shoulder and tried again, settling into a rhythm he hoped he could keep. The village shrank beneath his feet. Its windows became points of light inside a depth he could no longer measure. He aimed towards the faint greyness above. His fingers cramped around Sirius's belt, preventing him from changing his grip. Gabrielle's hair kept crossing his face. He turned his head away from it and felt the harness tighten again as the current pulled his godfather outward. Fifty-eight minutes.

Harry pressed both bodies against himself and drew his wand, careful not to lose the support of the harness. His first lightening charm ran along Sirius's sleeve without holding. Remembering how spells had bent in the pool, he altered the angle and cast again. This time the coat lifted against his arm, easing some of the drag. He kicked harder, reaching for the next patch of grey above them, and felt his left gill close.

Pain dragged along his neck as the slit sealed beneath smoothing skin. His mouth opened before he could stop it, filling with bitter water. The remaining gill pulled harder, but beneath it his chest had begun the terrible, familiar struggle to breathe. His lungs were remembering what they required, and he had nothing to give them. Webbing began shrinking between his fingers; the fastening he had tied so securely became more difficult to hold. Too soon. The cold, the effort and the draught had brought the change forward exactly as Hermione warned. Harry tried to kick faster. His feet were losing their broad, useful shape. Sirius's boot knocked against his knee. Gabrielle slipped, and Harry pulled her across his chest, pressing his chin against her hair to keep her from turning away from him.

Above, the light seemed to remain in the same place. He wanted Sirius awake. The wish came with a desperation that made him feel much younger than fourteen. Sirius would know what to do. He would take hold of Harry and make a joke once there was air enough for it, then be angry at the people who had designed this while Harry sat somewhere warm. For one awful moment, Harry wanted nothing else: someone else to hold on, someone else to know how to get them home. Sirius remained asleep against him. Harry tightened his grip and kicked again. The last gill fluttered. A breath passed through it too slowly, leaving his chest cramped around the air he could not take. Dark flecks moved across the greyness above.

All back.

Cedric's hand had closed around his upon the shore when saying it was still easy. Harry trapped Gabrielle against his right forearm and forced the wand upward between her shoulder and Sirius's. It almost slipped from his fingers. He caught it with the two which still obeyed him, expelled the water from his mouth in a burst of bubbles and tried to make the word large enough for all three.

"Ascendio!"

The spell caught the harness and the bodies pressed against it, throwing them upward together. Pressure tore at Harry's ears as Sirius's loose coat streamed behind them and Gabrielle's hair flattened across his arm. His right gill sealed. His chest convulsed around the demand for air, but Harry held on, fingers locked in cloth he could scarcely feel. Above them, the pale surface widened until its ripples became sharp and silver. They broke through.

Air entered Harry like another injury. He coughed, lost hold of Sirius and caught the back of his coat before the harness dragged him sideways. Gabrielle floated against his right arm, her head tipped beneath his chin. The lake's noise changed all at once: shouting from the stands, the slap of water against his ears, someone calling his name repeatedly from far beyond the place his feet could touch. The hostage charms broke at the surface, and Sirius inhaled sharply. His eyes opened without understanding. For a moment he fought the water around him, one hand striking outward before closing on Harry's arm.

"Harry?"

Harry tried to answer. The next cough bent him forward and put his mouth underwater. He kicked, but ordinary feet had replaced the webbed ones, and the lightening charm upon the coat had collapsed with the ascent. Keeping three heads above water took strength he no longer had. Sirius saw it before Harry could explain; his arm went beneath Gabrielle's back, taking the strain from Harry's cramped hand. The child coughed and began to cry, thinly at first, then with a force that made Harry want to cry too. She had air. Whatever else happened, she had air.

"Face up, Harry. Look at me," Sirius said, bringing him closer.

Harry tried. His neck felt stripped raw where the gills had closed, and the scratches upon his shoulder had become a burning edge beneath the cold. Sirius turned towards the shore, keeping Gabrielle across one arm while the other remained locked around Harry. He could swim, but the harness bound him to a boy whose limbs were no longer answering properly. They made one awkward movement, then another. Harry tried to find the knife with his free hand. His fingers touched the sheath and slid off it.

"Leave it," Sirius said, tightening his arm. "I've got you."

A dark shape rose through the water beside them, and Cedric broke the surface inside the remains of his Bubble-Head Charm. He had come back from the bank. Harry scarcely had time to recognise him before a hand caught him beneath the uninjured arm and lifted his mouth clear of the water.

"Can you kick?"

Harry managed one movement. It achieved very little.

"We'll call that a yes," Cedric said, tightening his hold. "I've got the rest."

Sirius looked at him, gratitude plain even through the water running down his face. Neither had breath for the words that would have gone with it. Draconia took Gabrielle, holding the struggling child close while she found firmer footing. "Slowly," Remus said.

"What happened?"

"Fleur didn't come."

"I know."

"I couldn't leave her."

The blanket rustled. Sirius's breath reached Harry's hair, unsteady and warm. Harry could hear his heart through the layers between them. He had spent much of the ascent trying to imagine that sound and wishing somebody else would take responsibility for keeping it possible.

"Look at me," Sirius said.

"Mr Diggory returned first with his hostage and receives excellent marks for speed, technique and completion!"

"Mr Krum arrived second, completing an ambitious—if somewhat incomplete—transfiguration and successfully recovering his hostage."

Durmstrang stamped in unison.

Fleur kissed the top of Gabrielle's head. Bagman looked towards Harry.

"Mr Potter reached the hostages before any other champion."

Draconia pressed the warming stone harder against his shoulder.

"Ow."

"I'd gathered that much."

Harry looked at him. The tent held rows of empty cots, a basket of clean towels and a half-folded screen. "You are," Harry said.

Sirius swallowed. "I know."

"Good kind."

Sirius laughed unevenly, and Harry rose before either could discuss why he ought to remain seated. He crossed the space between the cots. "I was frightened," Harry said.

"So was I when I woke."

"I didn't mean to make you—"

"Harry."

He stopped. Sirius held him another moment before drawing back enough to look at him.

"Next time," he said, "could you ask me before you agree?"

"Yes."

"Even if they say the protections are brilliant."

"Especially then."

"I should have known I'd find you both out of bed," he said.

"Hermione," Harry said.

"I need to check your temperature again in seven minutes."

"Hermione. Look at me."

She turned. Her mouth trembled once, and the diagnostic chart slipped from beneath her hand. "Late."

A joke rose to Harry's lips, ready to turn late into something small enough to laugh about. He saw her face and let it go.

"I'm sorry."

"You had Sirius. I kept telling myself you couldn't stay with someone else when you were already bringing him back. Then I thought about Gabrielle, and I knew you could. I knew you would." Her breath broke. "I couldn't make either thought go away. I just stood there."

He took her hands. They were warmer than his, though the fingers trembled just as badly.

"No."

The agreement made her cry harder. Harry put his arms around her, and she clung to him, pressing her cheek against his neck. Draconia stood close enough that her sleeve touched his. "He was asleep," she said. "Everyone kept saying you had Sirius, as if that meant you weren't on your own. He couldn't help you." Her fingers tightened again. "You were doing it again."

Harry turned enough to see her face. She looked at the empty cot where Sirius had been sitting. The warming stone lay upon it now, grey and ordinary, unable to do anything with the fear she had carried from the shore.

"Cedric came back," Harry said.

"I saw."

"I needed him. I couldn't do the last part alone."

Draconia's expression tightened. He had given her the truth, and he watched it hurt her in a way another promise might not have. She stepped towards them, putting one arm around Hermione's back and the other behind Harry's shoulders. Hermione shifted to make room without letting him go. Harry stood inside the awkward, careful embrace with their wet sleeves against him and their breathing close enough to hear. Nobody tried to explain what the closeness meant. They had wanted him back on shore. He was finally there, close enough to hold, and for a little while nobody asked for anything beyond that. Madam Pomfrey returned to find all three standing and asked why. They gave three different answers, none of which she accepted, before moving them firmly back towards the cot.

Hagrid invited them to tea that evening. Harry wore Luna's yellow fish socks beneath his boots because she had insisted they were intended for after the lake. Hermione had dried her robes. Draconia carried a repaired pair of gloves which she presented to Harry without acknowledging where the first pair went. The hut glowed against the winter dark.

Warmth met them the moment Hagrid opened the door. Fire crowded the grate, throwing amber light across the low rafters and turning the frost upon the windows into running water. Damp cloaks hung near the stove. The copper kettle rattled upon its hook, and Fang occupied the hearthrug with all four legs stretched towards the flames, exhausted by a day which appeared to have involved no movement at all. The table held a cake large enough to feed twelve people.

Blue icing covered the top in uneven waves. White sugar reeds leaned around the edges, several already collapsing beneath their own weight. Four figures stood in the centre: one with black icing hair, one brown, one silver-blonde and one tiny yellow shape with far too many limbs.

Harry removed his cloak. "Is that meant to be the giant squid?"

"Course," Hagrid said.

Hermione studied it. "It has nine tentacles."

"Lost count."

"Why are Hermione and I on the cake?" Draconia asked.

Hagrid looked from her to the cake, plainly bewildered by the question. "Yeh helped."

The answer silenced her. Harry glanced at Draconia. Firelight found the loose strands of silver-blonde hair near her face. She looked down at the three human figures, her fingers still resting on the edge of the table. Harry had seen her ready with an answer to almost anything. For once, she seemed content to leave Hagrid's alone.

"Also," Hagrid added, "didn' think Harry'd know which way ter swim without yeh."

"There it is," Harry said.

Draconia recovered. "I do prefer an accurate celebration."

Hermione touched the icing figure representing Harry. It tipped forward and landed face-first in a blue wave.

"More accuracy," she said.

Hagrid cut the cake with a knife better suited to clearing vegetation. The first slice contained most of the lake, half the squid and one of Hermione's sugar legs. Draconia asked for a piece containing fewer casualties. They crowded onto the bench opposite Hagrid. Harry sat in the middle because the other available chair had been claimed by a basket of bundled roots which hissed whenever anyone approached. Hermione's shoulder rested against his left. Draconia's touched his right whenever she lifted her cup. Neither contact felt accidental. Fang raised his head from the rug and looked towards Harry's repaired gloves.

"No," Harry told him.

Fang lowered his head again.

"He remembers," Harry said.

"He's waiting for you to become careless," Draconia replied.

"I'm choosing to call it respect."

"You would."

Hagrid poured the tea. It emerged dark enough to obscure the bottoms of their mugs.

"Yeh gave us a fright," he said.

Harry looked at the cake. "Everyone keeps saying that."

"Might be because yeh keep doin' it."

"The merpeople weren't going to hurt anyone."

Hagrid's brows drew together above his beard. "Wasn' talkin' about them."

The fire crackled in the quiet that followed. Harry felt Hermione's shoulder tense beside him, and Draconia lowered the cup she had been about to drink from. He looked down at the blue icing on his plate, suddenly aware of how closely they were all listening.

"I couldn't leave Gabrielle," he said.

"Know that." Hagrid's large hands closed around his mug. "Knew it soon as Fleur came out alone. Said ter Professor Sprout, 'Harry'll bring the little one.' She said yeh ought ter think of the time. Told her yeh would be thinkin' of it. Jus' wouldn' think it mattered more."

Harry pushed a ridge of icing across his plate with the edge of his fork, unsure whether Hagrid meant it as praise, worry or both.

"Sirius said he was proud."

"Good." Hagrid nodded firmly. "Should be."

"He also wants enough flotation charms to raise the entire village."

"Sensible."

Harry looked up. "You're supposed to disagree with that part."

"Could build yeh a boat," Hagrid said thoughtfully. "Got timber left from the chicken coop."

Hermione laughed into her tea.

Harry stared at her. "You're encouraging him?"

"I'm willing to consider every available safety measure."

"Draco?"

Draconia placed her cup down. "A boat would allow you to complain from above the water."

"Wonderful. You're all designing my transport now."

"It would have cushions," Hermione said.

"And books," Draconia added.

"I'm not sure either improves a boat."

"Keeping you in it would," Draconia said. "We could start there."

The laughter which followed was quieter than the helpless fit Fang had caused before the task. It did not need surprise to escape them. It moved naturally through the hut, mingling with the hiss of the fire and the rattle of sleet beginning against the windows. When it faded, they stayed comfortably crowded together, cups warming their hands. Hagrid rose to fetch more wood. Hermione noticed blue icing on Harry's cheek and reached for it with her thumb, then stopped an inch from his face. Her eyes met his as she seemed to realise what she had been about to do. From his other side, Draconia watched.

For a second, Harry felt the uncertainty of the staircase after the Ball again, when even a small movement had seemed capable of making everything worse. This time, he leaned the remaining inch into Hermione's hand. Her thumb brushed away the icing, gentle against his cheek. Neither spoke, and neither pulled back. Draconia unfolded a napkin and removed another streak from the opposite side of Harry's jaw with considerably more force than the task required.

"Ow."

"You missed some," she told Hermione.

Hermione's mouth curved. "Thank you."

Harry looked between them. "There must be easier ways to eat cake."

"You haven't managed one yet," Draconia said.

Hagrid returned with an armful of logs and gave no sign of finding anything unusual about them. There was still plenty the three of them had not settled. Hermione still had reasons to be angry, and Draconia's fear had not vanished because Harry was beside her now. He still did not know what both could become without hurting someone, and closeness alone would not answer that. For tonight, though, neither girl had moved away. Harry thought of Sirius's letter, now dry and safely folded inside his pocket. Tomorrow, he could call his godfather and find him at home. Remus would send another report about the gillyweed. Hermione would insist upon checking the scratches, and Draconia would find a reason to object to the condition of his gloves.

Tonight, they were here. Harry shifted on the bench to give Hermione more room, and Draconia's knee touched his as she leaned forward for the cake. He passed her the plate, watching her attempt to rescue the silver-blonde icing figure from a collapsing wave. Sleet rattled against the windows outside. Hagrid put another log on the fire, and Harry stayed between his friends while the kettle began to boil again.
