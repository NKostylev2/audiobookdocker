4

The portrait hole opened upon a Gryffindor common room that showed no intention of sleeping.

Students occupied every chair, sofa and stretch of carpet surrounding the fire. Younger pupils sat cross-legged beneath the windows while several older ones had dragged chairs into a rough circle near the hearth, their forgotten books lying open around their feet. The fire burned far too high for the mild autumn night, filling the room with dry heat and throwing restless shadows across the red hangings. Beyond the dark windows, rain drove against the glass in silver lines.

News had outrun Harry and Hermione.

The moment they climbed through the portrait hole, every conversation stopped. Faces turned towards them from every corner of the room, and the sudden silence possessed a physical weight. Harry remained with one foot upon the final step, considering whether he could reverse into the corridor before anyone noticed.

Hermione caught his sleeve.

"What happened?" someone called.

"Is Mrs Norris dead?"

"Did you really find her?"

"What's the Chamber of Secrets?"

"Why was your wand out?"

"It wasn't," Harry said.

His answer produced another wave of questions. Students rose from the carpet, craned over the backs of chairs and crowded close enough that Harry caught fragments of at least four different versions of the evening. In one, Mrs Norris had been skinned. In another, the message had appeared in fire. Someone near the staircase whispered that Harry's hands had been covered in blood.

Hermione stepped in front of him.

"Professor Dumbledore says Mrs Norris was petrified," she announced. Her voice carried across the room with a clarity Professor McGonagall might have envied. "She is alive, and no one knows who attacked her. Harry and I found her after leaving the feast. That is all."

The crowd quietened. Several older students exchanged glances, apparently embarrassed by how far their own stories had already travelled.

Dean leaned around the arm of a chair. "Why did you leave?"

Harry glanced at Hermione.

"A private matter," she said.

No one appeared willing to challenge the tone.

Harry silently thanked every book that had taught Hermione how to sound like Professor McGonagall. She took his arm and steered him through the room before the questions recovered, finding two chairs tucked into the narrow space between a bookcase and the far side of the fireplace.

The heat pressed against Harry's face. From here, the conversations behind them blurred into a low current of speculation, occasionally interrupted by the scrape of furniture or a sharply repeated word. The Chamber. The Heir. Mrs Norris. His own name surfaced often enough that he stopped attempting to decide who had spoken it.

Hermione waited until the rest of the room cautiously resumed talking.

"You should have told me exactly what the voice said."

Harry lowered himself into the chair. "I've told you now."

"After Professor McGonagall asked."

"You were there when I said it wanted to kill."

"You mentioned that part. You did not say it called you towards it, and you certainly didn't mention that it was hungry."

"I was distracted by the cat hanging from the wall."

"That does not make it unimportant."

"I didn't say it was unimportant."

Hermione folded her arms.

Harry stared into the fire. Coals shifted beneath a collapsing log, sending sparks spiralling up the chimney. The flames had begun to take the shape of the letters on the wall, however determinedly he tried not to see them.

"Do you think I imagined it?"

"No."

He turned towards her.

Hermione answered without hesitation or even the smallest attempt to soften the certainty.

"You heard something I couldn't," she continued. "That doesn't mean you made it up."

Harry rubbed his palms against his knees. "I'm the only one who heard it."

"I know." Hermione pulled her chair a little nearer. "We'll try to find out why."

Harry returned his attention to the fire. Hermione possessed an irritating habit of finding the question beneath the one he spoke aloud.

Her posture eased.

"I believe you, Harry."

The assurance was quiet enough that nobody else heard it, yet it cut through every whisper carrying his name. Some of the pressure beneath his ribs loosened. He had not realised how tightly he was holding himself until breathing became easier.

Outside, wind drove another burst of rain against the windows. The common room remained crowded and suspicious, but Hermione's certainty raised a small barrier between Harry and everyone else.

"What about Draconia?" he asked.

Hermione followed his gaze into the fire.

"She lied as well."

"She might really have been ill."

"She forgot whether she attended lunch."

"I noticed."

"She also brought her satchel to the corridor despite claiming she had only just woken."

"I noticed that too."

Hermione examined him. Firelight caught the loose edges of her hair and turned them copper.

"Do you think the notebook was inside it?"

Harry remembered the hard rectangular shape pressing against the green leather when Draconia's hand moved towards the buckle. He had seen only the outline, but he knew what fitted it.

"Yes."

"Then we ask her tomorrow."

"She'll hate that."

"She may."

"She'll tell us it is none of our business."

"Then we remind her of the rules we made at the Manor."

Harry rested his head against the chair. The embroidered lion beneath his cheek felt rough and overheated.

"No concealing dangerous things because you think you can handle them."

"Precisely."

"We don't know that the notebook is dangerous."

Hermione leaned closer, lowering her voice as two third-years passed behind them.

"She won't tell us about it, Harry. Then she misses a whole day and turns up where Mrs Norris was attacked. We ought to ask. That's all."

Harry stared at her.

"When you put everything together, it sounds bad."

"I'm worried too. Pretending we haven't noticed won't help her."

He could not argue with that.

Across the room, laughter rose abruptly and died just as quickly. Harry pictured Draconia beneath the red message, her face drained of colour and her fingers locked around his wrist. She had defended him before anyone else did. Whatever she had forgotten during the day, she remembered him the instant she saw Filch turn upon him.

"We ask her tomorrow," he agreed.

Hermione nodded.

They remained beside the fire until the common room began to empty. Neither attempted homework. By the time Harry climbed the staircase to the boys' dormitory, the rain had softened, though water continued tapping against the windows through most of the night.

Sleep came in brief, shallow stretches. Each time Harry drifted off, a voice moved somewhere beyond the curtains of his bed, whispering through pipes and stone. Whenever he opened his eyes, he found only darkness and the steady breathing of the other boys.

By breakfast, the story had acquired seven victims, three monsters and a secret passage beneath Harry's bed.

Seamus had heard that Mrs Norris died and was resurrected by Dumbledore. Dean had been told that the message was written in human blood. A third-year claimed Harry had been discovered laughing beneath it, which would have been impressive considering Harry could barely remember breathing.

He abandoned the Gryffindor table halfway through his toast.

The Great Hall felt larger that morning. Gaps appeared along the benches where students had chosen to sit nearer friends, and conversation moved in guarded waves beneath the pale ceiling. Whenever the doors opened, heads turned. Even the owls sweeping down with the post attracted nervous glances until letters and parcels replaced the brief fear of wings.

Draconia sat at the Slytherin table.

Her place was no longer conspicuously empty. She wore immaculate robes, her tie rested perfectly beneath her collar and her pale hair had been fastened neatly away from her face. Anyone looking casually might have believed nothing happened the previous day.

Harry no longer looked casually.

Shadows darkened the skin beneath her eyes. Her breakfast remained untouched, and although Pansy Parkinson spoke close to her ear, Draconia's attention remained fixed upon the silver handle of her knife. It took Pansy touching her arm before she reacted.

Then Draconia raised her head and found Harry immediately.

The speed of it suggested she had known where he was throughout the meal.

She stood. Several Slytherins watched her leave the table, their interest too carefully disguised to be accidental. Pansy caught her sleeve and whispered something which Harry could not hear. Draconia replied without turning. Whatever she said made Pansy release her.

Hermione joined Harry beside the doors with two books pressed against her chest.

Draconia passed them without greeting and continued into the entrance hall.

"That appears to be an invitation," Hermione said.

"She could try words."

"She knows we'll follow."

"That doesn't improve it."

They followed anyway.

Draconia led them into an unused classroom tucked beneath the marble staircase. Dust softened the edges of the abandoned desks, and the air smelled of chalk, old wood and the faint dampness that crept into rooms without regular fires. A faded diagram of a Hungarian Horntail occupied most of the board. Its painted wings opened and closed with exhausted irregularity, stirring small clouds of chalk dust whenever they struck the frame.

Morning light entered through a high window and divided the room into alternating bands of gold and shadow.

Draconia shut the door. She drew her wand and traced a small circle around the handle.

A faint shimmer moved across the wood before vanishing.

Hermione's attention sharpened. "That isn't on our curriculum."

"My education did not begin at Hogwarts."

"Your father taught you?"

"My mother. Father's silencing charms generally prevent the person rather than the room from making noise."

Harry imagined Lucius Malfoy considering this a meaningful improvement upon ordinary privacy.

Draconia turned towards him.

"What did the voice say?"

Harry had expected Hermione to begin the interrogation. Draconia's directness unsettled him more than Hermione's questions would have done.

"You heard us last night?"

"Professor Snape told me."

"Everything?"

"Enough to know you concealed part of it from Dumbledore."

There was no accusation in her voice. That made Harry feel worse.

He glanced towards Hermione. She gave a small nod.

"It told me to come to it," Harry said. "Then it said it was hungry."

Draconia waited.

Harry watched her face as he added, "The last thing it said was that it wanted to kill."

Every trace of movement left her.

It was not the controlled stillness she used when Pansy annoyed her or a professor challenged her. That stillness possessed intent. This arrived too quickly, as if some invisible hand had reached inside Draconia and stopped every part of her at once.

"Where did you first hear it?"

"In the Great Hall."

"That isn't possible."

"I know."

"The hall is protected by centuries of wards. A disembodied voice could not simply—" She stopped, one hand tightening around her wand. "You heard it through the walls?"

"It moved through them."

Draconia glanced towards the stones beside the door. Morning light touched only half her face, leaving the rest in shadow.

Hermione observed her carefully. "Does that mean something to you?"

"No."

The answer came before the question had fully left the air.

Harry stepped closer.

"Where were you yesterday?"

Draconia's gaze returned to him. "I told you. I was asleep."

"You thought you went to lunch."

"I was confused."

"You took your bag when you left the dormitory."

"Apparently."

"What was inside it?"

Her features cooled. "Books."

"And the notebook?"

The Horntail continued beating its tired wings against the board. Somewhere in the corridor, students hurried towards breakfast, their footsteps passing beyond the silenced door without entering the room.

Draconia's hand moved towards the satchel at her side.

Hermione noticed.

"Your mother packed it," she said. "Have you asked her why?"

"I have not yet had the opportunity."

"You write to her every Sunday."

"I had other matters to discuss."

"Draco," Harry said.

Her eyes snapped towards him. For an instant, fear replaced irritation, naked enough that Harry almost regretted speaking.

"We aren't accusing you."

"It sounds remarkably similar."

"We're worried about you."

"I had a headache. I fell asleep. Why does it have to mean something worse?"

"You lost most of a day."

"I was tired."

"You forgot you were supposed to meet us."

The words struck their target more directly than Harry intended. Draconia's chin lifted, but the hurt reached her eyes before she could prevent it.

Harry wished at once that he had chosen another way to say it.

"I apologised."

"That isn't what I meant."

"Then what did you mean?"

"You wouldn't usually forget."

The anger in her face wavered. She glanced towards the dusty window, avoiding both of them.

Hermione moved closer, and when she spoke again the sharpness had left her voice.

"Let us examine the notebook. If it is an ordinary enchanted object, we'll apologise and return it."

"No."

The refusal was immediate.

Harry felt the same cold unease that followed him from the locked classroom several weeks earlier.

"Why not?"

"Because it belongs to me."

"Then prove it isn't hurting you."

Draconia's face changed.

Her fingers touched the satchel buckle. Harry heard the quiet scrape of metal against leather and, for one hopeful second, thought she meant to open it.

The classroom door rattled.

All three spun towards it.

Someone on the other side pulled the handle twice, muttered that Peeves had locked another room and walked away. Footsteps receded beneath the marble staircase.

The interruption lasted only seconds. It was enough.

Draconia released the buckle.

When she faced them again, the fear had disappeared behind familiar disdain.

"This is absurd. We have lessons."

"We also have an agreement," Hermione replied.

"Our agreement does not entitle you to search my belongings."

"No, but our friendship entitles us to notice when you're unwell."

Draconia recoiled.

The movement was small, yet Hermione saw it. Her expression softened before Draconia's defences hardened again.

"You can worry without going through my things, Granger."

"I'm not trying to control you."

"You decided what was wrong before asking whether anything was."

"You can't account for an entire day!"

"We'll stop," Harry said.

Both girls turned upon him.

Hermione stared. "Harry—"

"We'll stop for now."

Draconia's shoulders lowered by a fraction. Harry met her eyes and held them until he knew she was listening.

"But if you disappear again or forget another day, we tell Professor Snape. With or without your permission."

Draconia's face went blank.

"You would not."

"Yes," Harry said. "I would."

The certainty in his own voice surprised him. Caring about someone did not mean allowing them to vanish simply because they objected to being found. Harry had learnt that much during the summer.

Draconia searched his face, waiting for him to retreat or soften the promise into something easier. Harry did neither.

The bell rang before she could answer, its sharp note passing through the walls despite the silencing charm. The sound stirred the Horntail into renewed effort.

At last, Draconia inclined her head.

"Fine."

It was neither agreement nor surrender. Hermione opened her mouth to demand something clearer, but Draconia had already removed the charm.

They left the classroom together. Students filled the entrance hall now, hurrying towards staircases and side passages with bags striking against their robes. The familiar current divided around the three of them, but a narrow distance remained between Harry and Draconia where none usually existed.

At the first staircase, their timetables separated them.

Draconia turned towards Charms with the other Slytherins. Harry and Hermione followed the Gryffindors and Hufflepuffs towards History of Magic.

Draconia did not say goodbye.

History of Magic occupied a high, overheated classroom overlooking the eastern grounds. Tall windows had fogged around their edges, blurring the November sky and turning the distant forest into a dark smear against the horizon. A fire crackled beneath the blackboard with enough enthusiasm to make the front rows unbearable, while the back of the room remained cool and draughty.

Harry and Hermione chose seats near the middle.

Professor Binns drifted through the blackboard precisely as the final bell stopped ringing. He carried a bundle of translucent notes beneath one arm despite no longer needing either notes or arms. Without greeting the class, he opened to the previous week's page and resumed a lecture concerning the revised wand-tariff agreements of 1612.

Within ten minutes, half the room had surrendered consciousness.

A Hufflepuff near the window rested his forehead against an open textbook. Neville's quill slipped gradually from his fingers, leaving a long black line across his parchment. Someone in the back row attempted to charm notes into writing themselves and produced three pages consisting entirely of the word cauldron.

Harry resisted sleep by counting how often Professor Binns described an ordinance as "particularly significant."

He reached twelve before Hermione raised her hand.

Binns continued reading.

Her arm remained in the air.

After nearly a minute, the professor's pale gaze lifted from his notes.

"Miss—er—Miss Grant?"

"Granger, Professor."

"Yes. Quite. Is there a question regarding subsection four of the tariff revisions?"

"No, Professor. I wanted to ask about the Chamber of Secrets."

The effect was immediate.

Neville's head jerked upright. The Hufflepuff at the window removed his face from his book, revealing the title printed backwards across his forehead. Every quill stopped moving.

Professor Binns regarded Hermione with profound disappointment.

"My subject is history, Miss Granger. It does not include folklore."

"The message on the second-floor wall referred to the Chamber by name."

"A written reference does not transform a legend into historical fact."

"No, but legends sometimes preserve distorted accounts of real events."

Twenty students turned towards Binns with an attention he had failed to inspire through decades of teaching. He glanced across their suddenly alert faces as though suspecting some elaborate act of disrespect.

"The Chamber of Secrets," he began reluctantly, "is an unsupported tale arising from the well-documented disagreement between the founders of Hogwarts."

His voice retained its usual dry monotony, but nobody looked away.

Hogwarts had been established more than a thousand years earlier by Godric Gryffindor, Rowena Ravenclaw, Helga Hufflepuff and Salazar Slytherin. For years they taught together, accepting magical children from across Britain. Eventually, Slytherin's growing distrust of students born to Muggle families turned disagreement into open conflict.

"He wished admission restricted to children of established magical ancestry," Binns said. "The other founders refused. Slytherin subsequently departed the school."

Hermione's hand remained raised. "And the Chamber?"

Professor Binns sighed.

The sound was peculiar coming from someone who had not drawn breath in centuries.

"According to the legend, Slytherin constructed a concealed chamber before his departure. Within it, he supposedly left a monster which only his rightful heir could control. That heir would one day return, open the chamber and remove those whom Slytherin considered unworthy of magical education."

The classroom no longer felt warm.

Several students glanced towards Hermione and the other Muggle-born children in the room. A Hufflepuff girl lowered her quill, her knuckles whitening around the feather.

"Muggle-borns," she whispered.

Professor Binns continued as though she had not spoken.

"The castle has been searched repeatedly by exceptionally qualified witches and wizards. No chamber has ever been found. The tale is therefore unsupported, architecturally improbable and historically irresponsible."

Harry raised his hand.

Binns looked reluctant to acknowledge a second student but eventually pointed at him.

"Was it opened before?"

Everyone turned again.

Harry felt the attention more sharply this time. He had been found beneath the message. That fact travelled silently between the surrounding desks.

"There have been periods during which students claimed the Chamber had been opened," Binns replied. "The most recent occurred approximately fifty years ago, when a series of disturbances threatened the continued operation of the school."

"What sort of disturbances?"

"The matter was resolved."

"That wasn't the question."

Hermione kicked Harry beneath the desk.

He moved his ankle out of range.

Professor Binns's expression remained unchanged. "The individual believed responsible was removed from Hogwarts. Subsequent searches uncovered no chamber, no monster and no reliable evidence beyond student panic. That will be sufficient discussion of mythology."

"But if someone was removed—"

"We will return to the Goblin Liaison Act."

The question died beneath the renewed drone of his lecture. No one listened to another word.

When the bell rang, conversation erupted before Binns finished disappearing through the blackboard. Students crowded into the corridor, repeating pieces of the legend and filling its missing spaces with theories of their own. Harry and Hermione remained behind long enough to avoid being trapped near the door.

"It was opened," Harry said.

Hermione packed her notes with unusually forceful movements. "Something happened fifty years ago. That doesn't prove Binns's explanation."

"Someone was expelled."

"Someone was blamed."

Harry thought of Draconia's words in the unused classroom and the way suspicion had already begun gathering around anyone who fitted the story too neatly.

"That isn't always the same thing," he said.

Hermione looked at him.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

They carried the question to the library.

Their usual table stood in a recess beside rain-streaked windows, half-screened from the rest of the room by shelves devoted to medieval enchantments. Hermione collected books on the founders, the castle's construction and hereditary magical abilities until a defensive wall of leather and parchment surrounded her chair.

Harry received Hogwarts: A History and instructions to search the index.

"The Chamber isn't listed," he announced several minutes later.

"That doesn't mean it isn't mentioned elsewhere."

"It has seven hundred pages."

"Then you should begin."

"I have begun. I'm beginning to resent the author."

Hermione slid another book across the table.

Harry examined its cracked spine. "Was that necessary?"

"You looked underworked."

"I was holding the heaviest book."

"You weren't reading it."

"That is a separate complaint."

Draconia arrived twenty minutes later.

She emerged between the shelves carrying several books against her chest. Her satchel remained over one shoulder despite the warmth of the library. Without greeting either of them, she placed the books beside the third chair and sat.

Harry's gaze dropped towards the bag.

Draconia followed it.

"Do you intend to spend the entire afternoon staring at my satchel?"

"I hadn't decided."

"It will not become more interesting."

"That sounds like a challenge."

Hermione closed A Genealogical Examination of Britain's Oldest Houses before Harry could test the claim.

"Professor Binns told us the story of the Chamber."

Draconia's attention sharpened. "He willingly discussed something?"

"Not willingly."

"That is more believable."

Hermione explained the founders' disagreement, Slytherin's departure and the creature supposedly concealed within the school. Draconia listened without interruption. Only the gradual stillness of her hands betrayed that the story affected her at all.

When Hermione finished, Harry asked, "Have you heard it before?"

"Every old wizarding family has heard some version."

"And?"

"And most versions are told by people desperate to make their great-grandfather's portrait seem interesting."

Harry waited.

Draconia exhaled through her nose.

"Father considers the Chamber a useful fiction. Families claim secret knowledge of Slytherin whenever they want influence, money or an audience. According to him, anyone genuinely descended from Salazar Slytherin would possess better things to do than announce it over dinner."

Hermione's eyebrows rose. "That is unexpectedly sensible."

"I'll inform him you approve. He will be devastated."

"Does your family claim any connection to Slytherin?"

"No." Draconia showed no offence at the question. "We possess enough ancestors without inventing additional ones."

Rain whispered against the windows. Across the library, students occupied the tables nearest Madam Pince's desk, leaving the darker recesses between distant shelves deserted. Nobody moved through the room alone if they could avoid it.

"If the Chamber is real," Harry said, "someone here has to be the Heir."

"Or merely believes they are," Hermione replied.

"Would that make a difference?"

"It could. Belief provides motive without requiring genuine ancestry."

Draconia leaned back. "Both possibilities assume the attacker is a student."

Harry remembered the voice gliding through solid stone. "You think it might be a professor?"

"I think you ought to find out what happened before you blame someone."

Her gaze rested upon him.

Harry understood.

Students at a nearby table watched them over the edges of their books. The moment Harry noticed, three heads turned away at once.

"You know they're talking about you too," he said.

Draconia's mouth curved without humour. "I am a Malfoy in Slytherin. If the castle developed indigestion, someone would blame my family."

"You don't seem worried."

"I am accustomed to suspicion."

Harry was not.

Draconia's features softened, as if his silence supplied the answer.

"For what it is worth, anyone who believes you opened the Chamber is an idiot."

"That is worth surprisingly little at Hogwarts."

"Then I shall say it to each idiot individually."

"That could take months."

Hermione reached for a clean sheet of parchment. "I'm prepared to make a list."

Harry looked between them. Fear still pressed against the castle, the Chamber remained hidden and Draconia's green satchel continued hanging from her shoulder like an unanswered question. Nevertheless, warmth stirred beneath Harry's unease.

"Thank you."

Draconia glanced away first.

They spent the afternoon searching for a chamber no historian admitted existed and a monster no book could identify. Madam Pince forced them out shortly before dinner.

They found nothing.

Over the following weeks, Hogwarts began to arrange itself around fear.

Students stopped travelling alone. First-years waited outside classrooms until someone older passed, then followed several yards behind while pretending the arrangement was accidental. Doors that once stood open were closed after sunset, and common-room passwords changed so frequently that Neville began carrying Gryffindor's written upon his wrist.

Filch prowled the corridors with red-rimmed eyes. He appeared at the slightest sound, lamp held before him and one hand gripping a mop like a weapon. Anyone lingering near a torch bracket received an interrogation concerning their feelings towards cats.

Mrs Norris remained rigid in the hospital wing.

Lockhart announced on three separate occasions that he was "personally monitoring the situation." The precise nature of that monitoring remained unclear. His lessons now involved identifying dramatic foreshadowing in his own books and rehearsing the correct expression to wear when confronting an unknown assailant.

Harry continued listening to the walls.

He heard water rushing through pipes, doors closing on floors above him and portraits arguing after their occupants believed the corridors empty. Suits of armour complained whenever the cold stiffened their hinges. Peeves sang increasingly obscene verses about Filch from somewhere inside the ceiling.

The voice did not return.

Its silence offered no comfort. Every ordinary noise became suspicious: a pipe knocking behind a classroom wall, fabric whispering around a distant corner, the scrape of something small beneath the floor. Harry began turning towards sounds before anyone else registered them.

Draconia kept her promise in the strictest possible sense.

She did not disappear for another entire day.

She still missed meetings. Sometimes a folded parchment bird arrived with notice; sometimes she appeared late carrying an explanation prepared too carefully to be spontaneous. Quidditch practice had moved. Professor Snape detained her. Pansy required assistance. A staircase carried her in the wrong direction, despite Draconia knowing the castle's movements almost as well as Hermione.

Harry stopped challenging every excuse.

One Thursday, Draconia arrived at breakfast carrying her Astronomy textbook and claimed she had been revising in the library. She placed the book beside her untouched porridge and spent several minutes staring towards the windows.

Hermione waited until Draconia left.

"The library closes before Astronomy begins."

"Maybe she started early."

"The book is upside down."

Harry looked across the hall. Draconia's abandoned place remained visible between Pansy and Millicent. One hand had rested against the flap of her satchel throughout breakfast.

Another time, she entered Potions and arranged the ingredients for a lesson they had completed the previous week. Harry quietly removed a jar of dried porcupine quills before she could add them to the wrong mixture.

Draconia stared at the jar in his hand.

Understanding returned slowly.

"Thank you," she said.

There was no irritation in her voice. It worried him.

Near the middle of November, a notice appeared in every common room announcing the creation of a Duelling Club.

The parchment had been decorated with crossed silver wands and a moving portrait of Gilderoy Lockhart. Every few seconds, the miniature Lockhart drew his wand, smiled and bowed to an opponent who never appeared.

Hermione read the notice twice.

"Lockhart is organising it."

"Then perhaps the monster will die of embarrassment," Harry said.

The first meeting would take place that evening in the Great Hall. Every student could attend, although first- and second-years were instructed to restrict themselves to supervised defensive magic.

By dinner, the announcement had temporarily displaced every other subject. Students demonstrated spells with cutlery, argued about possible instructors and speculated whether the club would permit formal challenges. Fred and George Weasley established odds on which professor would suffer the first injury until Percy confiscated their parchment.

Harry found Draconia waiting beyond the Great Hall doors after the meal.

Hermione joined them carrying three books.

Draconia stared at the stack. "We are attending a practical club. Why have you brought reading material?"

"In case Lockhart attempts to teach."

"You believe a book can protect you?"

"One of them is very heavy."

Harry examined its reinforced spine. "That might work."

They followed the crowd back into the Great Hall.

The House tables had vanished. In their place, a long golden platform stretched beneath hundreds of floating candles, its polished surface throwing warm reflections across the surrounding floor. The enchanted ceiling displayed a clear night, though drifting smoke from enthusiastic wand demonstrations occasionally obscured the stars.

Students packed the edges of the hall. Excitement charged the air, carrying sparks of nervous laughter from one group to another. Older pupils boasted about spells they clearly could not perform, while first-years clutched their wands with the grim determination of people expecting immediate combat.

Lockhart stood at the centre of the platform in plum-coloured robes.

Professor Snape waited beside him, dressed entirely in black and looking like the physical consequence of someone else's poor decision.

Harry stopped near the platform.

"This may actually be worth attending."

Hermione followed his gaze. "Professor Snape knows how to duel."

"Professor Snape knows how to murder someone with an expression."

Draconia studied the two professors. "Lockhart may be in danger."

"That sounded hopeful," Hermione said.

"I believe in practical education."

Lockhart raised his wand. Red sparks burst from its tip and showered the platform, finally winning him enough silence to begin.

"My dear students, welcome! It has come to my attention that recent events have left some among you feeling vulnerable."

Snape's eyes travelled towards the stretch of wall where the message had appeared. Although the stone had been scrubbed clean, a faint shadow remained beneath the nearest torch.

"Fortunately," Lockhart continued, "Professor Dumbledore has permitted me to establish this modest club so that I may pass along some of the defensive knowledge acquired during my many adventures."

"Modest," Draconia murmured.

The golden platform reflected Lockhart from several directions.

"I have graciously invited Professor Snape to assist with tonight's demonstration. He assures me that he possesses some limited experience in duelling."

Snape inclined his head.

Harry recognised the warning in his face.

Lockhart did not.

The professors walked ten paces apart and turned. Lockhart bowed with a complicated movement involving one arm, a dramatic sweep of his robes and considerably more hair than the situation required. Snape barely lowered his head.

They raised their wands.

"One," Lockhart called.

Snape moved before the second count.

A jet of red light struck Lockhart squarely in the chest and launched him backwards. He landed in a tangle of plum robes, slid several feet along the platform and came to rest with his hair pointing in three separate directions.

Silence held for half a heartbeat.

Then laughter erupted through the hall.

Even Hermione failed to conceal hers. Draconia covered her mouth with one hand, though the movement did little to hide the satisfaction in her eyes.

Snape lowered his wand.

"Waiting for your opponent to finish counting is seldom wise."

Lockhart pushed himself upright and attempted to restore his hair with both hands.

"An excellent demonstration. Naturally, I allowed Professor Snape the opening in order to illustrate—"

"Your vulnerability?" Draconia suggested.

She spoke quietly.

Harry heard her. Unfortunately, so did Pansy, whose laughter carried far enough to make Lockhart look towards them.

He smoothed his robes and pretended not to notice.

"Students will now divide into pairs."

The hall dissolved into movement. Friends seized one another, older students pushed towards the open space and several first-years attempted to retreat before anyone selected them.

Harry turned towards Hermione and Draconia.

"Three is inconvenient."

"I'll observe," Hermione said.

"You only want to criticise."

"Observation is essential to improvement."

"She wants to criticise," Draconia translated.

Before they settled the matter, Snape's voice cut across the hall.

"Potter. Malfoy. The platform."

Nearby conversations stopped.

Harry looked towards him. "Why?"

"Because I instructed you to."

"That is technically an answer."

Draconia caught Harry's sleeve before he earned detention. Together they climbed onto the platform beneath the attention of the school.

Lockhart appeared delighted. "A friendly inter-House demonstration! Precisely the cooperative spirit Hogwarts requires."

"We don't have to be friendly," Draconia whispered.

Harry took his position opposite her. "You usually are."

"Only because encouragement has failed."

They raised their wands.

"No permanent injuries," Harry said.

"I make no promises concerning your hair."

"My hair already looks injured."

"A fair point."

They bowed.

Draconia changed the instant she straightened. Exhaustion remained in the shadows beneath her eyes, but training aligned the rest of her: shoulders balanced, wand held lightly and weight distributed across both feet. Her attention fixed upon Harry with such clarity that, for the first time in days, she appeared entirely present.

Harry smiled.

Her eyes narrowed. "What?"

"There you are."

Draconia's concentration faltered.

Only for an instant.

"Expelliarmus!"

Harry threw himself sideways. Red light passed close enough to warm his ear and struck the wall behind him in a shower of sparks.

"That was before the count!"

"Professor Snape just explained the disadvantages of waiting."

"You listened?"

"Occasionally."

Draconia sent another spell across the platform. Harry ducked, rolled behind it and came to his feet quickly enough to answer with a disarming charm.

A silver shield flared before her. The impact drove Draconia backwards, her shoes scraping over the polished surface, but she recovered and aimed a jinx at his legs.

Harry jumped over it.

"Show-off," she said.

"You started it."

They circled one another.

Students shouted from every side. Gryffindor and Slytherin attempted to turn the demonstration into a House contest, while Hermione stood near the platform with both arms folded and the severe concentration of someone already preparing several pages of criticism.

Harry feinted left.

Draconia anticipated him and shifted to block. At the last moment, Harry changed direction, caught her wand arm with a disarming charm and sent the wand spinning from her fingers.

He caught it before it struck the platform.

The hall erupted.

Draconia stared at her empty hand. Disbelief crossed her face, followed rapidly by offence.

Harry offered the wand back.

"You cheated."

"I changed direction."

"You usually trip when changing direction."

"I've been practising."

"Against what?"

"Furniture."

She accepted her wand. Their fingers brushed and separated.

Snape stepped onto the platform.

"An adequate demonstration of adaptation," he said. "Though Potter's stance remains appalling."

Harry looked down at his feet. "They kept me upright."

"By coincidence."

Lockhart hurried to join them. The front of his robes still bore dust from his fall.

"Yes, very energetic. Very spirited. However, perhaps we should demonstrate how one responds to a more unusual opponent."

Snape's attention shifted towards Draconia. He bent and murmured a word close to her ear.

She froze.

Harry saw the hesitation before anyone else noticed.

Draconia looked at Snape. His expression remained unreadable, but whatever she found there persuaded her. She turned back towards Harry and raised her wand.

"Do you know what you're doing?" Harry asked.

"Yes."

"That wasn't convincing."

"It was not intended to comfort you."

She drew a careful breath.

"Serpensortia."

White light struck the platform between them.

A long black shape poured from it.

The snake landed in a twisting coil, its scales gleaming like wet ink beneath the candles. Students nearest the platform recoiled, robes and shoes scraping across the floor. Harry remained where he was, watching the creature raise its narrow head.

Its tongue tasted the air.

Too bright. Too loud. Surrounded.

The words reached Harry as clearly as if someone had spoken beside him.

He glanced towards the crowd.

Nobody answered.

Lockhart stepped forward, apparently delighted by the opportunity to recover his authority.

"A simple conjuration. Nothing to cause alarm."

The snake turned towards him.

Threat.

"It doesn't like you," Harry said.

Lockhart smiled indulgently. "Animals often require a confident hand."

He pointed his wand.

Snape moved to intervene, but Lockhart cast first.

His spell struck the platform beneath the snake and threw it high into the air. The creature twisted against the candlelight before dropping into the crowd with a heavy slap.

Screams broke across the Great Hall.

Students surged backwards, colliding with one another in their haste to escape. Justin Finch-Fletchley found himself trapped against the edge of the platform, his wand hanging uselessly at his side.

The snake rose before him.

Its body tightened into a striking coil.

Moving. Threat. Strike.

Justin's face went white.

Harry jumped down.

"Harry!" Hermione shouted.

Her warning vanished beneath the noise.

"Leave him alone," Harry said.

The words felt strange against his tongue. They emerged low and fluid, shaped more by breath than voice, yet Harry understood every sound as naturally as he understood his own thoughts.

The snake turned towards him.

He moved.

"He's frightened. Go back."

Its body loosened slightly.

Harry stepped closer, one hand extended without knowing what he intended to do with it.

"You're all right. Nobody is going to hurt you."

The snake lowered its head.

Relief moved through Harry—until he saw Justin.

The Hufflepuff remained pressed against the platform, staring at him with naked horror.

"What are you doing?" Harry asked.

Justin flinched.

Only then did Harry notice the silence.

Every student in the Great Hall watched him.

Hermione stood several feet away with one hand covering her mouth. Draconia had descended from the platform, her wand hanging forgotten at her side. All colour had left her face.

Snape swept between Harry and the snake. One sharp movement of his wand reduced the creature to a coil of black smoke, which unravelled against the floor and disappeared.

Nobody moved.

Harry searched the surrounding faces. "What?"

Justin pushed himself away from the platform.

"You were setting it on me."

"No, I wasn't. I told it to leave you alone."

"You were hissing at it!"

"I was talking."

"That wasn't talking."

A whisper rose from somewhere behind Justin.

"Parseltongue."

The word travelled through the crowd.

"Potter's a Parselmouth."

"He was speaking to it."

"Slytherin could talk to snakes."

"The Heir—"

The room altered around Harry.

Nobody made a deliberate decision to move, yet space opened between him and the nearest students. Those who had crowded forward to watch now drew back, pulling their friends with them. Curiosity hardened into suspicion across dozens of faces.

Lockhart raised both hands.

"Nothing to be concerned about! An unusual aptitude, certainly, but I had the situation entirely—"

"You launched the snake into the audience," Hermione said.

"A deliberate test."

"Of what?"

Lockhart opened his mouth.

No answer arrived.

Snape's voice cut across the rising noise. "This meeting is concluded."

Nobody needed telling twice.

Students moved towards the doors in a disorderly rush. Their whispers followed them into the entrance hall, repeated so often that the words ceased to sound like words and became something larger.

Parselmouth.

Heir.

Potter.

Harry remained beside the platform.

Hermione reached him first. She seized his arm and pulled him through a narrow side door. Draconia followed without being asked.

The door opened into a cramped antechamber used to store spare benches and damaged lecterns. Dust hung in the lamplight, disturbed by their entrance. Rolled banners filled one corner, while a broken hourglass leaked a narrow trail of blue sand across the floor.

Hermione closed the door and placed herself in front of it.

Harry looked between the girls.

"What just happened?"

"You spoke Parseltongue," Hermione said.

"I spoke to a snake."

"That is Parseltongue."

"I spoke English."

"No," Draconia said.

Her voice was barely audible.

Harry turned towards her.

She stood near the wall with both hands wrapped around her wand. Fear remained plainly visible in her face, but Harry could not tell whether it belonged to the snake, the watching students or him.

"You were hissing," she continued. "The snake answered you."

"I heard words."

"You understood it," Hermione said. "And it understood you."

Harry tried to fit the explanation around his memory. The snake's speech had not sounded foreign. Its meaning arrived without translation, as ordinary and immediate as Hermione's voice did now.

"I've spoken to one before."

Hermione blinked. "When?"

"At the zoo. Before Hogwarts. It told me it had never been to Brazil."

Neither girl answered.

"I thought it was magic," Harry added. "Normal magic."

"Speaking to snakes is not normal," Hermione said.

"Thank you. I gathered that from everyone fleeing."

"I didn't mean—"

"I know."

Harry turned over one of the benches and sat. Cold dust clung to his palms. Through the door came the fading thunder of hundreds of students filling the corridors.

He could already imagine the story changing with every staircase it climbed. By morning, he would have commanded a nest of snakes and probably grown fangs.

"Why did they look at me like that?"

Hermione and Draconia exchanged a glance.

Draconia answered.

"Salazar Slytherin was a Parselmouth."

Harry stared at her.

"It's very rare," she continued. "It can run in families. People might think you're related to him."

"And now everyone thinks I'm his heir."

"No one sensible," Hermione said.

"That eliminates fewer people than you'd hope."

She crossed the room and sat beside him, uncaring of the dust covering the bench.

"An ability does not prove ancestry, and ancestry would not prove guilt. You stopped the snake from attacking Justin."

"That isn't what Justin thinks."

"He was frightened."

"He looked at me like I was the monster."

Draconia moved nearer. "You are not."

The answer came too quickly for doubt to enter.

Harry looked up at her.

"You don't know why I can do it."

"No."

"You don't know what else it means."

"No."

"And you aren't frightened?"

Draconia's fingers tightened around her wand. "Of you?"

"Yes."

"No."

The answer was absolute.

She lowered her wand and sat upon the bench opposite him. Dust marked the hem of her robes, but she did not notice.

"I am frightened of what the school will decide," she said. "I am frightened that whoever opened the Chamber has just been given someone else to blame. Most of all, I am frightened that you will respond by wandering alone through the castle in an attempt to prove everyone wrong."

"That was only one of my ideas."

Hermione made an exasperated sound.

Draconia leaned forward. "But I am not frightened of you."

Harry held her gaze. Part of him wanted to believe her immediately. Another part remembered the notebook, the missed day and the confusion she continued hiding behind brittle excuses.

"The Sorting Hat thought Slytherin would suit me."

Hermione became very still.

Draconia's expression changed, though not with the fear Harry expected.

"It told you that?"

"It said I could do well there."

"But it placed you in Gryffindor."

"I didn't ask it to."

Hermione stared at him. "You never told us that."

"You knew I hesitated."

"I didn't know why."

Harry rubbed the dust from his palms. "What if it saw something?"

"Of course it saw something," Draconia said. "It is a magical hat whose sole purpose is looking inside children's heads."

"That isn't helping."

"It saw ambition. Resourcefulness. A willingness to ignore rules whenever they inconvenience you. None of those qualities makes you evil."

"You forgot recklessness," Hermione added.

"That belongs to every House in which Potter happens to be standing."

Despite everything, the corner of Harry's mouth moved.

Draconia's voice softened.

"The Hat may have believed you could belong in Slytherin. It still chose Gryffindor. People are more than one quality, Harry."

The words stirred a memory of Hagrid standing beside a display of cauldrons in Diagon Alley, telling him that people were usually more than one thing.

Harry looked down at his hands.

"Then where did this ability come from?"

Neither girl could answer.

For a while, the sounds beyond the door filled the antechamber. Hundreds of students carried Harry's secret into every corner of Hogwarts while he sat between the only two people who had not moved away from him.

Then Hermione looked towards Draconia.

"We still need to discuss the notebook."

Draconia's face closed.

Harry felt the fragile ease disappear.

"Not now."

"It is never now," Hermione said.

"The entire school has decided Harry controls an ancient monster, and you are concerned about stationery."

Harry stood immediately, placing himself between the two benches without consciously choosing to do so.

"We don't think you attacked Mrs Norris."

Hermione pushed away from the door.

The hurt did not leave Harry, but it shifted enough to make room for concern.

"Then tell us."

Draconia stared at him.

"I can't."

The admission emerged so quietly that Hermione appeared unsure whether she had heard correctly.

Harry stepped closer. "Why?"

Draconia's focus slipped.

Her gaze moved beyond Harry's shoulder and fixed upon something that did not exist in the room. A second passed. Then awareness returned with a small, startled movement, as if she had woken to find herself standing somewhere unexpected.

She backed away.

"I have to go."

"Draco—"

Draconia removed the silencing charm and opened the door.

The corridor remained crowded. Conversations faltered when students saw Harry. A group of Hufflepuffs moved instinctively aside, leaving a clear path through the centre of the passage.

Draconia stopped upon the threshold.

Harry expected her to leave.

Instead, she looked back at him.

"Come on."

He stared.

"If you stay in here, they'll think you're planning something."

"That seems unfair."

"People frequently are."

Hermione joined Harry's other side. "Where are we going?"

"Anywhere visible."

"That is not a destination."

"Away from here, for a start."

They entered the corridor together.

The reaction travelled ahead of them. Students stopped talking. Some watched Harry openly, while others studied Hermione and Draconia as if trying to understand why either girl willingly remained beside him.

Harry continued walking.

"You both realise this makes you accomplices."

Draconia stepped closer, the movement small but deliberate, closing a distance that had not been acknowledged until it no longer existed.

"They are wrong, Harry," she added, more quietly now. "About you, okay. And I'm not arguing that with you because I know you"

Harry met her gaze, searching it. "What if they are not?"

The question did not challenge so much as it tested, probing at the edges of something neither of them had fully named. For a fraction of a second, doubt flickered. It was there, undeniable, but as quick as it appeared, it vanished.

"They are, okay?" she said, her voice firm, certain in a way that seemed to hold itself together through will alone.

Her hand brushed against his. This time, there was nothing accidental in it. The contact was intentional, steady, and she did not withdraw. For a moment, neither did he. And in that brief, quiet space amid uncertainty, amid distance, amid the shifting perceptions of everyone around them, there remained something that had not yet been undone. It was fragile, unsteady. But real and for the moment it was enough.
