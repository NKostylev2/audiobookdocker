The first warning came in the shape of a house Harry had never seen. It stood on a hill beneath a sky the colour of bruised fruit, its chimneys crooked and its windows blind with grime. Rain had not yet begun, but the air carried the promise of it. He could feel the pressure of the coming storm against skin that did not seem to belong to him, while long grass whispered around his ankles.

Something moved inside it. Harry looked down and found a pale snake passing over the earth without bending a single stalk. It was too large to belong in an English field. Its scales held what little light remained, each briefly silver before it vanished beneath the hedge surrounding the house.

Harry followed. He did not want to. Some small, distant part of him understood he was asleep, and that dreams were meant to obey the person having them. This one did not. His feet carried him through an iron gate and up a drive strangled by nettles, towards a front door that waited open. Warm air breathed from the darkness beyond it.

Voices reached him from upstairs. One was quiet and educated, each word laid carefully beside the next, though he could not understand what it said. The sound travelled through walls, stretched by distance until meaning gave way to rhythm. The other voice barely sounded human.

He knew it.

Recognition passed through him without a name. His scar tightened, the house tilted, and suddenly he was no longer standing in the entrance hall. He looked through a narrow opening into a room lit by fire, where a tall man dressed in black stood with his back partly turned. Grey marked the hair above one ear. His left hand rested against a chair; on one finger, he wore a ring set with a heavy black stone.

Firelight moved over its surface. For one instant, Harry saw a shape carved there, something long and thin crossing what might have been a star. Then the hand closed. The chair beyond it shifted, and two red eyes opened in the dark.

Harry woke with his wand already raised.

Red light filled the room. He threw himself sideways, his shoulder striking the wall as the spell left his wand and a stack of schoolbooks exploded from the desk. Parchment lifted into the air. An ink bottle shattered against the wardrobe, spreading a dark stain across the door.

The spell struck nothing. There was no ruined house, no pale snake, no man wearing a black-stone ring. Only the smallest bedroom at number four, Privet Drive, held motionless beneath the grey light before dawn.

Harry remained pressed against the wall with his wand pointed at the empty space beside his bed. Pain travelled through his scar in narrow, deliberate pulses, each arriving a fraction behind his heartbeat. He counted five. By the sixth, he could breathe again.

Hedwig clicked her beak from the top of the wardrobe. Her feathers stood away from her body, making her look twice her usual size.

"Sorry," Harry whispered.

She stared at him.

"I agree. Very dramatic."

His voice shook badly enough to ruin the attempt. He lowered his wand, but his right hand had gone numb around the handle. Only after loosening one finger at a time could he set it across his knees. The digital clock beside his bed read 4:17.

Beyond the curtains, Privet Drive slept in orderly rows. A milk float hummed several streets away. Pipes shifted inside the walls, and someone in the neighbouring house coughed twice. Harry named each ordinary sound silently until the bedroom stopped feeling like another room disguised around him. Only then did he touch his scar. The skin was hot but unbroken.

He had learnt during the past year that pain could continue long after its cause disappeared. The Chamber had taught his body to wake at the sound of stone moving against stone; Dementors had taught it to mistake cold rooms for danger. For months after Hermione's Petrification and Draconia's possession, he had checked their faces whenever either girl became quiet, searching for the terrible absence he had once found in both.

Those memories belonged to him. This one did not. He had never seen the house on the hill or met the man near the fire. The eyes in the darkness belonged to someone he had faced twice and survived twice, if circumstances arranged by adults counted as victories. Harry had never been entirely convinced they should.

His hand moved towards the bedside drawer, then stopped halfway. The two-way mirror lay inside. Sirius had made the rules very simple: if anything happened, anything strange, dangerous or merely difficult to explain, Harry was to call. Time did not matter. Neither did whether he thought the problem important enough.

At half past four in the morning, however, the difference between a rule and an invitation seemed considerably larger. Harry imagined saying Sirius's name and watching him appear wild-haired and armed, probably before remembering to put on a shirt. He imagined the motorbike arriving in Surrey, blood wards and Ministry conditions forgotten, and being responsible for whatever followed.

His hand withdrew. He found his glasses instead.

The floor was cool beneath his feet. Harry crossed to the desk and rescued a sheet of parchment from beneath Standard Book of Spells, Grade Four. Its fall had broken the spine; Hermione would react as though he had injured something capable of feeling pain. He dipped a spare quill into the ink pooled across the wardrobe shelf and wrote before the dream could fray.

Old house on hill.

Storm coming.

Very large pale snake.

Two men—or one man and something else.

Black-stone ring. Mark looked like a line across a star?

Red eyes.

He paused. The second voice had said something as he woke, perhaps one word spoken with quiet satisfaction. He could almost hear it, but every attempt to recover the sound pulled it farther away. At last, he wrote a final line.

Could not understand what they wanted.

That was not entirely true. He simply could not remember enough to make the truth useful.

A floorboard creaked in the passage. Harry turned, wand rising again, though the bedroom door remained ajar. It had stayed that way every night since he returned from Hogwarts. Clause two of the conditions Sirius's solicitor had delivered to the Dursleys prohibited locks, bolts, bars or any other means of confinement upon his bedroom. Uncle Vernon had objected to the wording, the solicitor, the phrase criminal neglect and the fact that copies had been given to the Ministry, Professor Dumbledore and a Muggle social worker whose existence terrified him more than the other three combined.

He had not objected loudly enough to break the conditions.

A shadow crossed the gap.

"Who's there?" Harry asked.

The door moved inward. Aunt Petunia stood in the passage wearing a pale dressing gown, her face pinched and colourless without its daytime arrangement of powder and disapproval. One hand gripped the fabric at her throat. Her eyes travelled across the room, taking in the wand, the shattered ink bottle and the books upon the floor.

"What did you do?"

The question emerged as a whisper, making it less accusing than Harry expected.

"Redecorated."

Petunia looked at the black stain spreading down the wardrobe.

"At four in the morning?"

"Inspiration is inconsiderate."

Her mouth thinned. Usually, that meant the conversation had reached the point at which Harry was blamed for both his answer and the question requiring it. This time she did not raise her voice, only looked towards his forehead.

"Your scar."

Harry's humour left him.

"What about it?"

"You were holding it."

He had not realised she had seen.

"Headache."

Petunia's eyes moved to the wand again. "You don't fire those things because of headaches."

"You'd be surprised what they prescribe at St Mungo's."

She remained in the doorway while the house settled around them. Somewhere behind her, Uncle Vernon snored with a sudden choking interruption, then continued.

"Was it him?" she asked.

Harry said nothing. Petunia had spent thirteen years refusing to speak Voldemort's name despite claiming everything belonging to her sister's world was foolishness. He could not remember her ever referring to him so directly.

"I had a dream," he said at last.

"About him?"

"I don't know."

"You do."

The answer was so unlike her that Harry studied her more closely. She was frightened. Disgust and inconvenience were probably waiting nearby, but neither explained the fear that had pulled her from bed at the sound of his voice and carried her to a door she normally avoided.

"Yes," he said.

Petunia gripped her dressing gown more tightly.

"Is the house protected?"

There it was. Harry's attention fell to the parchment upon his desk.

"That's why I'm still here."

"That isn't what I asked."

"The wards are working. Professor Dumbledore checked them the day I arrived, and Bill Weasley checked them after him because Sirius wanted a second opinion. Then Lucius Malfoy sent someone who didn't speak for an hour and insulted the front garden when he finished. If Voldemort turns up, he'll have to survive three sets of magic and Uncle Vernon's hydrangeas."

Petunia flinched at the name.

"Don't say it."

"He doesn't appear when summoned."

"You don't know that."

"Actually, I've said it quite a lot."

Familiar irritation returned to her face, and Harry was relieved by it. He knew what to do with that expression. Petunia glanced behind her before lowering her voice.

"Your mother used to wake from dreams convinced something was wrong."

Harry forgot the joke he had been preparing.

"What was wrong?"

"Usually nothing. School things."

"What things?"

"I don't remember."

The speed of her answer proved she did. Harry took one step towards the door, and Petunia immediately retreated half a pace.

"Did anything hurt?"

"I said I don't remember."

She turned away.

"Aunt Petunia."

She stopped with her shoulders rigid. Harry could have demanded more. Sirius would have. Hermione would have constructed questions precise enough to corner a witness; Draconia would have waited in silence until Petunia filled it simply to escape being watched. Harry was tired, and whatever brief opening fear had created in his aunt was already closing.

"If it happens again," he said, "don't come into the room without knocking."

Petunia glanced back at the wand.

"I wasn't planning to."

She pulled the door until only a narrow gap remained, remembered the condition against shutting it without his permission, and let it swing open again. Her footsteps disappeared down the passage.

Harry returned to the bed and removed the mirror from the bedside drawer. Its surface reflected untidy hair, a sleep-creased cheek and eyes too wide behind his glasses. Sirius was not there. Of course he was not; Harry had not called him. He set it beside the clock, where he could reach it without getting up, then placed his wand beneath the pillow and lay facing the open door.

He waited for morning. Sleep did not return.

At seven, number four began pretending it contained an ordinary family. Water rushed through the upstairs pipes. Uncle Vernon opened and closed cupboards with the violence of a man personally betrayed by breakfast. Dudley descended the stairs heavily enough to announce each step, and the kitchen radio began delivering traffic reports.

Harry dressed and made his bed. He had not been required to make it. Nobody inspected the room now, and Sirius had been almost offended when he explained the old household schedule. Yet his hands performed the work before he considered leaving it undone: straightening the blanket, folding his nightclothes beneath the pillow, checking that nothing personal remained in sight. The habit had once prevented questions and confiscation. It continued after both became impossible.

He surveyed the room. His trunk sat at the foot of the bed, packed except for the robes he would not need until September and three books Hermione had asked him to read. Hedwig's cage stood open. His broom lay in its case, no longer locked beneath the stairs. Letters from Sirius, Hermione, Draconia and Remus occupied the desk in four untidy stacks, and the mirror rested openly beside the lamp. No one in the house could take any of it.

Harry checked anyway.

Inside the wardrobe, behind folded school jumpers, two packets of biscuits and an apple waited in a paper bag. Breakfast would be downstairs, lunch at one and dinner at six. The terms of residence specified adequate food in language so exact that Uncle Vernon had once weighed a baked potato to prove compliance. Harry still replaced the apple whenever it softened.

He closed the wardrobe and went downstairs.

Uncle Vernon sat at the head of the kitchen table behind the Daily Mail, only his hands and a widening section of forehead visible. Dudley occupied two chairs in practice if not technically, one knee angled into the space beside him. Aunt Petunia stood at the worktop cutting grapefruit.

A fourth place had been laid. The plate held eggs, toast and half a grilled tomato, with a glass of orange juice beside it. Harry knew without checking the amount was identical to Dudley's. Bill Weasley had been very clear about how magically witnessed agreements interpreted equality.

Harry sat. Uncle Vernon's newspaper lowered by an inch.

"There was a noise."

"Good morning to you too."

"A loud noise."

Harry picked up his fork. "That narrows it down. This house has Dudley."

Dudley scowled around a mouthful of toast.

"From your room," Vernon said.

"An ink bottle fell."

"Ink bottles do not produce flashes."

"This one had a theatrical temperament."

The newspaper lowered completely. Uncle Vernon's face had purpled along the cheeks, a colour that in previous summers had been enough to make Harry calculate the distance to the door. Now his body performed the calculation before his mind caught up: three steps to the passage, seven more to the front door, wand in his right pocket, mirror upstairs.

He hated that they could still do that to him. He hated more that they did not appear to notice.

"No magic," Vernon said.

Harry took a piece of toast. "The agreement says no deliberate magic in the presence of Muggles unless necessary for self-defence."

"You fired a spell."

"At a nightmare."

"Then it wasn't necessary."

"The nightmare disagreed."

Vernon folded the newspaper along its existing crease with excessive care. "You think these people hovering around the house give you permission to do whatever you like."

"No. The permission came from the Ministry. The hovering is Sirius."

Aunt Petunia set the grapefruit knife down sharply.

"He isn't here."

Harry's gaze shifted towards the window.

"Probably not."

All three Dursleys followed his gaze. The pavement outside was empty, but a tabby cat sat beneath the hedge across the road, washing one paw with great concentration. It paused long enough to look at the kitchen window, and Uncle Vernon pulled the net curtain closed.

Professor McGonagall had visited only once, on the first evening. The possibility of her returning had achieved what years of reason could not.

"Your godfather," Vernon said, investing the title with the suspicion usually reserved for contagious diseases, "will collect you tomorrow?"

"At sunrise."

"Not before?"

"Twenty-one consecutive nights. If I leave before the last one is complete, the protection doesn't renew."

Vernon glanced at Petunia.

"And once it does?"

"I leave."

"Permanently?" Dudley asked.

The room quieted. Harry studied his cousin, whose expression held neither happiness nor disappointment. For once, he appeared merely uncertain.

"I have to come back next summer," Harry said. "For the same thing."

"Twenty-one days?"

"And nights. Apparently evil is very particular about accommodation."

Dudley pushed his eggs around the plate. Uncle Vernon picked up the paper again.

"People like that man do not settle down."

Harry's fork paused.

"What?"

"Your criminal."

"Godfather," Aunt Petunia corrected quietly.

Vernon shot her a look. "The newspapers said what he did. Twelve years in prison. Escaped. Broke into a school."

"The newspapers also said he was innocent," Harry replied.

"They said he was cleared on a technicality."

"The technicality was that someone else committed the murders."

"Men like him do not become respectable because a judge signs a piece of paper." Vernon's eyes remained upon the print. "He'll tire of playing at family. Then what?"

Harry put down his fork. The answer he wanted was hot enough to hurt on the way out. He could tell Vernon that Sirius had spent twelve years in Azkaban without surrendering the knowledge Harry existed. He could list every adult who had stood in this kitchen and regarded the Dursleys with poorly concealed contempt, or explain that Sirius had already fought harder to keep him than Vernon had ever fought to make him leave.

Instead, Harry smiled.

"He's put up curtains," he said. "We're all very concerned."

Dudley made a sound suspiciously like a laugh and disguised it as a cough. Uncle Vernon raised the paper high enough to end the conversation, and Harry resumed eating. The eggs had gone cold. He finished them anyway.

Petunia collected the plates as soon as Vernon settled behind his newspaper. Harry reached automatically for his own, but she took it before his fingers touched the edge.

"Leave it."

The words were neither kind nor cruel. They dismissed him from the kitchen with the same efficiency she used to clear crumbs from the worktop. Harry went upstairs.

The smallest bedroom received the morning reluctantly. Grey light reached through the curtains and stopped halfway across the carpet, dust floating above the desk. The stain upon the wardrobe had begun drying at its edges, preserving the shape of the spell he had fired at something hundreds of miles away.

For twenty days, Harry had lived according to the clock. Breakfast at seven, lunch at one, dinner at six. A mirror call after eight unless Sirius found a reason to call earlier. Letters whenever Hedwig returned. Sleep when his body stopped arguing with the dark.

The hours between possessed less structure. He read without remembering pages, walked to Mrs Figg's under the conditions of the agreement and returned before Vernon could accuse him of enjoying himself. Some afternoons he lay upon the bed listening to ordinary lives continue beyond the glass: lawnmowers, bicycle bells, children shouting across gardens. Privet Drive had once taught him to become quiet enough that unhappiness required no response. The skill returned more quickly than he liked.

Nothing terrible had happened this summer. That was almost the problem. There were no bars across the window, no padlock upon the door and no meals pushed through a flap. The Dursleys obeyed every condition while making it clear that compliance was not welcome. Harry possessed food, letters and a route out. He was safer than he had ever been inside number four, yet by the end of the first week he had begun sleeping too long in the afternoons and staring at open books until the print lost meaning.

He had not told Sirius that part. Depression sounded too dramatic for a boy with a departure date circled upon the wall. Harry called it tiredness instead and waited for somebody to believe him.

He sat upon the bed and reached for the mirror. Sirius answered on the third call, his face appearing at an angle, one grey eye filling most of the glass before he drew back. Black hair stuck up on one side of his head, and a dark streak crossed his cheek.

"Harry?"

The concern in his voice arrived before focus did. Harry sat against the headboard with the mirror propped upon his knees.

"You look terrible."

Sirius blinked.

"Good morning to you too."

"Have you been sleeping in a fireplace?"

Sirius wiped his cheek and examined the soot upon his fingers. "The cottage chimney and I have reached a disagreement."

"Who won?"

"The chimney has no wand and will soon discover the limits of my patience."

"So the chimney."

Some of the tension left Sirius's face. Behind him, Harry could see a low ceiling crossed by wooden beams and a wall the colour of warm cream. A ladder stood open near what appeared to be the kitchen, one leg resting upon a book.

"Is that one of Hermione's books?" Harry asked.

Sirius glanced behind him. "It may once have belonged to her."

"She'll kill you."

"Then don't tell her."

"She notices when books are frightened."

Sirius looked back at the mirror. His smile remained, but the sleep had vanished from his eyes.

"You called three times."

"You were threatening a chimney."

"You usually stop after one if it isn't urgent."

Harry considered lying. Sirius waited, and that was one of the more difficult changes. Adults at Privet Drive had filled silence with accusations until he learnt to answer quickly enough to direct them elsewhere. Sirius left space. He did not pretend not to notice when Harry used a joke as a locked door, but neither did he force it open.

Harry picked at a loose thread upon the blanket.

"I had a dream."

Sirius's expression altered very slightly. "What kind?"

Harry reached for the parchment and read the list aloud. He did not mention Aunt Petunia. Perhaps her one sentence about Lily felt too small and private to survive Sirius's anger at everything surrounding it; perhaps he simply wanted to keep it for a little longer before other people began deciding what it meant.

When he finished, Sirius asked, "Your scar hurt?"

"A bit."

"Harry."

"A lot for a minute. It's stopped."

Sirius placed the mirror upon a table. The view shifted to show more of the room as he sat opposite it, sleeves rolled to his elbows, a smear of white paint marking one forearm.

"Did you lose consciousness?"

"I was asleep."

"Did you feel ill afterwards?"

"No."

"Were you inside your own body in the dream?"

Harry thought of feet carrying him up the drive and vision passing through walls.

"I don't think I had one."

Sirius's jaw tightened.

"That's a comforting sentence before breakfast."

"I try."

"I'm contacting Dumbledore."

Harry had expected the answer and resented it anyway.

"It was one dream."

"And pain in a curse scar connected to Voldemort."

"I've had worse dreams."

"Those belong to you."

Harry went still. Sirius held his gaze through the mirror.

"You knew the difference," he said. "That's why you wrote this one down."

It was irritating when adults became observant only after Harry wanted them to stop.

"Dumbledore will tell me not to worry."

"Then he can tell me himself." Sirius reached for something beyond the edge of the glass. "I'm also sending Remus the description of the ring."

"Why Remus?"

"Because Remus knows more about obscure Dark objects than is healthy, and because he has friends who know even more."

"You have those too."

"Mine tend to be wanted for questioning."

Parchment rustled. Harry leaned closer.

"You don't have to tell everyone."

Sirius's quill stopped.

"I know."

The simple answer made Harry feel unreasonable.

"I only mean it might be nothing."

"It may be."

"And if everybody starts watching me—"

"I know." Sirius set down the quill. "No one is putting you under guard because of a nightmare. You're still coming home tomorrow. I'm telling Dumbledore and Remus because they might recognise what you saw."

He paused.

"You should not have to decide alone whether pain connected to the man who killed your parents matters."

Harry stared at the crack running through the bedside paint.

"I didn't say it didn't matter."

"No. You said it had stopped. Those are not the same thing."

Outside, a car door closed. A lawnmower started somewhere down the street, faltered and started again. The small interruption gave Harry something to listen to before Sirius's voice softened.

"Look at me."

Harry did.

"Are you frightened?"

"No."

"All right."

Harry frowned. "You don't believe me."

"Not remotely."

"Then why ask?"

"Because one day you might answer."

Sirius said it without accusation, which made the words harder to dismiss. Harry turned the mirror slightly, watching sunlight move across its frame.

"I knew his voice," he admitted.

"In the dream?"

"Not exactly. I knew how it felt before I knew who it was."

Sirius said nothing.

"That's worse, isn't it?" Harry asked.

"It is something we take seriously."

"That's adult for yes."

"Yes."

Harry let his head rest against the wall. For a few breaths, neither spoke. Sirius picked up a mug, steam crossing the mirror and briefly obscuring his face.

"Tea?" Harry asked.

"Coffee. Remus thinks I'm calmer if I drink tea."

"Does it?"

"We may never know."

Harry smiled despite himself.

"What are you doing to the cottage?"

Sirius turned the mirror, revealing a kitchen in a state of organised destruction. Cabinet doors stood open, a faded blue cloth covered the table, and two chairs matched while three others appeared to have arrived from unrelated houses. Morning light entered through the window above the sink, illuminating dust in the air.

"Improving it."

"It looks injured."

"That is an intermediate stage of improvement."

"Hermione says that about essays."

"Your room is untouched," Sirius said quickly. "Mostly."

Harry looked back at him. "Mostly?"

"The shelves are up."

"Straight?"

"They're meant to look like that."

"Sirius."

"Remus is coming this afternoon."

"To straighten the shelves?"

"To point out where I'd gone wrong."

Sirius's smile widened. For a second, he looked younger than Harry had ever seen him, neither the hollow-faced prisoner from the wanted posters nor the careful man who sat through guardianship hearings with both hands clenched beneath the table. Someone who might once have thought summer meant nothing more serious than open windows and friends arriving late.

Harry tried to imagine him at fourteen. The picture refused to hold. Sirius had been raised in a house he still described as though it might hear him, and Harry understood places that remained present after they were left.

"Tomorrow morning," Sirius said.

Harry glanced at the calendar above the desk. Every date from his return on 10 July bore a red line. Tomorrow, 31 July, was circled rather than crossed.

"I know."

"At first light."

"You said."

"I will be there."

The certainty in his voice made something tighten beneath Harry's ribs.

"You don't have to arrive early. The wards won't renew until sunrise."

"I know."

"Standing outside won't make it faster."

"I know."

Harry studied his face. "You're going to be early."

"Immensely."

"The neighbours will call the police."

"Then I shall finally meet the only branch of law enforcement in Britain that has never arrested me."

Harry laughed. Sirius watched him with an expression that shifted too quickly to name.

"One more night," he said.

Harry's fingers closed around the mirror.

"One more," he agreed.

Hermione and Draconia arrived shortly before noon, accompanied by two adults, two umbrellas and enough protective magic to survive a minor siege. Harry saw them from his bedroom window. Mr Granger's car pulled to the kerb first, and Hermione climbed out before the engine had stopped completely, a canvas bag over one shoulder and her hair already escaping the plait intended to contain it. Her father followed more carefully, carrying a covered dish Mrs Granger had apparently decided no magical protection could replace.

A long black car drew up behind them. Narcissa Malfoy emerged from the rear door in cream summer robes disguised beneath a Muggle coat. The combination should have looked absurd. On her, it suggested the entire street had dressed incorrectly and would shortly be informed.

Draconia stepped out after her. She wore dark trousers, a white blouse with the sleeves folded neatly to her elbows, and the guarded expression she had adopted since the Chamber whenever she entered a place she did not control. Her eyes moved first to the house, then the upstairs windows, parked cars and narrow side passage. Only after counting the exits did she look up.

Harry raised one hand. Her expression changed, relief lasting less than a second before she concealed it beneath impatience and pointed meaningfully towards the front door. He went downstairs.

Uncle Vernon had already opened the door and stood behind it as though the thickness of painted wood might protect him from the visitors. His gaze passed over Mr Granger with reluctant recognition and stopped at Narcissa. He had met her twice and remained unable to decide whether her beauty made the magic more acceptable or considerably more threatening.

"Mrs Malfoy," he said.

"Mr Dursley."

Narcissa looked past him into the hall. Harry appeared at the foot of the stairs, and her attention moved over him with the efficiency of a diagnostic charm: face, shoulders, uncovered forearms, clothing, posture. Finding no visible injury allowed some of the coldness to leave her eyes.

Hermione did not bother with inspection from a distance. She crossed the threshold and hugged him, making Harry catch himself against the banister. He had become better at expecting this from her and no better at knowing what to do during the first instant. By the time he returned the embrace, she was drawing back to study him.

"You look tired."

"Hello to you too."

"Did you sleep?"

"Some."

"How much?"

Draconia entered behind her. "He only gets vague when the answer's bad."

"I liked you better outside," Harry said.

"No, you didn't."

She stopped before him. For one uncertain moment, he thought she might hug him as well. Draconia seemed to consider it, glanced towards the Dursleys and changed her mind. Her fingers closed briefly around his wrist instead, resting over the pulse as though confirming something private.

"You look—" she began.

Harry waited. Her gaze moved from his face to his hair, which he had attempted to flatten before abandoning the argument.

"Alive," she finished.

"That sounded almost complimentary."

"Then you misheard it."

The faint colour near her cheekbones suggested otherwise.

"You are supposed to be resting," she said.

"I've been imprisoned in Surrey for twenty days."

Uncle Vernon cleared his throat. Harry glanced back.

"Residing. Apparently."

"We agreed the children might use the back garden," Narcissa said.

Vernon's expression suggested the word children had been stretched past credibility.

"No funny business."

Draconia looked at him. "Could you define the ordinary variety? I would hate to cross a boundary through cultural confusion."

Mr Granger coughed into his fist.

"No magic," Vernon said.

"We understand," Hermione answered before Draconia could improve matters.

The adults remained in the kitchen under the terms of the visit. Hermione had explained in her last letter that her father refused to let Narcissa act as the sole witness to any confrontation with the Dursleys. Why Narcissa had accepted, Harry could only guess. The Malfoys were still learning to collaborate with Muggles without either side treating the process as an experiment.

He led the girls through the sitting room and out the rear door. The day had begun hot, but clouds moved quickly above the rooftops. Sheets hung motionless in neighbouring gardens. Bees worried at the lavender beside Aunt Petunia's path, and the grass carried pale stripes from Uncle Vernon's mower.

A table stood beneath the pear tree at the back fence. Harry had arranged three chairs around it. Draconia's faced the house and garden gate, leaving nothing behind her but the fence. Hermione's occupied the side least affected by glare, where she could read without squinting. A pot of tea waited between three cups, with milk for Hermione and a dish of thinly sliced lemon for Draconia.

Both girls stopped. Harry looked between them.

"What?"

"You did this?" Hermione asked.

"No, the garden expressed hospitality."

Draconia touched the back of her chair, her gaze moving from its position to his face. He pretended not to notice. During their first year, she had been content to sit anywhere from which she could observe the most people. Since the diary, closed doors and unseen movement made her shoulders tighten. She never complained. Harry had begun choosing seats before she needed to.

Hermione noticed every time. She said nothing now, only sat and passed him a parcel wrapped in brown paper.

"This is not your birthday present."

"That sounds promising."

"Your present is for tomorrow."

"Tomorrow is his birthday," Draconia said, taking her seat. "The fact that Black has declared an alternative celebration date does not amend the calendar."

"Sirius didn't declare it. The wards did."

"Wards do not organise cake."

Harry unwrapped the parcel. Inside lay a new bottle of ink, two quills and a book Hermione had mentioned in three separate letters.

"This also looks like a present."

"It's replacement equipment," she said. "You said you were low on ink."

"I was. Then I put the rest on the wardrobe."

Hermione narrowed her eyes. "What happened?"

Harry had intended to tell them. He had made the tea, arranged the chairs and placed his dream notes beneath the pot so they would not blow away. He had rehearsed a version that sounded factual rather than frightened. Sirius already knew; there was no reason to hide it. Yet the question pulled every old instinct tight inside him.

Nothing. Accident. I'm fine.

Draconia watched his face.

"You had another nightmare," she said.

Hermione looked at her. "Another?"

Harry sat.

"It wasn't like the others."

He drew the parchment from beneath the teapot and placed it between them. Hermione reached it first, her eyes travelling rapidly down the list, returning to the beginning and slowing. Draconia read over her shoulder without touching the page. Neither interrupted.

Harry described the house, the snake and the voices. The words sounded thinner in daylight. A dream reduced to a list could be mistaken for nonsense; spoken aloud beneath an ordinary pear tree, it became almost embarrassing. He continued anyway. When he reached the red eyes, Hermione's hand stopped halfway towards her tea.

"Voldemort?"

"I didn't see a face."

Draconia had gone very still.

"But you think it was him," she said.

"I think my scar did."

Hermione looked at his forehead as if the lightning-shaped mark might offer clarification.

"How badly did it hurt?"

"Sirius already asked."

"That is not an answer."

"You spend too much time together."

"Harry."

He sighed. "Enough to wake me. It stopped after a minute."

"And you fired a spell," Draconia said.

Harry glanced towards the house. "How do you know?"

"Your wand hand has ink along the side, which suggests you knocked the bottle over after drawing it. You only draw during nightmares when you wake holding it."

"That is disturbingly specific."

"You are disturbingly predictable."

Hermione reached across the table and turned his hand palm upward. A faint red mark crossed the base of his thumb where he had gripped the wand. Her fingers were warm.

"Did you hurt yourself?"

"Only the books."

"Which books?"

"Focus, Hermione."

"I can be concerned about two things."

"You're very talented."

"Thank you."

"I wasn't complimenting you."

"You should've made that clearer."

Draconia looked between them. "I am beginning to understand why other people find us exhausting."

"Other people lack stamina," Harry said.

Hermione's thumb passed once across the red mark before she seemed to notice what she was doing. She released his hand and reached for the notes, but Draconia drew the parchment towards herself.

"Show me the ring."

Harry took Hermione's new quill and turned the paper over. His first sketch resembled a black potato. The second acquired a band and something that might be an engraved line.

"I only saw it for a second."

Draconia leaned closer. Harry added a rough star behind the line, then crossed it out.

"Maybe it wasn't a star. It could have been a snake. Or a crack in the stone."

"Was the setting silver?"

"I don't know."

"Which hand?"

"Left."

"First finger?"

Harry tried to recreate the image.

"Middle, I think."

Draconia sat back. The movement was controlled, as was the way she folded her hands in her lap. Harry knew what that control meant.

"You recognise it."

"Perhaps."

"Draco."

"Several old families favour black stones."

Hermione leaned closer. "Which family favours that one?"

Draconia looked towards the kitchen windows. Narcissa stood inside speaking to Mr Granger, while Aunt Petunia occupied the far end of the room with her arms locked across her chest. No one appeared to be listening, but Draconia lowered her voice.

"Theodore Nott's father wears a ring like that."

Wind moved through the pear leaves. Harry studied the crude drawing.

"You're sure?"

"No."

The answer was immediate.

"I have seen it at formal dinners and Wizengamot receptions. The stone is old—older than the current setting. It bears the Nott device, a serpent laid across an eight-pointed star. Many signet rings are similar, and you were dreaming."

"But you thought of him."

"Yes."

Hermione's brow furrowed. "Was he one of the people Pettigrew named?"

"Not publicly." Draconia's fingers tightened. "Father suspected him before Pettigrew was arrested. Nott disappeared within days of the first searches, well before Pettigrew escaped Ministry transfer. The Ministry found his estate empty and several rooms burnt."

Harry thought of the man in black standing beside the fire.

"Did your father know him?"

Draconia's expression became remote in the manner Harry had come to hate.

"They attended school together."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Her gaze returned to him.

"Yes," she said. "He knew him."

No one mentioned the Mark Lucius still carried beneath his sleeve. No one needed to. Hermione reached for the parchment again.

"Sirius has sent this to Dumbledore?"

"And Remus."

"We should give them Nott's name immediately."

Harry looked at Draconia. She nodded once, and Hermione opened her canvas bag, removing a notebook, two rolls of parchment, a quill case, sealing wax, three reference books and a small square mirror.

Harry stared.

"Do you carry an entire desk everywhere?"

"Only when visiting you."

"That seems rude."

"It is experience," Draconia said.

The small mirror was not paired to Sirius's. Hermione had spent part of June working with Professor McGonagall on a network of emergency objects that could alert designated adults without allowing every conversation to be overheard. She tapped its frame three times with her wand and dictated a concise message for Dumbledore, Sirius and Remus.

"Do not say I'm certain," Draconia interrupted.

Hermione crossed out a line. "I wrote possible identification."

"State the basis."

"I have."

"State that he disappeared before Ministry questioning."

"I was getting to that."

Harry poured the tea while they revised a four-sentence message into something resembling evidence for a trial. When Hermione finally sent it, the writing vanished from the mirror in a curl of blue smoke.

"There," she said.

"Right," Harry replied. "Can we put the nightmare away now? I'd like to talk about something cheerful."

Hermione looked at him over the rim of her cup.

"Such as?"

"Whether Sirius is actually capable of cooking."

"No," Draconia said.

"You answered very quickly."

"Your godfather invited my parents to dinner last week."

Harry turned to her.

"Why didn't anyone tell me?"

"Because you would have worried."

"About dinner?"

"About the possibility that Father and Black might reach the second course without committing a felony."

"Did they?"

"Technically."

Hermione hid a smile behind her tea.

"Mother brought the food," Draconia continued. "Professor Lupin supervised the conversation. Your godfather attempted to serve potatoes and discovered he had failed to turn on the oven."

"That explains the chimney. He's trying to learn."

"You're giving him a lot of credit."

Harry smiled, though his attention remained upon the parchment between them. Nott. The name belonged to a thin boy in Slytherin who spoke rarely and watched Draconia as though she had violated a rule he intended to enforce one day. Theodore had left Hogwarts before the previous term ended, with no explanation circulating beyond family illness.

"Does Theo know where his father is?" Harry asked.

Draconia's cup touched its saucer without a sound.

"I don't know."

"Will he return to school?" Hermione asked.

"Most likely. The Ministry did not charge him with anything."

"He was thirteen," Harry said. "What would they charge him with?"

Draconia looked towards the fence.

"Being his father's son has been enough for people to decide worse."

Harry heard the part of the sentence she did not voice.

"I didn't mean—"

"I know."

He watched her lift the tea. Her hand trembled once against the handle and steadied. Without thinking, he moved the dish of lemon within reach. Draconia's eyes dropped to it, something softening in her face. Hermione saw that too. She looked down at her own cup, though the small smile near its edge remained visible.

The moment lasted only a breath. Then Draconia added far too much lemon.

"If Theodore returns, we do not accuse him of involvement because his father owns jewellery."

"Agreed," Hermione said.

Harry nodded.

"We ask him politely whether his missing father spends time in ruined houses with giant snakes."

"That is an accusation shaped like a question."

"I've learnt from experts."

They stayed until evening. For the first hour after the message, every subject curved back towards the dream. Hermione wanted to know whether the house contained portraits, what kind of trees surrounded it and whether Harry had smelled salt water. Draconia asked about the man's accent, clothing and posture. He answered until memory and imagination became impossible to separate, the little he had seen wearing thin beneath their questions.

Then Mr Granger brought the covered dish into the garden and announced his wife had made enough sandwiches to feed a dental conference. The investigation gave way to lunch, and that change saved the day.

Hermione complained about her parents' insistence that she accompany them on a walking holiday during the second week of August. Draconia described the etiquette tutor Narcissa had dismissed after the woman suggested friendship with Muggle-borns was an eccentricity best outgrown. Harry told them about Sirius's shelves and the wounded chimney. Between bites, they argued over which bedroom at the cottage should contain the largest desk. Hermione believed it ought to be whichever received the best morning light; Draconia maintained that furniture allocation should not reward people incapable of reading in imperfect conditions.

"There are three bedrooms," Harry reminded them.

"Four," Hermione said. "Sirius finished the small one under the eaves."

Harry stared at her.

"How do you know?"

She became very interested in a sandwich. Draconia examined her fingernails.

"What are you planning?" Harry asked.

"Nothing," Hermione said.

"That was terrible."

"I was not prepared to lie."

"You had twenty days."

Draconia leaned back in her chair. "Perhaps the room is for Professor Lupin."

"Remus already has a room."

"Then perhaps Black intends to collect vulnerable children until the cottage becomes structurally unsound."

Harry studied them both. Hermione bit the inside of her cheek; Draconia's expression possessed the careful vacancy she used when concealing something she considered benevolent.

"My birthday present is in that room," he said.

Neither answered.

"You know," Harry continued, "Slytherin has built an entire reputation around deception. You should be embarrassed."

"I have never claimed Granger was a Slytherin."

"You were worse."

"I chose silence."

"Your silence is smug."

"That sounds like a personal difficulty."

Harry threw a crust of bread at her. Draconia caught it before it struck her blouse. Hermione gasped his name with considerably more outrage than the situation required, then failed to avoid the second crust. Within a minute, the table had become indefensible.

They were fourteen, or very nearly in Harry's case, and for once no hidden creature waited below the floor. No traitor watched from inside another shape. The greatest immediate danger was Narcissa opening the kitchen door at the precise moment a slice of cucumber slid from Hermione's shoulder.

All three froze. Bread lay in the grass, Harry held half a sandwich behind his back, and Draconia sat with perfect posture and a smear of butter near her wrist.

"We were discussing defensive strategy," she said.

"I see." Narcissa's gaze rested on her daughter. "You appear to have lost."

Harry expected a denial. Instead, Draconia laughed. The sound escaped before she could prevent it, bright and surprised, so unlike the controlled amusement she allowed in public that Narcissa's expression briefly opened with wonder. Hermione began laughing too, and Harry followed without entirely knowing why.

Narcissa shook her head and returned inside. The laughter faded by degrees, leaving them breathless around the ruined lunch. Loose strands of Hermione's hair had escaped around her face; grass clung to Draconia's sleeve. Harry looked at both and tried to keep the moment from changing.

For most of his life, happiness had made him wary. It arrived too rarely to trust and ended too suddenly to examine. Hogwarts had changed that, but the past three years had also taught him how quickly a feast became a warning, a match became an attack, or a friend became still beneath white hospital sheets. Sometimes he found himself counting good moments while they happened, already preserving them against loss.

Hermione met his eyes. Her smile faded first.

"Where did you go?" she asked.

"Nowhere."

Draconia brushed grass from her sleeve. "You do that even when you're sitting right beside us."

Harry's attention dropped to the table. He could give them the easy answer: the Dursleys, the dream, one more night. All of it was true. None of it was the whole thing.

"I was thinking this is nice," he said.

Hermione waited.

"And?"

"There doesn't have to be an and."

"There is always an and when you say something is nice as though apologising for it."

Draconia reached across the table and took the sandwich from behind his back.

"He thinks noticing happiness will frighten it away," she said.

Harry stared at her. She took a bite.

"You don't know that."

"No," Draconia agreed. "I know what your face does when either of us leaves a room."

The garden seemed suddenly too exposed. Hermione's expression softened.

"Harry—"

"I'm not doing anything."

"We know."

"You make it sound like I follow you about checking you're alive."

Neither girl spoke. Harry pushed his glasses higher.

"Not all the time."

"We check too," Hermione said.

He looked at her. She folded the paper napkin beside her plate into smaller and smaller squares.

"When you go quiet," she continued, "I count how long it takes you to answer. Draconia watches whether you finish meals. We both notice when you choose the chair nearest the door."

Draconia put down the stolen sandwich.

"Granger."

"He should know."

"Know what?" Harry asked.

Hermione held his gaze.

"That it isn't only you."

Wind stirred the abandoned napkins. The words loosened something inside Harry and hurt in the same place. He thought of the final night in the Shrieking Shack, of Hermione and Draconia entering a memory he had spent years pretending had left no mark. He remembered what he had seen when the spell turned upon them in return: Hermione small and alone among voices calling her impossible, Draconia on the Chamber floor while another mind moved behind her eyes.

They had not discussed every image. Some remained too sharp to hold directly, but that did not mean they had forgotten. Harry reached for the teapot, found it empty and set it down again.

"All right," he said.

"All right what?" Draconia asked.

"I'll try not to disappear while sitting in front of you."

"That is not quite—"

Hermione placed a hand upon Draconia's arm.

"It's a start," she said.

Harry's gaze followed the hand to Hermione. She removed it, suddenly self-conscious, while Draconia adjusted the cuff of her sleeve. The movement brought her shoulder against his where their chairs had shifted closer during lunch. Neither moved away immediately.

Harry noticed. He wished he had not, because noticing made the space between them feel different, warmer and far more complicated than a garden chair deserved. Then Hermione saw the butter upon Draconia's wrist and wiped it away with the napkin she had folded beyond usefulness.

"Honestly," she muttered.

The strange tension dissolved. Harry decided not to examine the relief too closely.

The reply to Hermione's message arrived while they cleared the remains of lunch. Her square mirror chimed from inside the canvas bag, and all three reached for it at once. Hermione was nearest, Draconia fastest, and Harry had the advantage of not caring whether either considered elbowing dignified. He emerged with the mirror.

Blue letters travelled across its surface.

Description received. Possible identification is being examined. Do not approach or question Theodore Nott. Professor Dumbledore has been informed. Harry is not to remain alone tonight.

—S. Black

Harry read the final sentence twice.

"Absolutely not."

Hermione took the mirror from him. "You cannot object to instructions by pretending they weren't addressed to you."

"Watch me."

Draconia read over her shoulder. "Black is his guardian. You are required to obey him."

"You don't believe that in any other context."

"I believe it whenever he agrees with me."

Harry glanced towards the kitchen.

"None of you can stay here overnight."

"That is why mirrors exist," Hermione said.

"I am not sleeping while Sirius watches me."

"I doubt he planned to watch you sleep."

"He once watched me eat soup for forty minutes."

Draconia took the mirror.

"You had a fever."

"I also had hands."

"Which were shaking badly enough to redecorate Professor Lupin's floor."

Harry held out his hand.

"Give that back."

"It isn't yours."

"Then stop using it to organise my life."

Draconia passed it to Hermione.

"Tell Black he'll need someone to take over when he's asleep."

"Shifts?" Harry repeated.

"Someone should be available if your scar hurts again."

"I know how to say a name into a mirror."

Hermione placed it inside her bag. "Then why did it take you three hours to call him this morning?"

Harry opened his mouth, but nothing useful arrived. Draconia's satisfaction was unbearable.

"Disturbingly predictable."

"I'm beginning to regret arranging your chair."

Her smile faded. Harry regretted the joke before the last word left him. Draconia's fingers passed over the wood at her side.

"No, you aren't."

"No," he admitted. "I'm not."

The kitchen door opened before any of them could decide whether more needed saying. Mr Granger stepped into the garden with Narcissa behind him. Afternoon light had turned thin beneath the thickening clouds, and a warm wind moved the hem of her coat.

"We should leave before the rain begins," Mr Granger said. "Your mother has rung twice, and both times she's pretended it was to ask whether the dish came back."

Hermione glanced upwards.

"It isn't going to rain."

Thunder sounded at a distance. Draconia rose.

"A powerful demonstration."

They carried the cups inside. Farewells at number four had become elaborate because no one trusted them to remain temporary. Mr Granger shook Harry's hand, then abandoned formality long enough to pull him into a brief, awkward embrace. Narcissa touched two fingers beneath his chin, raising his face towards the window light.

"If the pain returns, you call an adult immediately."

"You're not the first to tell me."

"Then perhaps the problem is not a lack of clarity."

Her gaze held his until he nodded.

"Your room is ready at the Manor whenever you wish to visit," she said, releasing him.

Behind her, Uncle Vernon's face tightened at the suggestion that Harry possessed multiple destinations.

"Thank you," Harry said.

Hermione hugged him again upon the front step, this one lasting longer than the first.

"Tomorrow," she said near his ear.

"Apparently."

"That is less than a day."

"I can count."

"I know. I'm reminding the part of you that thinks bad hours last longer than ordinary ones."

She drew back before he could answer. Draconia waited beside the path, and Harry went to her.

"Happy birthday for tomorrow," she said.

"You're coming to the cottage the day after."

"I am aware."

"Then you can say it then."

"It will be inaccurate then."

Draconia glanced towards the house and lowered her voice.

"If anything feels wrong tonight, do not investigate it."

"That is very broad."

"It is intended to be."

"What if the wardrobe attacks?" Harry asked. "It has cause."

"Then attempt diplomacy until an adult arrives."

He smiled. She did not. The seriousness in her face made him reach for her before he decided what he was doing, his hand closing around hers. Draconia looked down. Harry nearly let go, but her fingers turned within his and held on.

"I'll call," he said.

"Before you decide whether it is worth calling about."

"That seems impossible."

"Make an effort."

Her thumb moved once across his knuckles. Hermione stood near the Grangers' car, watching without appearing to. Something unreadable passed over her face, not quite hurt or surprise. Harry became conscious of every person upon the street and released Draconia's hand.

"Tomorrow," she repeated.

The cars departed beneath the first scattered drops of rain. Harry remained at the end of the path until both had turned out of Privet Drive. When he went inside, Uncle Vernon was waiting in the hall.

"This is not a meeting place."

Harry stepped around him. "You should tell the visitors. They seem confused."

"I will not have people like that arriving in black cars and frightening the neighbours."

"Mr Granger's car is blue."

"You know who I mean."

Harry stopped upon the first stair.

"Yes," he said. "I usually do."

Uncle Vernon's eyes flicked towards the sitting-room window, where Narcissa's car had been visible to half the street.

"The Malfoys," he said. "That woman behaves as if she owns the place."

"She doesn't want it."

"And that girl—"

Harry turned. Vernon stopped. The wand remained inside Harry's pocket; he did not touch it. He only looked at his uncle until whatever description had been about to follow changed direction.

"All of them," Vernon finished. "Gone tomorrow."

"No," Harry said. "I'm gone tomorrow. They can go wherever they like."

He continued upstairs. The distinction mattered more than Vernon would ever understand.

The bedroom had kept the heat of the afternoon. Harry opened the window and stood beside it while rain darkened the road, carrying the last traces of tyre marks towards the drains. For several minutes he could still hear the garden as it had been: Hermione's outrage when the bread struck her, Draconia's unguarded laugh, three chairs scraping closer around one table. The memory occupied the room so clearly that he turned once, expecting to find them behind him.

There was only the empty bed.

Harry hated how quickly absence changed the scale of things. The room had felt cramped that morning. Now it appeared too large for one person and too small to contain the hours remaining before sunrise. He attempted Hermione's book, reached the bottom of the same page four times without learning what it said, and opened Draconia's most recent letter instead.

He already knew every line. She had crossed out I hope you are sleeping and replaced it with Try not to become nocturnal. Hermione's last letter lay beneath it, a postscript reminding him tea did not constitute a meal, followed by a second conceding that this depended upon what accompanied the tea.

Neither girl was gone. Hermione would be home before dinner. Draconia would probably spend the evening interrogating her father about the Nott ring. Sirius sat at Hawthorn Cottage beneath shelves incapable of forming a straight line, and Harry could speak to any of them by mirror. Knowing that helped. It did not make the room less quiet.

The distinction frightened him because it resembled the truth of the past three years. Loving people had not cured the part of him that expected every closed door to become permanent. It had only given the fear more names to use.

He crossed another square upon the calendar. One remained.

Harry turned fourteen beneath a ceiling stained with the shadow of his own misfired curse. At midnight, Hedwig descended from the wardrobe and landed upon the bed. A small parcel was tied to one leg, three envelopes bound with green ribbon hanging from the other. Her delivery bag remained tucked close beneath one wing.

"You had those all day," Harry accused.

Hedwig nipped his finger.

"I see. Professional secrecy."

The letters were from Hermione, Draconia and Remus. Hermione's covered four pages and included a separate note clarifying that the book from the garden was not his present. Draconia's occupied a single sheet of expensive parchment and contained, in addition to her wishes for the year, a list titled Fourteen Things You Must Attempt Not to Do While Fourteen. The first item was Enter a concealed chamber without either of us. The fourteenth was Treat this list as a challenge.

Remus had written only a short letter. Its final paragraph held Harry longer than the others.

Fourteen was the year your father discovered that confidence and competence were not interchangeable. Sirius has not yet made the same discovery. I trust you to learn from both examples. I am proud of the person you are becoming, Harry—not because of what you have survived, but because survival has not made you cruel.

Harry read the sentence twice, folded the letter and placed it beneath his pillow. The parcel contained a bar of Honeydukes chocolate and a small brass compass tucked within the wrapping. Instead of north, its needle pointed towards words engraved around the face: food, shelter, help, home. At that moment, it indicated home, pointing north-west through the bedroom wall.

Harry's throat tightened. The mirror chimed before he could decide what to do with the feeling, and Sirius appeared wearing a paper hat that leaned over one ear.

"Happy birthday."

Harry held up the compass.

"Remus sent this."

"I know. I found it."

"Did you send anything?"

"Perhaps."

"Is it hidden in the fourth bedroom?"

Sirius's eyes widened with an innocence he did not possess.

"What fourth bedroom?"

"Hermione is terrible at secrets."

"That is why I gave Draconia the important part."

"She's worse when she thinks she's protecting someone."

"I'm disappointed in both of them."

Harry turned the compass. The needle swung faithfully towards the cottage, steadying no matter how he tilted it.

"What happens if I'm already home?"

Sirius grew quiet.

"Then it points towards the people in it," he said.

Harry lowered his face before his expression could become visible.

"Useful," he managed.

"That was the thought."

Sirius cleared his throat. A candle appeared in his hand, pushed into the centre of what looked like an extremely small cake.

"You baked that?"

"Don't insult me on your birthday. Mrs Figg baked it."

"Why is it leaning?"

"Travel."

"Across the street?"

Sirius ignored him and lit the candle with his wand.

"I know this isn't the celebration we planned."

"It's fine."

"Harry."

He stopped. Sirius had removed the paper hat, and without it his face looked tired. He had spent the day dealing with the dream, contacting Dumbledore, questioning Lucius and preparing a house for a boy who had never owned enough things to fill half a room.

"It isn't fine," Sirius said. "You should not have to spend your birthday there."

"It's one day."

"I know."

"And then the wards last another year."

"I know that too."

"Then stop looking as if you're about to duel the sunrise."

"I have considered it."

Harry smiled.

"I'm all right."

Sirius's eyebrow lifted.

"Mostly," Harry amended.

"Better."

He raised the little cake towards the mirror.

"Make a wish."

"You're meant to blow it out."

"The mirror does not transmit air. We discovered that during the pepper incident."

Harry had not been told about a pepper incident and decided to preserve the mystery. He looked at the candle, its small flame leaning when Sirius moved the cake, then standing upright again.

Wishes had once been another form of pretending. At ten, Harry had wished to leave Privet Drive without believing there was anywhere to go. At eleven, the world had opened. At twelve, it had nearly taken two of his friends. At thirteen, it had returned Sirius and revealed how thoroughly adults could fail one another. At fourteen, he knew enough to be careful.

He looked at the compass still pointing towards home.

"Done," he said.

Sirius extinguished the candle between his fingers.

"What did you wish for?"

"If I tell you, it won't come true."

"Convenient superstition."

"You're wearing a paper crown at midnight."

"Hat," Sirius corrected. "Crowns imply responsibility."

They ate cake on opposite sides of the mirror. Harry had a slice Hedwig produced from the bottom of her delivery bag after he apologised for questioning her professionalism. Sirius took enormous mouthfuls and claimed the icing needed testing before anyone else was exposed. For twenty minutes, the room did not feel like Privet Drive.

Then a door slammed downstairs. Sirius heard it through the mirror.

"What was that?"

"Vernon."

"Why is he awake?"

"He's sensed unauthorised happiness."

Footsteps climbed the stairs. Uncle Vernon appeared in the doorway wearing striped pyjamas and an expression of exhausted fury.

"Some of us have work in the morning."

Harry looked at the clock.

"It is the morning."

"Who are you talking to?"

Sirius leaned nearer the mirror.

"Invite him in."

"I'd like to enjoy my birthday."

Vernon heard the voice and stiffened.

"Is that him?"

"Depends. How many innocent escaped convicts are you expecting?"

"Give me that thing."

The demand came from habit. Harry knew it the instant Vernon spoke, and his uncle seemed to recognise it one second later. Neither moved. Harry's hand closed around the mirror, where Sirius had gone utterly still.

"No," Harry said.

Vernon's face darkened. His right hand lifted a fraction, not far enough to reach Harry, but far enough to wake every old reflex.

Harry flinched.

Silence entered the room. Vernon saw it. For years, Harry had imagined being noticed would be worse than being hurt; now shame struck hotter than the instinct itself. His wand was in his hand before he consciously reached for it.

"Step back," Sirius said.

His voice was quiet, which frightened Vernon more than shouting would have. Harry's uncle lowered his hand and retreated into the passage.

"He threatened me," he said, though Harry had not spoken.

"No," Sirius replied through the mirror. "He protected himself. The distinction will be explained to you again if necessary."

Vernon looked from the mirror to the wand.

"At sunrise," he said thickly. "You're out."

Harry's fingers steadied around the handle.

"That has been the plan for twenty-one days."

His uncle turned and went downstairs. Harry waited until the bedroom door below had closed, then placed the wand upon the blanket. Sirius did not ask why he had flinched. That mercy was worse than the question.

"He wasn't going to hit me," Harry said.

"Perhaps not."

"He hasn't since the conditions."

"I know."

"It was just—"

"I know."

Harry rubbed the heel of his hand across his eyes.

"Stop saying that."

"Would you rather I pretended not to?"

The answer should have been yes. It had been yes for most of Harry's life.

"No."

Sirius adjusted the mirror. Harry had assumed he was in the cottage kitchen. Now he saw darkness around him, broken by the amber circle of a streetlamp and the pale edge of a pavement.

Harry sat upright.

"Where are you?"

"Somewhere with terrible hydrangeas."

Harry crossed the room and pulled the curtain aside. Privet Drive stretched empty beneath the summer night. At first he saw nothing. Then a figure separated from the darkness beneath the chestnut tree across the road.

Sirius sat upon the low garden wall beside his motorbike, a dark jacket covering his shoulders and one boot resting against the kerb. His face shone faintly in the mirror Harry held to the glass.

"You said you'd come at sunrise."

"I said you could leave at sunrise."

"How long have you been there?"

"Not long."

"Sirius."

"An hour."

The clock read twelve thirty-seven.

"Sunrise is after five."

"I brought coffee."

"That doesn't make this sensible."

"No, but it makes it possible."

Harry rested his forehead against the cool window. Across the street, Sirius lifted one hand, and Harry raised his own. Glass, distance and old magic remained between them, but none of it felt empty.

"You can sleep," Sirius said through the mirror. "I'll be here."

Harry's gaze drifted to the bed.

"I'm not tired."

"Liar."

"Bad influence."

"Accurate."

Harry returned beneath the blanket with the mirror beside his pillow. Sirius propped his against the motorbike's instrument panel. For a while, they spoke about nothing: the route to the cottage, whether Hedwig would tolerate travelling in the sidecar, the likelihood that Remus had repaired the shelves after Sirius went out.

Eventually, Sirius began reading aloud from the motorbike manual. His voice flattened warnings about fuel lines and braking distances into something steady enough to follow without listening. Beyond the open window, the street remained quiet. Harry let his eyes close, knowing where that voice came from even when he could no longer see the mirror.

He slept.

The next dream began inside the drawing room. Rain struck the windows, and an old man stood beneath floating candles, water darkening the shoulders of his coat. His hand hovered uselessly near an empty pocket. Harry saw no fear in his face until the wand rose from the chair.

The man said something Harry could not hear. Green light crossed the room. His body fell, and Harry fell with it.

For an instant, he lay trapped behind eyes that no longer saw, the floor cold against his cheek. Somewhere above him, the man wearing the black-stone ring asked a question. A pale hand tightened around a wand. The red eyes turned towards Harry as though they could see through death, distance and dream.

"Begin."

Pain split his skull.

Harry woke without knowing he had screamed. The mirror lay beneath one hand, Sirius shouting his name from inside it, while Hedwig circled the room with her wings striking loose parchment into the air.

"Harry. Look at me."

He could not. His scar was no longer a line of heat; it felt as though something beneath the skin had opened and was attempting to burn its way out. He pressed both palms to his forehead, nausea twisting through him.

"He killed someone," he gasped.

Sirius swore.

"Harry, I need you to lower your hands."

"Why?"

"Because I need to see you."

"Still here."

"Prove it."

Harry forced his hands down. The room wavered, Sirius's face appearing in the mirror and at the window beyond it, one close enough to touch and the other trapped across the street.

"Tell me five things you can see," Sirius said.

Harry shut his eyes.

"Open them."

"Bossy."

"Inherited trait. Five things."

Harry swallowed against the nausea.

"The mirror. Hedwig. The wardrobe."

"Two more."

"My trunk." His eyes found the brass compass upon the blanket, its needle pointing through the wall towards Sirius. "The compass."

"Four things you can feel."

"My scar."

"That's one."

"The blanket. The wall. My glasses." Harry realised they hung crooked from one ear. "This is stupid."

"Three things you can hear."

Harry listened: Hedwig's wings, Sirius breathing through the mirror, the faint rumble of a lorry upon the main road. He named them. By the time they reached one thing he could taste, the pain had retreated enough for the room to hold still. He leaned back against the headboard, exhausted by the effort of returning to a place he had never left.

Sirius's face had turned colourless.

"Was it the same house?"

Harry nodded.

"The man with the ring?"

"He was there."

"Who died?"

"An old man. I don't know him."

"Did Voldemort see you?"

The question tightened the room.

"I think so."

Sirius looked away from the mirror. Across the street, Harry saw him rise from the wall and walk to the edge of the pavement, where an invisible boundary stopped him.

He might have forced his way across it. The wards knew him, but they also knew he was the adult who had legally claimed Harry as his own. Bill and Dumbledore had warned that admitting a new guardian during the final night might confuse the old magic about which household sheltered him. No one knew whether a few minutes would undo twenty-one nights. That uncertainty was precisely why Sirius remained upon the pavement, both hands clenched at his sides.

"What time is it?" Harry asked.

"Four fifty-two."

The sky had begun to pale at its edges. Thirteen minutes until sunrise. Harry looked towards the doorway and found Aunt Petunia standing there again, wearing the same dressing gown. This time, Uncle Vernon and Dudley waited behind her. Vernon's face was tight with anger interrupted by uncertainty; Dudley stared at Harry's scar.

"What happened?" Petunia asked.

"A nightmare," Harry said.

"That wasn't a nightmare."

No one corrected her. She entered but did not approach the bed, her attention fixed upon the window where Sirius stood visible beneath the streetlamp.

"Is it renewed?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"How long?"

Sirius answered from the mirror.

"Twelve minutes."

Vernon bristled at his voice but remained silent. For twelve minutes, the five of them waited. No one attempted conversation. Hedwig returned to the wardrobe, Dudley shifted his weight, and a sprinkler started two gardens away, casting silver arcs through the grey air. The ordinary world prepared for morning without caring that ancient magic measured the same light.

At five minutes past five, the first edge of the sun appeared above the rooftops. Something moved through number four.

It began below Harry's feet, a low vibration inside the walls, too deep to hear and impossible not to feel. The window glass brightened. Fine golden lines travelled across it like cracks forming in reverse, joining until a web of light covered the pane. Aunt Petunia gasped as the light passed through ceiling and floor, outlining the door, skirting boards and every corner where shadow had gathered.

For one suspended instant, the whole house seemed to remember the blood from which the protection had been made. Harry felt it recognise him. There was no welcome in it, nothing so gentle as love, but the magic knew him. It knew something in Aunt Petunia too. Golden lines brightened around the hand she placed against the wall, then travelled towards him like threads returning to a knot.

He did not understand what passed between them. Dumbledore had explained conditions, durations and consequences, but never why Lily's protection could recognise love's absence and continue working anyway. The magic settled against his skin. The heat in his scar went cold, and the golden lines disappeared.

The clock changed to 5:06.

Sirius crossed the street. He did not run; Harry knew what the restraint cost him by the set of his shoulders and the speed of his ordinary walk. The front door opened before he reached it. Petunia had left the room without Harry noticing, and now footsteps sounded upon the stairs.

Sirius appeared in the doorway. For a second, no one moved. He looked tired, unshaven and furious, his hair escaped from the tie at his nape. A leather jacket hung open over a dark jumper despite the warm morning. Harry had never seen anyone look more like safety.

Sirius's gaze found the scar, travelled over Harry's face and lowered to the wand still upon the bed. The fury changed shape.

"Twenty-one nights," he said.

Harry swallowed.

"You counted?"

"Every one."

Something broke through the last of Harry's caution. He crossed the room, and Sirius caught him before either could pretend the movement had been accidental.

The embrace was too tight and badly arranged, one hand against the back of Harry's head and the mirror trapped between them. His glasses pressed painfully into someone's shoulder. He held on anyway. Sirius smelled of coffee, night air and motor oil, solid beneath Harry's hands in a way no reflection could quite manage.

"You're all right," he said.

Harry's face was hidden against the leather jacket.

"Mostly."

Sirius laughed once, though the sound failed near the end.

"Better," he murmured.

Behind them, Dudley looked away. Aunt Petunia stood at the top of the stairs with one hand resting against the wall that carried her sister's magic. Vernon had retreated far enough that only part of his striped sleeve remained visible. Harry released Sirius before the moment became something he had to explain.

"My trunk's packed."

Sirius looked at it.

"Since when?"

"June."

The answer escaped before Harry could improve it. Sirius's face went still, and Harry reached for Hedwig's cage.

"We should go before Vernon remembers he has neighbours."

Sirius did not pursue the answer. He lifted the trunk with one hand and the broom case with the other, then stopped at the bedroom door.

"Anything else?"

Harry surveyed the room one last time. The ink stain marked the wardrobe, the broken book lay upon the desk, and a dent in the wall showed where his shoulder had struck it. For twenty-one nights, the room had contained his body without ever becoming his. He took the letters, the compass and the mirror.

"No."

They descended together. In the hall, Uncle Vernon waited beside the open front door.

"The arrangement remains for next summer," Sirius said.

"So we've been told."

"The same conditions."

Vernon's jaw moved.

"He'll be fifteen."

"Well spotted."

"If this little experiment of yours fails," Vernon said, looking at Harry rather than Sirius, "don't expect us to sort it out."

Harry adjusted Hedwig's cage. Once, the words would have found the fear already waiting inside him. Vernon wanted that fear now, wanted him to imagine Sirius growing tired, the cottage disappearing and every offered place revealing itself as temporary. The fear existed. It simply no longer stood alone.

"You couldn't sort out a grapefruit without written instructions," Harry said. "I think we'll manage."

Dudley snorted. Vernon turned purple, and Sirius's mouth twitched.

"Anything you want to say, Petunia?"

She looked at Harry. For a moment, he thought she might mention Lily's dreams. Instead, her gaze moved to the lightning-shaped scar.

"The protection renewed," she said.

"Yes."

"Then it was worth it."

Harry considered the twenty-one nights, the hidden biscuits and every hour counted towards the door.

"It was necessary," he replied.

He stepped outside before she could mistake the difference. Morning air lay cool upon his skin and smelled faintly of cut grass. Curtains shifted along the street as neighbours discovered reasons to be awake before six. Sirius carried the trunk towards the motorbike while Harry secured Hedwig's cage inside the sidecar.

Dudley came as far as the threshold.

"Harry."

He looked back. His cousin shoved both hands into the pockets of his dressing gown.

"See you next year, then."

It was not an apology, and Harry did not pretend it was. It was, however, the first time Dudley had spoken about his return without making it sound like a threat.

"Try not to eat the house," Harry said.

Dudley's mouth tilted.

"Try not to blow up your school."

"No promises."

Harry closed the sidecar. Sirius fastened the trunk to the rear rack with a charm, checked the straps twice and handed him a helmet. A second paper hat had been fixed to the top with Spellotape.

Harry stared.

"Safety equipment," Sirius said.

"The hat?"

"Essential."

"I'm taking it off."

"After we turn the corner."

"Before."

"Then you can walk."

Harry put on the helmet. Sirius swung onto the motorbike, and the engine started with a low mechanical growl that travelled through the quiet street and provoked three separate curtains. Harry settled into the sidecar with Hedwig's cage secured behind him.

"Where are we going?" he asked.

Sirius looked down.

"You know where."

The brass compass in Harry's pocket pointed in the same direction as the bike. He looked once at number four. The front door remained open, Petunia standing behind Dudley, Vernon gone. Its windows reflected the rising sun, revealing no trace of the golden magic now resting inside the brick.

Harry did not wave.

Sirius guided the motorbike away from the kerb. They travelled between sleeping houses at a perfectly legal speed for almost forty seconds. At the end of Privet Drive, he checked the mirrors, turned onto the empty main road and accelerated hard enough to press Harry into the seat. The paper hat tore free and vanished behind them.

"Tragic," Harry called.

"I have others."

Rows of identical roofs gave way to shops with their shutters down, then warehouses, hedgerows and fields silvered by morning. Sirius kept both wheels upon the road until the last cluster of houses disappeared behind a line of trees. Then he touched a switch near the throttle.

The motorbike lifted. Harry's stomach dropped as the road fell away, wind catching his hair beneath the helmet. The rising sun spread across the countryside, touching rivers, roofs and distant church spires until the world appeared briefly made from the same gold as the wards.

Sirius glanced towards him.

"Ready?"

Harry thought of the cottage kitchen visible through the mirror: crooked shelves, unmatched chairs, a fourth bedroom everyone had failed to conceal. He thought of Hermione's four-page letter, Draconia's impossible list and Remus's compass pointing towards people rather than places.

Behind them, Privet Drive became too small to distinguish. Ahead, the needle pointed home.

Harry smiled.

"Take me there."
