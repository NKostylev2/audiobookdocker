The Chamber of Secrets

The first Tuesday of Harry's summer began with an owl flying into a closed window.

The impact rattled the glass and sent Aunt Petunia's carefully arranged breakfast china trembling in the kitchen cupboard. Harry looked up from the flower bed in time to see the bird tumble into the hydrangeas, scattering pale blue petals across the lawn. One small wing appeared between the leaves, flapped with considerable indignation, and vanished again.

He dropped the hand fork and hurried across the garden.

The owl had righted itself by the time he reached it. It stood among several broken stems with its feathers raised and its narrow face turned towards the offending window. After glaring at the glass, it transferred the full weight of its displeasure to Harry, as though he had personally placed the house in its flight path.

"Sorry," Harry whispered. "It's always been there, but I'll have a word with it."

The owl did not appear amused. Aunt Petunia's face materialised on the other side of the window. Her eyes narrowed.

Harry quickly lifted the owl from the flowers and carried it behind the hedge, beyond the kitchen's view. The bird was unfamiliar—tawny, narrow-faced and smaller than most of the school owls—and a roll of parchment had been tied to one leg with red thread.

Harry's name was written across it.

Something inside his chest, quiet for most of the four days since leaving Hogwarts, stirred awake.

The owl pecked him while he untied the knot.

"All right. I said I was sorry."

It pecked him again, apparently finding the apology insufficient.

Harry unfolded the letter.

Harry,

You have been gone for four days, which I realise is not very long, but you promised to write on Tuesday and I thought it would be sensible to write first in case you were uncertain how to begin.

Mum says that is impatient. Dad says it is organised.

The journey home was uneventful after we left King's Cross, although Dad took a wrong turn because he was trying to ask me about the moving staircases while driving. I have explained that staircases are not supposed to move in Muggle houses. He said he found this reassuring.

How are you? Properly—not simply "fine." Have your aunt and uncle said anything about school? Are they allowing Hedwig outside? Have they been feeding you properly? Draconia says I am not allowed to ask all three questions in my first paragraph because it sounds like an interrogation, but she is not here to stop me.

I've begun revising the first-year material so that I don't forget anything before September. Mum says studying during the summer defeats the purpose of having one. She is plainly mistaken.

Write back soon.

Love from Hermione.

Harry read the letter twice.

The garden around him became temporarily less important: the clipped lawn, the straight hedges, the spotless windows and the row of nearly identical houses standing beneath the pale summer sky. Even Aunt Petunia, who remained visible behind the glass and appeared to be calculating the precise cost of the damaged hydrangeas, receded to the edge of things.

Hermione's voice existed between the lines, brisk and concerned and entirely unchanged by the distance between Hogwarts and Surrey. Harry could imagine her bent over the parchment at a tidy desk, adding the final question while insisting to herself that she was not worrying. He could imagine Draconia objecting to the schedule and then examining it closely enough to criticise its punctuation.

They were real.

Hogwarts was real. The castle had not disappeared merely because Harry could no longer see it, and neither had the two people who had crossed a chessboard and gone through a trapdoor with him.

Hermione had written first because she thought he might not know how.  Harry smiled. The owl pecked his thumb again and extended its leg.

"You're worse than she is."

It clicked its beak in a way that suggested this was a compliment.

Harry had prepared his reply the previous night. He kept it folded beneath his mattress beside Draconia's brass compass and Hagrid's wooden flute, where the Dursleys were unlikely to search unless they suspected magic. He had considered hiding the letters inside his school trunk, but Uncle Vernon had locked that in the cupboard beneath the stairs on the evening Harry returned. Apparently, a trunk full of spellbooks was less objectionable if kept near the old place where its owner had once slept.

"I'll be back."

The owl watched him hurry inside. Aunt Petunia intercepted him at the kitchen door.

"What was that?"

"An owl."

"I can see it was an owl."

Harry waited, dirt drying beneath his fingernails.

Her lips thinned. "What did it want?"

"It brought me a letter."

"From that school?"

"From Hermione."

He attempted to step around her. Aunt Petunia moved sideways, blocking the doorway with the practised ease of someone who had spent years ensuring Harry could not pass through places without permission.

"There will be no more of those creatures crashing into my garden."

"I didn't ask it to hit the window."

"That is hardly the point. What will the neighbours think if they see birds delivering messages at all hours?"

"It's ten in the morning."

Aunt Petunia lowered her voice. "Do not be clever with me."

"I thought that was why you sent me to school."

Her mouth opened. Harry decided, a moment too late, that the remark had probably not been worth it.

Behind her, Uncle Vernon lowered the telephone directory he had been reading beside his breakfast. Dudley sat opposite him, using one thick finger to pursue the last smears of egg around his plate.

"What was that?" Vernon demanded.

"Nothing," Harry said.

"He was being insolent," Aunt Petunia said.

"I was agreeing that education is important."

Vernon's face began its familiar journey from pink to purple.

Harry looked back at Aunt Petunia. "I have to send a reply."

"You have to finish the flower beds."

"I will."

"And clean the windows."

"I cleaned them yesterday."

"They are filthy."

The window behind Aunt Petunia remained spotless except for the faint dusty mark left by the owl. Harry glanced meaningfully at it.

"I can probably manage that bit."

"Enough."

Harry recognised the shape of the argument. It had nothing to do with windows, chores or damaged flowers. An owl had arrived and reminded Aunt Petunia that Hogwarts still existed. For the past four days, she had behaved as though Harry's trunk were not locked beneath the stairs, Hedwig did not occupy a cage in his bedroom and her nephew had not returned from school knowing how to make objects float.

No one had asked what he had learned. No one had asked whether he had enjoyed himself or made friends. Uncle Vernon had collected him from King's Cross, looked Draconia and Hermione over as though they were suspicious stains on the station floor, and spent the drive home explaining that there would be no mention of "that nonsense" in his house.

By the time they reached Privet Drive, Harry had almost begun to wonder whether the year had happened at all.

He lowered his eyes before Aunt Petunia could interpret his silence as rebellion.

"I'll finish everything."

She examined him, searching for the part of the answer he had not spoken.

"See that you do."

She stepped aside.

Harry walked upstairs without running. Once inside his bedroom, he shut the door, waited for Aunt Petunia's footsteps to retreat, and pulled the folded reply from beneath his mattress.

The room was the smallest bedroom at Number Four, but it belonged to him in a way the cupboard never had. Dudley's broken toys remained stacked inside the wardrobe, and the wallpaper had faded in pale rectangles where shelves once stood. The mattress sagged towards the middle and the desk wobbled if Harry leaned upon its left side. Even so, he possessed a window, a bed and a door the Dursleys occasionally remembered to knock upon before opening—although this was less courtesy than fear of encountering something magical on the other side.

After a year in Gryffindor Tower, the room felt unnaturally still.

At Hogwarts, there had always been something beyond the curtains: wind worrying at the tower windows, boys snoring in neighbouring beds, staircases groaning through the castle and distant footsteps moving along corridors that never seemed entirely empty. At Privet Drive, Harry lay awake listening to the central heating pipes cool and Uncle Vernon snore through the wall. More than once, he had woken with one hand raised, reaching for bed curtains that were no longer there.

On the second night, he had dreamed of the underground chamber. He had awoken with Quirrell's scream trapped behind his teeth and his scar burning beneath his fringe. No one came to see what was wrong. In the morning, Aunt Petunia complained that he had disturbed Dudley.

Harry had not mentioned the dreams in his letter.

Hedwig clicked her beak from inside her cage.

"I know."

The cage door was secured by a small brass lock. Uncle Vernon had bought it the morning after Hedwig returned from her first hunt, announcing that he would not tolerate a predator coming and going through an upstairs window. The key now lived in his trouser pocket during the day and on his bedside table at night.

Fortunately, Aunt Petunia's hairpins worked nearly as well.

Harry took one from beneath the loose edge of the carpet, inserted it into the lock and worked it carefully against the mechanism. The lock opened with a small click.

Hedwig stepped onto his wrist and reached at once for the parchment.

"This one's not yours."

She nipped his finger. Apparently, that was irrelevant.

Harry reread his reply.

Hermione,

I was going to write today. I remembered the schedule.

The Dursleys are the same. They don't want to talk about Hogwarts, which is probably better than what they would say if they did. Hedwig is fine, but Uncle Vernon doesn't like me letting her out. I'm working on that.

They are feeding me. You asked three times, so I assume that is important.

I haven't started revising. It has been four days.

I am properly fine.

Tell your dad the staircases only move at Hogwarts. Probably.

Harry.

It was not everything.

He had not written that Uncle Vernon locked Hedwig's cage or that Aunt Petunia gave him a list of work each morning and inspected his hands before dinner to ensure the dirt beneath his nails proved he had finished it. He had not explained how difficult eating became beneath the Dursleys' disapproving attention, or that Dudley had started taking food from Harry's plate simply to see whether anyone would stop him.

He had not described the peculiar heaviness that settled over him whenever he remembered how many weeks remained before September. It was not quite sadness. Sadness hurt in a clean, recognisable way. This was flatter than that—a blankness which made getting out of bed feel unnecessary and left him staring at the same page of his Defence book without reading a word.

Those things did not belong in a first letter. He added one more line.

I miss you too.

Hermione had not written that she missed him, but Harry suspected she had meant it anyway. He folded the reply, carried it back outside and tied it to the tawny owl's leg.

"Take that to Hermione."

The bird launched itself from his arm. It cleared the hedge this time, circled the garden once and disappeared beyond the rooftops. Harry watched until it became a dark speck against the brightness and then vanished altogether.

Aunt Petunia rapped sharply against the kitchen window and pointed towards the abandoned flower bed. Harry returned to work smiling.

That letter reached Oxford. For a little while, all of them did.

Draconia's first letter arrived on Friday morning precisely when Hermione's schedule said it would.

It did not arrive by owl.

Harry was washing Uncle Vernon's car when something pale fluttered past his face and landed upon the bonnet. He nearly soaked it with the hose before realising it was a bird folded from thick cream parchment. The paper creature shook several drops of water from its wings, gave him an offended peck with its pointed beak and unfolded itself beneath his hand.

Uncle Vernon, supervising from a garden chair in the shade, lowered his newspaper.

"What was that?"

"A bit of paper."

"It moved."

"It's windy."

There was not enough wind to disturb the steam rising from the pavement. Uncle Vernon looked suspiciously towards the perfectly still hedge. Harry turned the hose upon the front wheel before the parchment could do anything else incriminating.

"Mind the paintwork!"

"I'm using water."

"I know what you're using!"

Harry looked down at the hose. "Good. I was worried."

Vernon folded his newspaper with ominous care. Harry judged that he had approximately ten seconds before his uncle stood up and discovered the letter. He directed the stream towards the opposite side of the car.

"There's a mark near the number plate."

Vernon heaved himself from the chair and went to inspect it. Harry slipped the parchment into his pocket. He waited until he had been sent indoors to refill the bucket before unfolding it beside the kitchen sink.

Potter,

Granger's timetable is tyrannical, intrusive and colour-coded. I have decided to obey it so that she cannot claim I am unreliable.

The Manor remains standing. Father has spent most of the week entertaining people who laugh before he finishes speaking, while Mother has spent it rescuing me from their sons. I have been introduced to seven people I apparently met as an infant. None of them have improved.

I asked whether you might visit later in the summer. Father said the matter would require consideration. Mother asked whether your relatives feed you properly, which means she has not forgotten them.

Have you used the compass? Remember that it can only guide you somewhere you have already been. It cannot locate a person, reveal a secret passage or lead you to buried treasure, regardless of what you and Granger seemed to assume when I gave it to you.

Also, if your relatives have said anything particularly stupid, write it down. I require entertainment.

Draconia.

Harry read the letter again, more slowly.

She had asked whether he could visit.

The possibility opened before him so suddenly that he did not know what to do with it. He pictured the Manor without having any idea what it looked like: dark walls, enormous rooms, perhaps peacocks wandering about because Draconia's father seemed like the sort of person who would own an animal purely because nobody else nearby did. He could not imagine Uncle Vernon allowing the visit. He could imagine Lucius Malfoy arriving to request it, which was considerably better.

Aunt Petunia entered the kitchen while Harry was still smiling at the parchment.

"What have you got?"

"Instructions."

"For what?"

"How to behave around people who laugh before Uncle Vernon finishes speaking."

Her eyes sharpened. "Give it to me."

Harry folded the letter and put it into his pocket.

"No."

The word escaped before caution could stop it.

Aunt Petunia stared at him. Before Hogwarts, Harry might have surrendered the letter simply because refusing would prolong the trouble. But he had spent a year learning that some things could be worth the consequences. Draconia had crossed an enchanted chessboard with him. Hermione had stood between him and a troll.

Aunt Petunia had not earned their words.

Her hand extended. "Harry."

"It's mine."

For several seconds neither moved.

Then Uncle Vernon shouted from outside that the so-called mark had been a reflection and demanded to know why Harry was wasting time.

Aunt Petunia's fingers curled.

"Finish the car."

Harry went, taking the letter with him.

That evening, he replied after the house had gone quiet.

Draconia,

Hermione wrote first, so she still wins.

Your paper bird nearly drowned in a bucket. You should teach the next one to swim.

The compass works. I thought of the kitchen yesterday and it pointed towards the window, which was technically the quickest route. You should have included a warning.

Uncle Vernon spent six minutes looking for a mark on his car that wasn't there. Is that particularly stupid enough, or should I wait for something better?

I would like to visit.

Harry.

He stared at the final line for a while. It looked too honest sitting alone at the bottom of the parchment. Harry considered crossing it out. Instead, he folded the letter and gave it to Hedwig after picking the lock on her cage.

She carried it to Wiltshire and returned the following afternoon with a thin green ribbon tied around one leg. Harry kept the ribbon beneath his mattress with the letters, although he would not have been able to explain why.

- - - - - - -

The following Tuesday brought another letter from Hermione, this time attached to the leg of an elderly grey owl which landed sensibly upon the fence.

Hermione had received Harry's reply. She objected to his definition of properly fine, informed him that four days was more than sufficient time to begin revising and included her parents' telephone number in the margin "in case owl post proves inconvenient."

She also wrote that she and Draconia had exchanged three letters.

She pretends to dislike the timetable, but her second letter arrived twelve minutes before midnight on Friday, so technically she was early. Do not tell her I said so.

Mum and Dad say you may stay with us for part of August if your aunt and uncle agree. We have a spare room, although it is currently full of boxes. Dad says he can move them. Mum says he has been saying that since February.

You did not answer properly about whether you are eating enough.

Love from Hermione.

Harry carried that letter in his pocket for the remainder of the day.

The parchment grew warm against his leg while he weeded the path, cleaned the kitchen cupboards and accompanied Aunt Petunia to the supermarket to carry her bags. Whenever the weight returned to his chest, he touched the folded edge through the fabric.

Hermione wanted him to stay too.

Two invitations did not amount to an escape, precisely. Neither had been approved, and any plan involving Uncle Vernon's agreement possessed an obvious weakness. Even so, August no longer appeared as an uninterrupted stretch of Privet Drive. There was something beyond it which might happen.

That night, Harry sat at his desk with Hedwig watching and attempted to answer Hermione's question about food.

Hermione,

I've eaten every day.

He looked at the sentence. It was true, but she would notice what it avoided.

Harry added:

Usually more than once.

That looked worse. He crossed it out so thoroughly that the nib tore the parchment. Eventually, he began again.

Hermione,

I'm eating enough. Dudley sometimes helps himself to anything interesting, but he considers vegetables dangerous, so those are safe.

I would like to stay in August. I don't know whether the Dursleys will allow it. Uncle Vernon thinks visiting friends leads to gangs, crime and people wearing leather jackets.

Tell your dad I sympathise about the boxes. My room is still full of Dudley's broken things, but they're useful if I ever need half a tennis racket or a television that only shows green.

I have revised Charms. A little.

Harry.

He added the telephone number to the inside cover of his first-year Charms book. He did not intend to lose it.

Draconia wrote again on Friday.

Her letter was longer than the first, although the narrow handwriting made it appear otherwise.

Harry,

Mother says that if your relatives refuse both invitations, she will speak to them herself. Father said this sounded like a threat. Mother said he understood her correctly.

I advise obtaining their refusal in writing. It may amuse her.

Granger has sent me a revision timetable for the entire summer. I returned it with the unnecessary portions removed. She claims the blank parchment was insulting.

Father is in a foul temper because the Ministry has begun searching old magical homes for prohibited objects. He says Arthur Weasley has mistaken interfering in other people's houses for a political achievement. Mother has ordered several rooms closed and told me not to wander. Naturally, this has made me curious.

One of the house-elves dropped an entire tray when Father mentioned Hogwarts yesterday. Father was furious. The elf has been behaving strangely since.

This is probably irrelevant.

Have your relatives agreed to anything yet?

Draconia.

Harry reread the final question, then the sentence about the house-elf.

He knew very little about house-elves. Draconia had mentioned them several times without explaining much, usually in the same manner Aunt Petunia might mention a washing machine or Uncle Vernon the people who cleaned his office. Hermione disliked this and had already promised to find three books on the subject when they returned to Hogwarts.

Harry wondered what could frighten a magical creature merely about the name of the school.

Then Dudley pounded upon the bedroom door and ordered him out of the way of the television, despite the television being downstairs.

Harry folded the letter away. He would ask Draconia in his reply.

The next Tuesday arrived without an owl. Harry noticed before breakfast. He woke at half past six and looked automatically towards the window, expecting to find a bird waiting on the sill or circling beyond the rooftops. The sky remained empty.

That was not alarming. Hermione's owl might have been delayed. Weather existed, even for magical birds, and Oxford could not be so near that every journey took the same length of time.

At ten, Harry was sent outside to trim the hedge. At eleven, he heard wings and stood so quickly that the shears slipped from his hands. A pigeon crossed the road, settled upon a chimney and began preening itself.

By one, Uncle Vernon had noticed Harry looking upwards.

"What are you staring at?"

"The sky."

"What for?"

Harry considered this. "It was there."

Vernon lowered his newspaper. "Don't get smart with me."

"I wasn't giving it credit."

The newspaper snapped shut. Harry returned to the hedge before his uncle could decide what that meant.

No letter arrived that afternoon or evening. Harry left the curtains open after dark and told himself Hermione might have used a school owl which preferred travelling at night.

Wednesday passed. Then Thursday. On Friday morning, Harry woke with the certainty that Draconia's paper bird would strike him in the forehead before breakfast. By noon, he was prepared to accept a less accurate landing.

Nothing came. He did not worry immediately. Hermione's parents might have taken her somewhere. Malfoy Manor was evidently being searched, closed or cleared, and Draconia could have been occupied attempting to enter every room her mother had forbidden.

Harry wrote to both of them anyway. The letters began as jokes.

Hermione,

Your owl is late. I assume you have revised so much that you've forgotten what day it is.

And:

Draconia,

Hermione is already late, so you can still win if you send yours before midnight.

He kept both replies short, folded them and waited until after Uncle Vernon had gone to bed. The brass key to Hedwig's cage sat upon Vernon's bedside table, but the hairpin remained beneath Harry's carpet. By midnight, the house had settled. Pipes clicked softly inside the walls. Dudley's snores travelled across the landing in uneven bursts.

Harry freed Hedwig and tied both letters to her leg.

"Hermione first," he whispered. "Then Draconia."

Hedwig touched her beak briefly to his wrist and launched through the open window.

Harry watched her pale shape cross the garden. She cleared the hedge, rose above the neighbouring roofs and turned north.

Then something happened.

There was no flash and no visible barrier. The darkness ahead of Hedwig appeared to bend, like air above a summer road, and a sharp crack broke the silence. Hedwig jerked sideways in mid-flight. For one terrible moment, one wing folded and she dropped behind the roofs.

Harry gripped the windowsill.

"Hedwig?"

The night gave no answer.

He remained at the window for nearly an hour, barely breathing whenever something moved below. At last, wings beat heavily beside the house and Hedwig struck the sill with enough force to scrape both feet across the wood.

Harry caught her before she fell.

Her feathers were disordered. One wing hung lower than the other, and a grey stain marked the white plumage along her back. Both letters remained attached to her leg.

"What happened?"

Hedwig snapped her beak and tried to look towards the window.

"Were you hurt?"

She permitted him to examine the wing, protesting only when his fingers reached the joint. Nothing appeared broken, although the muscles trembled beneath his touch. Harry fetched water from the bathroom and tore a piece from the bread he had hidden after dinner.

Hedwig drank, then pressed herself into the corner of her cage and watched the open window.

Harry untied the letters. The knots were still his.

He had seen Hedwig fly through storms. He had watched her cross the Great Hall through crowds of hundreds of students and deliver letters without dropping so much as a feather. She had found Hagrid's hut, Hermione's home and Malfoy Manor without difficulty before.

She had not become lost above Little Whinging.

Something had turned her back. Harry closed the window and remained awake until sunrise. At breakfast, Uncle Vernon objected to the shadows beneath his eyes.

"You look dreadful."

"Thank you."

"That was not a compliment."

"I thought it would be strange if it was."

Dudley laughed before realising he was not supposed to. Uncle Vernon's stare moved from Harry to the ceiling, as if expecting unnatural creatures to descend through it.

"You haven't been letting that bird out?"

"The cage is locked."

It was not technically an answer.

"See that it remains that way."

Harry spread a thin layer of marmalade across his toast. His stomach felt too tightly drawn to accept it, but Aunt Petunia watched what remained on his plate and occasionally used uneaten food as proof that he did not require as much the following day.

He swallowed without tasting it. That night, Harry tried sending the letters again.

Hedwig refused.

She stood on the desk with her wings partly spread, placing herself between Harry and the open window. When he moved the envelopes towards her, she struck his hand hard enough to draw blood.

"Ow!"

Hedwig clicked her beak furiously.

"All right. Don't."

Harry lowered the letters. Her feathers remained raised until he shut the window.

Only then did she permit him to touch her.

Harry sat upon the edge of the bed, holding his bleeding hand in the hem of his shirt. The room seemed smaller than it had the previous evening. He could hear Uncle Vernon laughing at something on television below and Aunt Petunia moving plates in the kitchen.

No one else knew anything was wrong. No one else would have cared if they did.

The thought arrived with the ease of long practice. Harry rejected it at once, but that did not prevent it leaving a mark.

He tried Muggle post next.

Hermione had written her full address beneath the telephone number, explaining that an owl would understand merely Hermione Granger, Oxford, but the Royal Mail required greater precision. Harry copied it carefully onto an envelope, placed both letters inside and used a stamp taken from the back of Aunt Petunia's writing desk.

The following morning, she sent him to the corner shop for milk.

Harry carried the envelope inside his shirt. At the end of Privet Drive, after checking that no one was watching from the windows, he slipped it through the mouth of the red postbox.

The small metallic sound it made upon landing felt absurdly satisfying.

For the next three days, he waited. On the fourth morning, the envelope appeared upon the mat with the ordinary post.

A strip of white paper had been pasted across Hermione's address.

NOT KNOWN AT THIS ADDRESS.

Harry checked every number and letter against the address in her parchment. They were identical. He took the returned envelope upstairs and placed it beside the others.

The following afternoon, he tried the telephone.

Aunt Petunia had gone to Mrs Next Door's house, Dudley was terrorising smaller children at the park and Uncle Vernon would not return from work for another hour. Harry took his Charms book from his room, opened the cover and carried Hermione's number downstairs.

The telephone stood upon a little table in the hall. A small lock had been fitted through the dial.

Harry stared at it. The lock was new.

Uncle Vernon had installed it at some point during the week without mentioning it, which meant either Dudley had been making expensive calls again or the Dursleys had anticipated Harry trying to reach someone. Harry suspected the second possibility, though he did not understand how they could have known.

He was examining the mechanism with another hairpin when the front door opened.

Aunt Petunia stopped on the threshold. Harry straightened.

Her eyes travelled from the telephone to the hairpin in his hand.

"What do you think you are doing?"

"Checking whether the lock works."

"You were trying to telephone those...people."

"Only one of them."

"You are not to bring anything from that school into this house."

"I didn't realise conversations were physical things you could bring places."

"Do not use that tone with me."

Harry lowered the hairpin. "What tone?"

"That one."

It was useful, Harry thought, that Aunt Petunia could hear offences invisible to everyone else. She snatched the Charms book from beneath his arm. The moment she saw the moving figures on its cover, she dropped it with a gasp.

Harry picked it up before she could recover. Aunt Petunia pointed upstairs.

"Your room. Now."

"I haven't made lunch."

"You won't be needing it."

Harry climbed the stairs before the argument could become worse. His appetite had disappeared anyway.

That evening, Uncle Vernon confiscated Aunt Petunia's hairpins, searched Harry's room and moved his school trunk from beneath the stairs into the cupboard under the stairs, where a second lock was added to the door. He found none of the letters. Harry had placed them beneath a loose floorboard as soon as he heard Vernon's car in the drive.

The next morning, the lock on Hedwig's cage had been replaced with a larger one.

After that, Harry possessed six letters he could not send, a telephone number he could not call and an owl too frightened to cross the boundary of Privet Drive.

The silence became harder to dismiss. Harry counted the days despite trying not to. Tuesday arrived again. Hermione did not write. Friday followed. Draconia's paper bird did not appear.

He did not decide that they had forgotten him. The thought came, because some thoughts arrived regardless of whether they were welcome, but Harry refused to keep it.

Hermione did not leave things unfinished. If she had been prevented from writing, she would be investigating why. If one owl failed, she would send another. If every owl in Britain refused, she would probably compose a formal complaint to the Ministry and make her father drive her to Surrey.

Draconia did not make promises carelessly. She might complain about Hermione's timetable until September, but she would obey it purely to prevent Hermione being right about her. More importantly, Draconia knew what silence could do. She had watched Harry hesitate before returning to the Dursleys. She had pressed the compass into his hand and told him that being unable to see a road did not mean it had ceased to exist.

Neither of them would simply stop. Harry knew that during the day. At night, knowledge became less reliable.

He would lie beneath the thin sheet while heat gathered in the room, listening to the slow movements of the house and imagining possibilities. Perhaps Hermione had written and her owl had been hurt. Perhaps something had happened at Malfoy Manor. Perhaps the house-elf Draconia mentioned had been frightened for a reason.

Or perhaps his replies had been wrong.

Perhaps I miss you too had been too much. Perhaps Draconia regretted inviting him once her parents learned more about the Dursleys—or more about Harry. Perhaps friendship felt different outside Hogwarts, without trolls, trapdoors and suspicious professors to hold it together.

Harry knew those explanations were unreasonable. That did not stop his mind producing them.

The Dursleys had taught him early that affection could be withdrawn without warning, although what they offered him had rarely deserved the name. Meals became smaller after accidents. Doors were locked after questions. A good week could end because Dudley was bored or Uncle Vernon had been embarrassed at work. Harry had learned not to rely upon anything that could be taken away.

At Hogwarts, he had broken that rule. He had relied upon Hermione. He had relied upon Draconia. Somewhere between Halloween and the underground chambers, he had begun expecting them to remain beside him.

The letters had been proof that they would.

Now there were none.

Harry stopped taking his schoolbooks from beneath the floorboard. His unfinished History essay remained folded between two chapters for more than a week. Hagrid's flute stayed beneath the mattress because holding it reminded him of the night below the trapdoor, and remembering the trapdoor led inevitably to remembering the hospital wing, the feast and the moment the train had carried him away from everyone who knew him.

He continued performing Aunt Petunia's chores. Those required movement rather than interest. He weeded gardens, washed windows, swept the drive and repainted a garden bench which had not needed painting. When a task ended, he often remained where he was until someone ordered him elsewhere.

Food lost what little appeal it possessed. Harry hid bread out of habit and then forgot to eat it. At night, he woke from dreams he could not remember with his heart racing and his fingers clenched around the sheet. By morning, exhaustion had flattened even his fear.

Aunt Petunia interpreted his quietness as improved behaviour.

Uncle Vernon called it "finally learning his place."

Dudley noticed Harry no longer argued when food disappeared from his plate and began taking more. Only Hedwig appeared to understand. She watched Harry from inside her locked cage, occasionally giving a low, questioning hoot. When he sat too long without moving, she rattled the door until he looked at her.

Harry began carrying Draconia's compass in his pocket.

When he thought of the kitchen, the needle pointed downstairs. When he thought of the cupboard, it turned towards the landing and dipped. When he thought of Hogwarts, it spun uselessly beneath the glass.

The compass could guide him only to places he had visited. Neither Oxford nor Malfoy Manor existed upon Harry's personal map. He found himself resenting that.

Once, he held the compass flat in his palm and thought not of a place but of Hermione. The needle circled three times and stopped nowhere.

He tried Draconia. It spun again.

"Brilliant," Harry muttered. "Very reassuring."

The compass remained unapologetic. The strange incidents began near the middle of July. At first, they were small enough for Harry to doubt.

A branch moved in the hedge on a windless afternoon. Something pale disappeared behind the garden shed when he turned his head. Hedwig woke in the middle of the night and fixed her amber eyes upon the empty corner beside his wardrobe, feathers pressed close to her body.

Harry searched the room and found nothing.

Two days later, he discovered a small print in the damp earth beneath his window. It resembled a bare foot, except that no child's foot could have been so narrow or possessed such long, crooked toes.

Rain erased it before Harry could decide what it meant. Then came the noises.

Soft cracks sounded from rooms nobody occupied. A board creaked behind Harry when he stood alone upon the landing. Once, while Aunt Petunia was lecturing him about leaving fingerprints on the refrigerator, a cupboard door opened by itself over her shoulder.

Aunt Petunia blamed Harry.

"I'm standing over here."

"That doesn't prove anything."

"It proves I didn't touch the cupboard."

"You know perfectly well what I mean."

Harry did know. She meant magic, although she would rather accuse him of it for an hour than speak the word once. He looked at the open cupboard.

For an instant, something moved in the dark gap beneath the kitchen table. Harry bent down.

Nothing was there.

After that, he began feeling watched. The sensation followed him from room to room. It settled between his shoulders while he worked in the garden and remained outside his door when he tried to sleep. Hedwig felt it too. She spent long periods staring through the window, tracking movements Harry could not see.

One afternoon, while clipping the hedge beside the pavement, Harry became certain someone was observing him.

He stopped.

Privet Drive lay almost motionless beneath the heat. Cars stood baking in their drives. A sprinkler ticked across the lawn two houses away, sending brief rainbows through the sunlight. Somewhere beyond the roofs, a dog barked and was abruptly silenced.

Harry searched the windows opposite.

Nothing moved. Then the hedge rustled. He lowered the clippers. A pair of enormous green eyes stared through the leaves.

Harry froze.

They were too large and too low to belong to a person. They did not blink. For several seconds, Harry and whatever occupied the hedge watched one another through the branches.

Then the eyes vanished. Leaves shook violently. Something small moved between them with a speed no cat could have managed.

Harry stepped closer.

"Hello?"

No answer came. He parted the hedge. Only tangled branches and packed earth waited on the other side. A soft crack sounded near the garden gate.

Harry spun around.

"Boy!"

Uncle Vernon's voice crashed across the garden. Harry dropped the branches.

Vernon stood in the doorway wearing a short-sleeved shirt strained across his stomach and an expression of tremendous personal offence.

"Are you planning to finish that hedge today?"

"There was something in it."

"There is usually something in a hedge. It's a hedge."

"I think it was watching me."

Uncle Vernon glanced towards the neighbouring houses. His face tightened.

"What have I told you about saying things like that where people might hear?"

"I don't think the hedge is going to tell anyone."

Vernon's moustache bristled.

Harry looked back at the leaves. "It had eyes."

"Most living creatures do."

"So you agree there was something there."

"I agree that you are wasting time!"

Vernon crossed the lawn, seized Harry by the shoulder and marched him towards the house.

"I will not have you creeping around the garden and muttering to bushes. The Masons are coming to dinner on Friday, and everything will be normal."

Harry had heard a great deal about the Masons.

Mr Mason owned a company which sold drills. Uncle Vernon hoped to secure the largest order of his career and had spent the previous fortnight rehearsing the dinner as though preparing a military operation. Aunt Petunia had selected the menu, flowers and approved topics of conversation. Dudley's role was to appear polite, which required the most rehearsal.

Harry's role was simpler.

He would not exist.

"You will be in your room," Vernon continued once the front door had closed. "Door shut. Curtains closed. No walking about, no noise and no unnatural business."

Harry looked towards the stairs.

"What about dinner?"

"You'll eat beforehand."

Aunt Petunia entered from the kitchen carrying two shopping bags. "Only after the garden is finished."

Vernon released Harry's shoulder and took the bags from her.

"The Masons are respectable people. If this dinner goes well, I could have the order signed before pudding. You will not ruin that by frightening them with tales about things watching from the shrubbery."

"I wasn't planning to discuss the hedge over dinner."

"You will not be at dinner."

"Ill just pretend I don't exist I guess."

Vernon pointed upstairs. "Go."

Harry climbed the steps before his expression betrayed him. Inside his room, Hedwig was awake. She stood upon her perch with her head tilted, staring down towards the hedge.

"You saw it too."

Her feathers rose. A faint impression marked the dust on the outer windowsill. Five long fingers. Harry closed the curtains.

Friday was the thirty-first of July. Harry's twelfth birthday began at seven in the morning when Aunt Petunia hammered upon his door and ordered him to clean the downstairs windows.

No one mentioned the date.

Harry had not expected the Dursleys to remember. He had spent ten birthdays learning not to expect that, and the eleventh had been unusual enough to compensate for several ordinary disappointments. Even so, as he carried a bucket into the garden, he looked automatically towards the sky.

Hermione would remember. Draconia would pretend she had nearly forgotten and then send something selected weeks ago. Perhaps birthdays did not follow the ordinary Tuesday-and-Friday arrangement.

At breakfast, the post fell through the letter box.

Harry's head lifted. Uncle Vernon gave him a warning look before collecting it. He sorted through two bills, a catalogue and a postcard addressed to Aunt Petunia. Nothing bore green ink, red thread or a Malfoy seal.

Dudley received the final rasher of bacon. Harry went outside. By midday, no owl had appeared. At three, he heard a sharp crack beyond the garden wall and ran to the gate, but the pavement was empty. A tabby cat watched him from beneath a parked car.

The evening of the Masons' dinner arrived with oppressive precision. By six, every downstairs surface gleamed. Aunt Petunia had arranged flowers in the hall and baked a pudding decorated with sugared violets. Uncle Vernon wore his best suit. Dudley's hair had been combed flat and parted so severely that it appeared painted upon his head.

Harry received two slices of bread, a lump of cheese and a glass of water at half past five. Aunt Petunia watched him eat.

"You are to take the plate directly to the kitchen when you finish. I don't want crumbs upstairs."

"It wouldn't be a proper birthday without the threat of crumbs."

Her hand stopped upon the back of a chair. Harry looked down at his plate. For one moment, something uncertain passed across Aunt Petunia's face. She had remembered after all. Perhaps she had remembered all day.

Then her mouth tightened.

"Do not start."

"I wasn't."

"You know perfectly well we have more important matters tonight."

Mr Mason and his drill order, Harry thought, had finally outranked the day Aunt Petunia's sister died. He finished the bread. At quarter past six, Uncle Vernon conducted his final inspection.

"You will remain upstairs until the guests leave."

"I know."

"No owls."

"Hedwig's cage is locked."

"No noises."

"I know."

"No funny business."

Harry's temper stirred, too tired to become anything useful.

"I'll schedule the funny business for tomorrow."

Vernon stepped closer. "You will not ruin this for me."

Harry met his uncle's eyes and understood that Vernon genuinely considered him capable of destroying the evening merely by existing too loudly above it.

"I won't."

The doorbell rang. Everyone moved.

Aunt Petunia hurried towards the kitchen. Dudley arranged his face into an expression of revolting innocence. Vernon smoothed his moustache and opened the door with a booming laugh Harry had never heard him use with his family.

"Mr and Mrs Mason! What a pleasure!"

Harry slipped upstairs, closed his bedroom door and sat upon the bed.

Below, rehearsed voices moved through the hall. Uncle Vernon made a joke about Japanese golfers. Mr Mason laughed. Aunt Petunia offered drinks. Dudley welcomed the guests with a sentence he had practised fourteen times, though he still placed the emphasis upon the wrong word.

Harry waited for wings against the window. None came. He reached beneath the loose floorboard.

Eight letters waited there—four addressed to Hermione and four to Draconia. The earliest had begun lightly. The latest did not. Beside them lay the four letters he had received, their parchment softened at the folds from being read too often.

Harry unfolded Hermione's second letter.

We have a spare room.

He read Draconia's.

Mother says that if your relatives refuse, she will speak to them herself.

The words belonged to a future which had seemed possible when they were written.

Harry placed both letters back and opened the one he had composed that afternoon.

Hermione,

It's my birthday. I don't mean that accusingly. I know you would remember.

Hedwig can't deliver anything. Something stopped her when she tried, and now she is frightened to leave. I tried ordinary post, but it came back even though I copied your address exactly. The Dursleys have locked the telephone.

I haven't heard from you or Draconia in weeks.

I don't think this is either of you. If this somehow reaches you, tell Draconia I'm all right. There is something watching the house.

Harry.

He had written I'm all right because Hermione would expect it.

He had not decided whether it was true.

Downstairs, Mr Mason began explaining the importance of reliable machinery. Uncle Vernon responded with reverent agreement. Cutlery touched china. Aunt Petunia laughed too loudly at something which could not possibly have been funny.

Harry looked around the dim bedroom.

The Dursleys had taken his school things, confined Hedwig and closed every route by which he might reach his friends. Something unseen had turned his owl from the sky. Green eyes watched through hedges, cupboards opened by themselves and small handprints appeared outside an upstairs window.

It was his birthday. He had never felt further from Hogwarts.

A floorboard suddenly creaked. Harry looked towards the door. The sound had come from behind him. He turned, his heart rate suddenly picking up. Something sat upon his desk.

Harry surged to his feet.

The creature had long ears, enormous green eyes and a narrow face balanced above a thin body wrapped in what looked like an old pillowcase. Its bare feet rested among Harry's ink bottles. One bony hand lay over the telephone number Hermione had written inside his Charms book.

Those were the eyes from the hedge. Hedwig pressed herself against the bars of her cage and gave a low, furious hiss. The creature lifted one long finger to its lips.

Harry stared.

"What are you?"

The creature's ears sank until their pointed ends nearly brushed its shoulders.

"Dobby, sir," it whispered. Its voice was high and trembling, barely audible over the murmur of conversation below. "Just Dobby. Dobby is a house-elf."

Harry's eyes moved over the pillowcase, the bare feet and the long, thin fingers curled around the edge of his Charms book. A house-elf.

Draconia's last letter returned to him: One of the house-elves dropped an entire tray when Father mentioned Hogwarts yesterday.

Harry looked towards the bedroom door. Uncle Vernon's laughter rose from the dining room, enormous and false, followed by Mr Mason's lower chuckle. Cutlery touched china. Aunt Petunia began praising the advantages of living in a quiet, respectable neighbourhood.

"How did you get in?"

"House-elves can go where house-elves is needing to go."

"That doesn't answer the question."

Dobby's enormous eyes widened, as though nobody had previously objected to one of his answers on grounds of usefulness. Harry glanced at the locked door, then towards the barred shadow of the window frame.

"Were you in the hedge?"

Dobby twisted his fingers together.

"And outside my window?"

The elf's ears drooped further.

"You left the handprint."

"Dobby needed to see Harry Potter, sir."

"You could have knocked."

"Dobby could not risk being seen."

"So you watched me through a hedge for two weeks?"

Dobby seemed to consider this description unfair.

"Dobby was waiting for the proper moment."

"You chose the dinner Uncle Vernon has been talking about since June."

"Yes, sir."

"Your sense of timing needs work."

The elf slid down from the desk and landed soundlessly upon the carpet. He bowed so low that his long nose brushed the floor.

"Dobby has come to warn Harry Potter."

The humour left Harry's voice.

"Warn me about what?"

Dobby lifted his face. Fear filled every part of it—not the startled fear of being discovered, but something older and more deeply settled. Harry had seen a similar expression whenever Aunt Petunia mentioned his mother and whenever Hagrid spoke Voldemort's name.

"Harry Potter must not return to Hogwarts."

For several seconds, Harry wondered whether he had misunderstood.

"What?"

"There is a plot, sir. A terrible plot. Terrible things will happen at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry this year."

Harry looked at the creature more closely. Dobby was not lying. Whatever else the elf had done, he believed every word he was saying.

"What sort of things?"

Dobby's mouth closed.

"Who is planning them?"

The house-elf began to shake.

"Dobby cannot say."

"Can't or won't?"

"Dobby has been forbidden to speak of his masters' business."

The last word emerged before Dobby seemed able to stop it. His eyes bulged. Both hands flew to his mouth.

"Your masters," Harry repeated. "The people you work for know about this?"

Dobby seized one long ear and twisted it viciously.

"Bad Dobby! Wicked Dobby!"

He lunged towards the wardrobe. Harry caught him around the middle an instant before his head struck the wood.

"Stop that."

"Dobby must punish himself!"

"Why?"

"Dobby almost spoke ill of his family."

"You didn't even tell me who they are."

"Dobby knows what Dobby nearly said."

He fought surprisingly hard for something so small. Harry tightened his grip and pulled him away from the wardrobe, painfully aware of the voices directly beneath them.

"If you keep making noise, Uncle Vernon will punish both of us."

Dobby stopped struggling. The effect of the words was immediate. His thin body went still in Harry's hands, and his eyes moved towards the door.

"Harry Potter's family punishes him?"

"They call it discipline."

Dobby studied his face with an awful, understanding attention. Harry did not enjoy being understood by someone wearing a pillowcase. He released the elf carefully. Dobby remained where he was, rubbing the ear he had twisted.

"Who sent you?"

"No one sends Dobby. Dobby comes alone."

"Your masters don't know you're here?"

Dobby shook his head so rapidly that his ears struck his cheeks.

"If they found out?"

The elf looked at the floor. Harry glanced at the fading bruises around Dobby's wrists. He had initially mistaken them for shadows.

"What would they do?"

"Dobby deserves whatever his masters decide."

"That isn't what I asked."

Dobby did not answer. Harry sat upon the edge of the bed, placing himself between the elf and the wardrobe in case another punishment occurred to him.

"All right. Nobody sent you. You heard about a plot, and you decided to warn me yourself."

"Yes, sir."

"Why me?"

Dobby's expression transformed. Awe displaced some of the fear, which Harry found no more comfortable.

"Dobby has heard of Harry Potter's greatness from his Mistress sir. All house-elves has heard how Harry Potter defeated He-Who-Must-Not-Be-Named and made the world safer, even if wizards did not change as much as Dobby hoped they would."

Harry resisted the urge to cover his scar.

"I was one."

"A great and good wizard even then."

"I couldn't talk."

Dobby blinked. That objection had apparently never occurred to him.

"My mother stopped him," Harry said. "I survived."

"Harry Potter is humble."

"Harry Potter is trying to explain what happened."

The elf gazed at him adoringly. Harry frowned, realising he had spoken in third person, abandoned the effort.

"If something dangerous is going to happen at Hogwarts, I need to tell Professor Dumbledore."

"No!"

Dobby's cry rang through the room. Conversation ceased below. Harry caught the elf as he staggered backwards, clapping one hand over Dobby's mouth before he could say anything else.

Uncle Vernon's chair scraped across the dining-room floor. Harry held still. Heavy footsteps entered the hall. One stair creaked. Harry pressed a finger to his lips. Dobby nodded frantically against his palm.

From below, Mr Mason asked a question about delivery contracts. Uncle Vernon paused. Harry could almost hear his uncle choosing between investigating the noise and losing control of the conversation downstairs.

"Just the plumbing," Vernon called, forcing a laugh. "Old pipes. Perfectly reliable house otherwise."

His footsteps retreated. Harry waited until the dining-room door closed before removing his hand.

"Not another sound like that."

"Dobby is sorry, sir."

"Why can't I tell Dumbledore?"

"Dobby cannot allow Harry Potter to interfere."

"Interfere with what?"

"With—"

Dobby's jaw locked. He struck the side of his own head with one fist. Harry caught his wrist before the second blow.

"Stop punishing yourself and try answering a question."

"Dobby cannot betray his masters."

"You've already disobeyed them by coming here."

"Dobby is betraying them to save Harry Potter. Dobby cannot betray them more."

Harry released his wrist. The elf's reasoning appeared to have been assembled in a way Hermione would have found personally offensive.

"When did you learn about this plot?"

Dobby's gaze moved nervously around the room.

"Not until this summer. Something was kept hidden for many years. Something belonging to bad times."

Harry felt the hairs rise along his arms.

"What was?"

"Dobby cannot say."

"And now it isn't hidden?"

The elf made a small, miserable sound.

"That's why the letters stopped when they did," Harry said. "You didn't start watching the house until you knew the plot had begun."

Dobby pressed his lips together. Harry had his answer.

"Does the person using this thing know what it can do?"

Dobby's face changed. Only slightly—but Harry saw it.

"No," he said slowly. "They don't."

"Dobby did not say that."

"They think they do, but they're wrong."

"Dobby did not say that either."

"What do they think will happen?"

The elf started wringing his hands again.

"Old magic does not always obey the wizard who carries it. Some things remembers their first master better than the next."

The moment the words escaped, Dobby snatched Harry's desk lamp and brought it down upon his own head. Harry caught it halfway.

"That was nearly useful."

"Bad Dobby!"

"Was the first master Voldemort?"

Dobby recoiled so violently that his back struck the desk.

"Do not say the name!"

"That's who you mean."

"Dobby cannot—"

"He left something behind, and someone is taking it to Hogwarts."

Dobby covered his face. Harry's thoughts moved rapidly. The Stone was gone. Quirrell was dead. Voldemort had fled, bodiless and weakened, but Harry had never believed he had vanished for good. Dumbledore had not believed it either.

"Does he want revenge?"

Dobby shook his head and nodded at the same time.

"Is he after me?"

"Harry Potter will be in terrible danger."

"That isn't the same thing."

"Harry Potter must promise not to return."

"I'm not promising that."

Dobby stared at him.

"Someone tried to steal the Philosopher's Stone last year," Harry continued. "Voldemort was using one of the professors. Hermione, Draconia and I stopped him."

"Exactly, sir! Harry Potter is always placing himself in mortal danger."

"I didn't place myself anywhere. The danger was already there."

"Harry Potter went beneath the school!"

"So did they."

"Harry Potter faced the Dark Lord!"

"Because they got me far enough to do it."

Dobby's expression suggested this distinction was unimportant.

"If something is going to happen at Hogwarts," Harry said, "I'm not staying here while Hermione and Draconia walk into it."

"They is not as important as Harry Potter."

"They are to me."

Dobby fell silent. Harry had not intended the words to come out like that. He was not entirely certain what they meant, except that his own safety could not be separated neatly from theirs. Knowing they were in danger while he remained trapped at Privet Drive would not be safety. It would simply be another kind of fear.

He looked at Dobby.

"You know Draconia, don't you?"

The elf went rigid. Harry remembered Draconia's letter. The frightened house-elf. Her father's anger over Ministry searches. Rooms at the Manor being closed. Objects being removed or concealed.

"Do you work for someone she knows?"

Dobby stepped backwards.

"Do you work for her family?"

His entire body began to tremble. Harry did not need him to answer.

"Does Draconia know about the plot?"

Dobby covered his mouth.

"Is she involved?"

The elf shook his head desperately. Relief came so fast that it hurt.

"Is she in danger?"

Dobby's eyes filled with tears.

"Miss—"

He bit down upon his hand. Harry pulled it away from his mouth.

"Don't."

"Dobby cannot speak of the young mistress."

There it was. Not proof, perhaps, but close enough that Hermione would already have considered the matter settled.

"You are a Malfoy house-elf."

Dobby made a strangled noise and tried to dive beneath the desk. Harry caught his pillowcase.

"Draconia mentioned you."

"She must not know Dobby came here!"

"Why? Will she punish you?"

Dobby looked bewildered.

"The young mistress does not punish Dobby. Not like—"

His hands clapped over his mouth again. Harry's anger faltered.

Draconia had spoken about house-elves with the thoughtlessness of someone discussing furniture. Hermione had objected. Harry had stayed out of the argument because he knew nothing about it.

Looking at Dobby's wrists, he began to think Hermione had known enough.

"If Draconia is in danger, you should warn her too."

"Dobby cannot. Dobby belongs to the family. Dobby cannot interfere with the family's plans."

"But you can interfere with mine?"

"Dobby is already a bad elf."

"I'm beginning to agree."

Dobby's face crumpled. Harry immediately regretted the words, although he remained too angry to take them back. The house-elf's eyes drifted towards the letters spread across the bed.

Harry followed his gaze.

The strangeness of the summer arranged itself all at once: the owls turning back, Hedwig being struck from the sky, the returned Muggle post, the small noises inside the house and the green eyes watching from the hedge.

Cold replaced Harry's anger for one terrible instant.

"How do you know about my letters?"

Dobby backed against the wall.

"What did you do?"

"Dobby only wanted Harry Potter to believe his friends had forgotten him."

The room seemed to contract. Harry looked at the unanswered letters beneath his hand. The earliest still contained jokes. The later ones contained questions he had tried not to make sound frightened.

"They wrote to me."

Dobby's ears sagged.

"How many?"

The elf hesitated, then reached into the front of his pillowcase.

He withdrew a thick bundle of parchment tied with mismatched ribbons.

Harry knew the handwriting before he could read a single word. Hermione's careful script covered one envelope. Draconia's sharper letters marked another. There were folded paper birds among them, their wings motionless beneath Dobby's fingers.

Harry rose.

"Give them to me."

"Harry Potter must promise first."

"They're mine."

"Dobby hoped that if Harry Potter believed his friends did not care, Harry Potter would no longer wish to return to school."

"You thought making me believe I had nobody would protect me?"

"Dobby knows that those who has no one learn to obey."

The words stopped Harry. Dobby did not sound cruel. He sounded certain.

For the first time, Harry understood that the plan had made sense to him. Somewhere in the house where Dobby served, loneliness was not merely an unhappiness. It was a method. People deprived Dobby of kindness until the absence of it became another chain, and Dobby had assumed Harry could be controlled in the same way.

"You're wrong," Harry said.

Dobby's fingers tightened around the letters.

"Hermione doesn't leave things unfinished. Draconia doesn't forget promises. I knew something had happened."

"But Harry Potter was sad."

That landed closer than Harry wanted it to. He held out his hand.

"Give them to me."

"Will Harry Potter promise not to return to Hogwarts?"

"No."

"Even knowing terrible things waits there?"

"Especially then."

Dobby began to cry in earnest. Harry kept his hand extended.

"You frightened Hedwig. You stopped Hermione and Draconia from knowing whether I was safe. You don't get to call that protection."

"Dobby is sorry."

"Give me the letters."

The house-elf looked at the parchment, then at Harry.

"If Harry Potter will not listen, Dobby must find another way."

He vanished. Harry lunged for the bundle. His fingers closed upon empty air. A sharp crack sounded downstairs. Then Aunt Petunia screamed, Harry ran.

He reached the landing as the dining-room door flew open. Uncle Vernon emerged first, his face red and confused, followed closely by Mr Mason. Harry slipped past them and reached the kitchen in time to see Aunt Petunia's sugared-violet pudding floating near the ceiling.

Mrs Mason stood directly beneath it.

For one absurd moment, everyone stared upwards.

The pudding drifted serenely above their heads. Cream slid down one side and gathered along the edge of the serving dish. Aunt Petunia's hands rose helplessly.

Harry saw Dobby crouched on top of a cupboard. Whether the others could not see him or were too distracted by the hovering pudding, Harry did not know.

The elf looked miserable.

"Dobby," Harry whispered. "Don't."

Dobby raised one finger.

The serving dish tilted.

Harry drew his wand before remembering it was locked beneath the stairs. Even if he had possessed it, using magic would bring the Ministry.

That, he realised, was precisely what Dobby intended.

"He wants them to stop me going back," Harry said.

"What?" Uncle Vernon demanded.

The pudding dropped. It struck Mrs Mason across the head. Cream, sponge and violet icing exploded across her hair, face and evening dress. The serving dish shattered upon the tiles. One sugared flower landed in Mr Mason's pocket.

No one moved. Then Mrs Mason began screaming. The kitchen dissolved into chaos.

Aunt Petunia rushed forward with a cloth, apologising so quickly that the words collided with one another. Mr Mason shouted about dry-cleaning, humiliation and irresponsible households. Dudley dropped to his knees and began scooping pudding from the floor until Uncle Vernon kicked his hand away.

Dobby vanished with another crack. Harry remained in the doorway. Uncle Vernon turned.

The rage upon his face stripped away every trace of the smiling businessman who had welcomed the Masons into his home.

"You."

Harry stepped backwards.

"I didn't do it."

Mrs Mason continued shrieking behind them. Mr Mason announced that Vernon would never receive so much as a drill bit from his company and shepherded his wife towards the front door.

"A house-elf did it," Harry said.

Vernon's face darkened.

"A what?"

Harry realised the explanation had not helped. The front door slammed.

For several seconds, silence spread through Number Four. Aunt Petunia stood amid the ruins of the pudding with cream on both hands. Dudley crouched beside the fallen sponge, chewing furtively. Uncle Vernon stared at Harry as if nothing else remained in the house.

Then he crossed the hall and seized the front of Harry's shirt.

"I warned you."

"It wasn't me."

"I told you exactly what would happen."

"And I stayed upstairs."

"Do not lie to me!"

"I'm not."

Vernon dragged him towards the staircase. Harry caught the banister to keep his feet beneath him. Aunt Petunia made no attempt to intervene. Her eyes remained fixed upon the violet icing smeared across the floor, as though the destruction of the pudding were more comprehensible than what her husband might do next.

"You ungrateful little freak," Vernon hissed. "We give you a room. We put food on your plate. We tolerate that filthy bird, and this is how you repay us?"

Harry tightened his grip on the banister.

"You barely feed me."

Vernon stopped. His fingers closed more tightly around Harry's shirt until the collar pressed against his throat.

"What did you say?"

Harry should have lowered his eyes. Before Hogwarts, he would have done so. He would have apologised and let Vernon explain how fortunate Harry was, because that was how one survived Number Four: allow the Dursleys to build their version of the world and never point towards the cracks.

But Hermione had asked whether he ate enough in nearly every letter. Draconia had called the Dursleys astonishingly incompetent within minutes of learning they existed.

Harry knew now that the rules of this house were not laws of nature. There were people beyond it who would hear Vernon's account and recognise it as a lie.

"You heard me."

Vernon shoved him up the remaining stairs and through the bedroom door. Harry struck the edge of his desk. An ink bottle toppled, pouring black across the wood and onto the carpet.

Hedwig cried out and battered her wings against the cage. Vernon slammed the door. The key turned in the lock.

Harry stood with one hand braced against the desk, listening as his uncle walked away. His heart hammered, but beneath the fear ran something unexpectedly steady.

They had written. Hermione and Draconia had not forgotten him. Dobby had held the proof in both hands. A heavy metallic sound struck the letterbox below.

Vernon muttered and crossed the hall. Harry moved to the window.

A barn owl sat upon the garden gate. It gave a single hoot before launching back into the dark. Uncle Vernon tore open the official envelope downstairs.

Harry knew what it was before Vernon began to read. Dobby had performed magic inside a Muggle home, and the Ministry had attributed it to the only registered underage wizard living there.

The house remained quiet for almost a minute. Then Harry heard Vernon laugh. It was not the booming, rehearsed laugh he had used for Mr Mason. It was smaller and far more dangerous.

Heavy footsteps returned up the stairs.

The lock turned.

Uncle Vernon entered holding the letter in one fist. The violet Ministry seal had been torn through the centre.

"You didn't tell us you aren't allowed to use magic outside school."

Harry looked at the parchment. "No."

"And if you do it again, they expel you."

Harry said nothing. A slow smile spread beneath Vernon's moustache. For the first time since Harry had returned from Hogwarts, his uncle no longer looked frightened of him.

The knowledge transformed him.

"All those tricks," Vernon said softly. "All those threats. And you can't do a thing."

"I haven't threatened anyone."

"Don't get clever."

"I wasn't aware accuracy counted."

Vernon crossed the room and pulled Hedwig's cage away from the window. She struck at his hand through the bars.

"You'll learn."

"She needs to hunt."

"She needs nothing."

"She'll get ill if you keep her locked up."

"Then perhaps you should have thought of that before ruining my dinner."

"It wasn't—"

Vernon wheeled around. Harry fell silent. His uncle carried the cage to the farthest corner of the room and lowered it heavily onto the floor.

"No magic," he repeated. "No school. No owls. We'll see how special you are without any of it."

He left. The door locked again. Harry waited until the footsteps descended before sitting on the floor beside Hedwig's cage.

"I'm sorry."

She pressed her head between the bars until her feathers brushed his fingers. Dobby had wanted to prevent Harry from returning to Hogwarts. Instead, he had handed Uncle Vernon the means to imprison him.

- - - - - - -

The bars appeared the following morning.

Uncle Vernon fitted them across Harry's window himself. He worked in the heat with grim satisfaction, drilling each bracket into the brick while Harry watched from inside. Metal filings gathered along the outer sill. When the final bar was secured, Vernon gripped it with both hands and pulled.

Nothing moved.

"There."

The window opened only a few inches behind the metal. Hedwig paced inside her cage, striking the wire with her wings whenever the drill sounded.

Vernon removed the bedroom door's outer handle and installed a cat-flap near the floor. At breakfast, a plastic bowl of cold tinned soup slid through, followed by one slice of bread. The bowl struck the skirting board and spilled part of its contents onto the carpet.

"No school," Vernon said from the landing. "No friends. No birds coming and going. You're staying here."

Harry sat on the bed. Vernon waited, perhaps expecting him to plead. Harry did not.

The most important part of Dobby's plan had failed. Harry knew the letters existed. Somewhere outside Number Four, Hermione was watching her owls return, and Draconia was counting the Fridays. They might not know what had happened yet, but they had not abandoned him.

Vernon could lock the door without changing that. When Harry offered no response, his uncle crouched beside the cat-flap.

"Nobody knows you're here."

Harry looked at him.

"My friends do."

"Those freaks haven't come looking, have they?"

"Give them time."

Vernon's face tightened. He slammed the flap shut.

Only after his footsteps retreated did Harry cross to Hedwig's cage. The new lock had no visible keyhole; Vernon had wound a short length of chain twice around the door and fixed it with a padlock too heavy for a hairpin.

Harry pushed the bread between the bars. Hedwig refused it until he tore off a piece for himself.

The room grew hotter as the day advanced. With the window barely open, no breeze reached the bed. Harry dampened an old flannel in the small bathroom adjoining Dudley's former room and draped it across the top of Hedwig's cage. He poured half his drinking water into her bowl.

Then he searched for a way out.

The bedroom door had no handle on his side, though the square metal spindle remained. Harry attempted to turn it with the legs of Dudley's broken toy soldiers and achieved nothing beyond bending one of them in half. The hinges faced the landing. The bars were fixed outside the brickwork. His wand remained inside the locked cupboard below, along with his broom and school trunk.

He could shout, but the nearest neighbours would telephone Aunt Petunia rather than enter the house, and Uncle Vernon would make certain they saw nothing. He could break the window, although broken glass would not solve the bars.

He could argue it was accidental magic. That thought lasted less than a minute. The Ministry had already warned him once. A second incident might truly prevent him returning to Hogwarts, which was exactly what Dobby wanted.

By the afternoon, Harry had exhausted every practical option. He drew Draconia's compass from beneath his mattress and held it flat in his palm.

He thought of the front door. The needle swung down and to the left.

"Helpful."

He thought of the cupboard containing his wand. The needle shifted slightly.

"Hogwarts."

It spun.

"Hermione."

Nothing.

"Draconia."

The needle circled without settling.

Harry closed his hand around the brass case.

Dobby's bundle of stolen letters remained vivid in his mind. He had seen Hermione's handwriting. He had seen Draconia's paper birds. However effectively Dobby had isolated him, he had also given Harry something the Dursleys had spent years denying him: proof that someone would notice his absence.

Hermione would build a theory. Draconia would refuse to wait for permission. Eventually, they would compare what they knew. Harry hid the compass beneath his mattress, lay back against the pillow and stared at the ceiling.

For the first time, his hope did not depend upon an adult happening to recognise what the Dursleys were doing. It depended upon two girls who had followed him towards a troll, through the third-floor corridor and beneath a trapdoor despite possessing enough sense to know better.

- - - - - - -

Hermione Granger had constructed a table. It occupied two pages of a notebook normally reserved for summer revision. Dates ran down the left side. Across the top, written in blue ink and underlined twice, were four headings:

METHOD SENT

DELIVERY RESULT

HARRY'S RESPONSE

POSSIBLE EXPLANATION

The first two rows were complete. Her first letter had reached Harry on the Tuesday after term ended. His reply arrived the following afternoon, containing an unsatisfactory definition of properly fine and a joke about moving staircases.

Her second letter had also been delivered. Harry's response arrived by Hedwig, assuring Hermione that he ate every day and admitting that Dudley stole from his plate.

Hermione had underlined that sentence. After that, the pattern broke.

The owl carrying her third letter returned without approaching Little Whinging. The fourth vanished for two days before reappearing outside the magical post office with its feathers raised and Hermione's parchment still attached. A larger school owl accepted her fifth letter, flew south and returned before sunset.

In the final column, Hermione had written:

Weather?

Incorrect address?

Anti-owl wards?

Deliberate interference?

The first two possibilities had been crossed out.

An ordinary Muggle letter had been returned beneath a label stating that no Harry Potter lived at Number Four, although Hermione had copied the address directly from the school records. She had checked it six times.

Her telephone call had been more alarming.

A man answered on the fourth ring.

"Number Four, Privet Drive?"

"Yes."

"My name is Hermione Granger. May I speak to Harry, please?"

Silence followed.

"Who?"

"Harry Potter."

"There is no Harry Potter here."

Hermione looked down at Harry's address.

"Is this Mr Dursley?"

The receiver struck its cradle. When she called again, nobody answered.

Hermione sat at the desk in her bedroom, staring at the table. Harry's early replies lay beside it. She had arranged them in order, and the change was unmistakable—not in what Harry said, but in what he avoided. He joked about his uncle. He deflected questions about food. He ignored every request for more detail about how he was being treated.

Then he disappeared. Her mother appeared at the open door carrying two mugs of tea.

"Still nothing?"

Hermione shook her head. Mrs Granger placed one mug beside the notebook and looked over the table. Unlike some adults, she did not smile at Hermione's lists or describe them as excessive. She read every row.

"Your father checked the address again," she said. "It exists."

"I know."

"And the man who answered?"

"His uncle. It had to be."

"He may have misunderstood."

"He heard Harry's full name."

Mrs Granger pulled out the other chair.

"Perhaps the family has gone away and someone else is watching the house."

"Then why did he claim Harry didn't live there?"

"I don't know."

"Neither do I."

Hermione hated those words. She preferred ignorance which could be corrected: a fact hidden in a book, a spell she had not yet learned or an answer requiring a better question. This ignorance sat at the end of every route she tried. Owls would not approach. Muggle post returned. The telephone produced a lie.

Mrs Granger touched the first of Harry's letters.

"Could Harry have decided not to answer?"

"No."

Hermione's certainty emerged before the question had finished. People forgot promises during holidays. They became busy, misplaced letters or assumed there would be time tomorrow. Harry forgot instructions, library books and occasionally that meals occurred at regular hours.

He did not forget that people were waiting for him. More importantly, he knew what his silence would mean. Hermione had spent a year noticing when he vanished, and Harry had spent a year pretending not to be grateful for it.

"He would send Hedwig without a letter if he couldn't write," Hermione said. "We arranged it on the train. One hoot meant he was fine. Two meant he needed help."

"And she hasn't come?"

"No."

Mrs Granger glanced towards the window.

"What about your other friend?"

Hermione had already pulled a fresh sheet of parchment towards her.

"Draconia will know whether Hedwig reached the Manor."

She and Draconia had exchanged letters throughout the first weeks of summer, although neither mentioned this arrangement when writing to Harry. Their correspondence had begun with an argument about revision and expanded into books, Hogwarts, magical history and whether Harry's claim to be fine possessed any meaning whatsoever.

Draconia thought it did not. Hermione found it irritating when she was correct.

She dipped her quill.

Draconia,

Have you received anything from Harry since the seventh? My last three owls returned without delivering their letters. A Muggle letter also came back, and his uncle denied that Harry lived at the address when I telephoned.

Something is preventing contact.

This is not Harry forgetting.

Hermione.

Mrs Granger read it over her shoulder.

"What happens if her owl cannot reach him either?"

"Then we know the problem is at Harry's end."

"What will we do?"

Hermione folded the parchment.

"Go there."

The answer was obvious. Mrs Granger did not object. That frightened Hermione more than if she had.

She took the letter to the magical post office herself. The witch behind the counter selected a broad-winged brown owl accustomed to heavily warded homes, and Hermione watched it disappear eastward.

The owl returned less than an hour later.

It carried no reply on separate parchment. Draconia had written directly beneath Hermione's message.

Granger,

I have received two letters from him. The last arrived on the fourth. Since then, I have sent seven. Four returned. Three vanished.

Come to the Leaky Cauldron tomorrow at ten. Bring your parents and every returned letter.

Do not tell them not to worry. They would be correct to.

Draconia.

The dates aligned. Harry's letters stopped during the same week Draconia's house-elf began behaving strangely. She looked at her mother.

"We were right."

Mrs Granger read Draconia's reply.

Hermione gathered the letters from her desk and arranged them into a single bundle.

"What are you doing?"

"Preparing the evidence."

"Hermione, if you believe Harry is being held somewhere—"

"I know exactly where he is."

Hermione tied the bundle more tightly than necessary.

"The problem is reaching him."

- - - - - - -

Draconia spread every undelivered letter across her bedroom floor.

Four had returned unopened. Three had simply vanished after leaving the Manor. Hermione's returned letters lay among them, sent ahead by the post-office owl. Beside the undelivered parchment sat the two replies Draconia had received from Harry during the first weeks of summer.

She had removed those from the locked drawer beside her bed.

The first contained jokes about Privet Drive and her compass. The second ended with four words written more carefully than the rest:

I would like to visit.

Draconia touched the sentence with one finger. She had shown that line to her mother. She had not shown it to anyone else.

The letters written after Harry's silence had changed gradually. She could trace the progression through the way she addressed him.

Potter, Granger's owl arrived on time. Yours has not.

Then:

Have your relatives prevented you from writing? Send Hedwig with a blank page if necessary.

Later:

This has stopped being amusing.

The most recent began with his first name.

Harry, if you receive this, answer in any way you can, I miss you.

None had reached him. Draconia sat among the parchment with her knees drawn close, anger and fear occupying the same place beneath her ribs.

She had spent the first week telling herself there was an ordinary explanation. The Dursleys disliked magic. Harry might have lost access to Hedwig. He might not know how to address a letter through Muggle post.

She had spent the second week considering less ordinary explanations. Wards. Ministry interference. An injury Harry had decided not to mention because he possessed the infuriating belief that concealing pain prevented other people from worrying.

By the third week, she had stopped pretending Harry's silence was voluntary. He had promised to write.

More importantly, he had looked directly at her when he made the promise. Harry sometimes evaded questions and frequently underestimated danger, but he did not make careless promises to people he trusted.

Draconia knew what it had cost him to trust them at all. Her bedroom door opened. Narcissa entered without waiting for an answer.

She wore pale blue summer robes, and her hair had been pinned loosely away from her face. Her gaze travelled from Draconia to the parchment covering the carpet.

"Miss Granger replied?"

Draconia handed her Hermione's letter. Narcissa read it standing beside the door. No visible alarm crossed her face, but she returned to the first line when she reached the end.

"You told me Mr Potter had not answered," she said. "You did not tell me that your owls were returning."

"I thought the wards surrounding his house might reject them."

"Ordinary Muggle homes do not possess wards capable of repelling magical post."

"I know that now."

"You knew it then."

Draconia looked away. Narcissa picked up one of the returned envelopes. The seal remained intact, but a thin cut ran along its upper edge. She turned it towards the light.

"Why did you not come to me sooner?"

"I wanted to solve it."

"Alone?"

"Hermione was working on it too."

"Without either of you telling the other."

"We have told each other now."

"That is not a defence of the original strategy."

Draconia folded her arms.

Narcissa examined the envelope again. A faint residue shimmered across the parchment where the owl's magic had been interrupted. It was almost invisible unless the light struck at precisely the correct angle.

"What is that?"

Narcissa did not answer immediately. She selected another returned letter, then one of Hermione's, comparing their edges.

"The same magic touched both."

"Can you identify it?"

"Yes."

Something in her mother's voice made Draconia sit straighter.

"What is it?"

"House-elf magic."

The room seemed to cool. Draconia looked at the letters surrounding them.

"One of ours?"

Narcissa's expression remained still.

That was confirmation enough.

"Why would they want to stop Harry's post?"

"I don't know."

"They belongs to us."

"They are bound to this family," Narcissa corrected quietly.

"So someone here ordered It."

"No."

Draconia stared at her.

"You cannot know that."

"I know how your father gives orders. If Lucius wished to prevent you from corresponding with Harry Potter, he would instruct the Manor wards to refuse Harry's owl. He would not send a house-elf across the country to interfere with every form of post entering a Muggle home."

"He might if he wanted me not to know."

"He would also know that house-elf magic leaves a trace I could recognise."

Draconia looked down at the shimmering edge of the envelope. The reasoning was sound. She found no comfort in it.

"If Father did not order it, given that one of our elves has been missing for three days, they've acted alone."

"Yes."

"House-elves aren't supposed to be able to do that."

"They can disobey. The consequences usually ensure they do not."

Draconia thought of Dobby flinching whenever Lucius raised his voice. She thought of his thin arms, his habit of punishing himself for mistakes and the tray shattering across the drawing-room floor at the mention of Hogwarts.

Guilt threaded itself through her fear.

"What frightened them enough to accept the consequences?"

Narcissa's gaze shifted towards the closed bedroom door. Beyond it lay the eastern wing of the Manor. Several rooms had remained locked since Ministry officials announced their searches. Lucius had spent days inside his study sorting family possessions and arranging for certain objects to be moved.

Draconia had known better than to ask what they were. Now Harry's silence had begun during the same week. She rose, gathering the letters into her arms.

"Where are you going?"

"To Father."

Narcissa stood.

"No."

Draconia stopped.

Her mother's voice remained quiet, but it allowed no argument.

"He knows something."

"Possibly."

"If its Dobby's doing, then it is his elf."

"Dobby serves the family."

"Father is the head of it."

"And accusing Lucius Malfoy of abducting Harry Potter through stolen correspondence will make him considerably less inclined to explain himself."

"I wasn't going to phrase it like that."

"You were going to phrase it more offensively."

Draconia opened her mouth. Narcissa raised one eyebrow. Draconia closed it again. Her mother took half the letters from her arms and straightened their uneven edges.

"You will come with me," Narcissa said. "You will allow me to speak first, and you will not accuse your father of attempting to isolate your friend until we understand what Dobby heard."

"What makes you think Dobby heard anything?"

"He began interfering with Harry's post immediately after your father removed several concealed objects from the eastern wing."

Draconia went still.

"You noticed that too."

"I notice everything which leaves my home."

"Did you know what the objects were?"

"No. Lucius has spent twelve years convincing himself that ignorance shared within a marriage is protection."

Narcissa's expression suggested she did not agree.

"Could one of them be dangerous?"

"Everything your father conceals from the Ministry is dangerous in one fashion or another. That does not mean he understands what Dobby believes is about to happen."

Draconia thought of Harry's second letter and his unguarded admission that he wanted to visit. She thought of Hermione in Oxford, assembling evidence because worry alone was not useful enough for her. She thought of Dobby somewhere beyond the Manor's wards and Harry trapped behind a silence none of them had chosen.

"If Father caused this—"

"We will discover what he caused before deciding what to do about it."

"And Harry?"

Narcissa looked at the name written across the uppermost envelope.

"Miss Granger is meeting us tomorrow."

"You'll allow her into the Manor?"

"I said we would meet her."

"Where?"

"The Leaky Cauldron. Her parents are Muggles, and asking them to enter a heavily warded ancestral home before they know whether we can be trusted would be discourteous."

Draconia stared at her mother.

Narcissa's mouth curved faintly. "I am capable of recognising discourtesy even when your father practises it enthusiastically."

"And after we meet them?"

"First, we speak to Lucius. Then we determine precisely what Dobby has done."

Narcissa opened the bedroom door.

Draconia did not follow immediately.

"What if Father refuses to help?"

Her mother paused. It was a question Draconia would not have asked several months earlier. The Malfoy family did not question Lucius's decisions outside closed rooms. Even inside them, disagreement was carefully dressed as advice.

Narcissa looked back at her.

"Your father is many things," she said. "But he is not foolish enough to dismiss thirteen intercepted letters, a missing house-elf and Harry Potter vanishing behind a Muggle door."

"That was not my question."

"No," Narcissa agreed.

For an instant, something cold and immovable appeared beneath her elegance.

"If Lucius refuses, we will proceed without him."

Draconia tightened her hold upon Harry's letters.

"Then what?"

Narcissa's gaze settled upon the four carefully written words at the bottom of the page.

I would like to visit.

"Then," she said, "we find your friend."
