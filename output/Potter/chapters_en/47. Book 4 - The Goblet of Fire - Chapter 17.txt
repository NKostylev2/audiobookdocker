17

Nine days proved long enough for fear to acquire a timetable. Harry woke before sunrise, usually at three seventeen, while the other boys were still asleep behind their curtains. For a while he lay without moving, waiting for the dormitory to become familiar again: Neville's uneven breathing, Dean's foot protruding from his blankets, the Firebolt mounted beside Harry's trunk. Beneath his pillow, his fingers found the cool edge of the silver mirror. Once he could name everything around him, he got up and dressed.

He ate because Hermione watched the level of porridge in his bowl and Draconia had begun carrying emergency biscuits in three separate pockets, each apparently reserved for a different degree of stubbornness. He passed beneath revealing charms on his way to lessons, stated his name to portraits that had known him for four years, and trained until Madam Pomfrey threatened to confiscate his wand if he appeared at the hospital-wing door with another injury. Every route across the grounds showed him the maze. It had swallowed the Quidditch pitch completely. Black-green walls rose above the stands. A gap glimpsed on the way to Herbology had vanished by the time he came back, yet the hedges never stirred while he watched them. Ministry warders circled the boundary in pairs, their wand-lights moving like pale insects along the leaves as evening settled over the grounds.

At night, Harry crossed another square from the calendar beside his bed. Eight days left, then seven; as the numbers diminished, the preparations multiplied. There was always another fitting, another test or another meeting in Dumbledore's office, and fear became easier to carry when he only had to take it as far as the next appointment. By the time seven days remained, Harry possessed an emergency Portkey, a Ministry tracking charm, two antidote kits, a silver whistle which summoned an Auror and a bracelet Professor Flitwick claimed could anchor him to Hogwarts against unauthorised transport. The bracelet had been tested upon a chair, three sacks of flour and Professor Flitwick himself. The chair remained in Hogwarts, the flour appeared in Belgium, and Professor Flitwick returned from the edge of Hogsmeade with one eyebrow missing. He declared the results encouraging with such conviction that Harry almost believed him.

Harry wore the bracelet anyway. "How encouraging?" he asked, trying to keep his wrist still.

Flitwick stood upon Dumbledore's desk so that he could reach Harry's wrist. Tiny runes moved around the narrow silver band, slipping beneath one another as the professor guided them with his wand. Each adjustment sent a brief, cold tingle through Harry's fingers.

"Considerably more than they were before breakfast."

"That's not a number."

"Magic rarely respects percentages, Mr Potter."

"Hermione does."

Hermione raised her head from the original Triwizard contract spread across the neighbouring table. "Seventy-eight per cent under controlled conditions," she said, before Flitwick could offer anything more reassuring.

Harry examined the band. "And uncontrolled?"

Flitwick tightened the final clasp with particular care. "We are doing our best not to create those."

It sounded so much like one of Dumbledore's answers that Harry glanced towards him before he could help himself. The Headmaster occupied the opposite side of the office with Amelia Bones, Bill Weasley and two Unspeakables whose names they had not offered. Sirius leaned against the mantel, arms folded. Remus sat near the window with a duplicate of the contract across his knees. Beyond the glass, the maze cut a dark square from the sunlit grounds. Lucius and Narcissa stood behind Draconia, close enough to answer any question and far enough away for her to pretend she needed neither of them. Cedric had brought both parents; his mother had not let him out of arm's reach since they arrived.

Amos Diggory read the same paragraph for the fourth time, lips moving beneath his moustache. His wife kept one hand upon Cedric's shoulder. The contract occupied eighteen sheets of treated dragon hide joined by golden thread. Most of its language predated modern English, and every translation led to further footnotes, exceptions and warnings left by people who had been dead for centuries. Hermione had developed a personal dislike of several of them. She turned the next sheet with the care of someone who would have preferred to tear it in half.

"Here," she said, tapping the sixth sheet with one finger. The conversations around the office stopped. "The completion condition. 'The Trial of Seeking shall endure until the consecrated prize accepts the living magic of a bound champion.'"

"Accepts," Sirius repeated. "Not touches."

"I'm afraid that distinction won't help," Bill said. He drew his wand and passed its tip over the old runes, gathering red light around three words.

"The Cup was made into the consecrated prize when the Goblet selected the champions. Its magic recognises them through the same binding. Contact closes the circuit."

"A spell?" Cedric asked.

"Skin, wand or magic cast directly upon it."

Harry turned towards Dumbledore. "So I can't Summon it."

"Not without completing the task," Dumbledore said.

"Can someone else touch it?"

"Anyone may touch the object. Only a champion can complete the contract."

Harry glanced at Sirius. His godfather had spent two days trying to find a completion condition which did not require a champion to make contact, and the discarded translations lay in a thick pile beside Remus's chair. Sirius met his eyes before looking back at Bill.

"Then tell us what you have actually found in the thing."

"The Cup has passed every examination we can perform through the seal," Flitwick said. "There is no transport enchantment upon it. Bill and I have also examined the contract and the authenticated task instructions. Their dormant magic matches the original Tournament pattern."

"Dormant," Mrs Diggory repeated.

"The full circuit only draws power when the task begins," Bill said. "We can examine its terms and test its safeguards. We cannot reproduce the entire final task without calling upon the champions' bindings."

Harry remembered Amelia telling him that Cedric had collapsed when the Department tried to sever the Cup from the contract. Cedric had heard that explanation now; his mother's hand tightened upon his shoulder.

"Harry's vision gave us a date and a warning," Bill continued. "It did not show a Portkey being made, and our examinations have not found one. We are preparing for forced transport because it is one way to get him beyond Hogwarts. It is not the only one."

"Then an Auror collects it first," Sirius said. "Carries it out to Diggory, the task ends, and Harry never has to go near it."

Amelia shook her head, her finger resting on another passage. "Only a champion may enter the maze before the task is complete, unless someone has raised an emergency signal. Sending an Auror in deliberately would breach the neutrality clause."

"A clause already breached when someone entered Harry under a fourth school."

"The contract interpreted that as an accepted irregularity."

"Of course it did."

Sirius pushed away from the mantel, the heels of his hands leaving the edge of the stone. Every day nearer the task made him less patient with another explanation of why nothing could be done. He had stopped pretending the Ministry's caution sounded sensible to him.

"What happens if Harry enters and refuses to seek the Cup?"

No one answered at once. Harry found the relevant passage before Hermione could turn to it; someone had marked the words in black ink, and he had read them often enough to know where they were.

"'Each champion shall strive in good faith towards victory until victory is achieved.'"

"The binding judges intent," Remus said.

"It already has," Harry replied.

He remembered the pain tightening behind his ribs when he tried to withdraw after the Goblet chose him. It did not matter whether he wanted to compete, only whether he made the attempt. His hand drifted towards his chest before he caught it, and Sirius's eyes followed the movement.

"How much can it make you do?"

"Enough."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the one I have."

Sirius's jaw hardened, and Harry regretted the edge in his voice. None of this was his godfather's fault. Harry knew how hard he was trying; it was simply difficult to feel protected while sitting in another room full of adults discussing the danger he still had to enter. Before he could work out how to say that, Draconia turned a page.

"Trying your best doesn't mean ignoring a trap."

Amelia turned towards her, the light catching her monocle. "Explain."

"Harry has to attempt the task. It says nothing against caution, cooperation or retreating from a suspected trap." Draconia touched the edge of the contract. "If someone has tampered with the Cup, or the maze stops letting him call for help, waiting would be reasonable. We ought to make sure the contract agrees."

"The contract may not recognise reason," Hermione said.

"Then we provide it with a definition."

Draconia drew a clean sheet of parchment towards herself. "An addendum, signed by all the participating authorities. Suspicious magic, failed emergency equipment or evidence of interference. Any of those allows the champion to stop and wait for an official. Without being punished for it."

One Unspeakable spoke for the first time. "The contract is closed."

Draconia looked up from the parchment. "Then why are you here?"

Lucius lowered his gaze, concealing something dangerously close to pride. The Unspeakable's hood turned towards Amelia.

"Observation."

"Then observe someone doing something useful," Draconia replied.

"Draco," Narcissa murmured.

"I was polite."

"By your standards."

Hermione had already begun drafting the addendum when Bill moved beside her. "We can't alter the original, but an interpretive covenant might help. We would be agreeing on how the binding should apply its ambiguous terms."

"How likely?" Harry asked.

"More likely than the flour returning from Belgium."

Flitwick coughed. "The flour was never intended to return."

Harry examined the silver bracelet again, turning his wrist until the clasp caught the light. Sirius noticed. "We're not relying on one charm."

"We're relying upon twelve charms and a contract which considers kidnapping an irregularity."

"Thirteen," Hermione said without looking up. "The blood-keyed Portkey makes thirteen."

"Much better."

"Harry."

"I'm wearing them." Harry looked up, his voice losing its edge. "I am trying."

Sirius held his gaze for several seconds before he nodded. "I know."

The addendum took three hours. Madame Maxime signed for Beauxbatons. Karkaroff signed only after Viktor told him, in front of everyone, that he would do so or return to the ship alone. Dumbledore signed for Hogwarts. Bagman represented the Tournament administration, Amelia the Ministry and each champion their own intent. When Harry's quill touched the parchment, the words flashed gold and the Goblet's binding shifted around his ribs. He went still, waiting for the familiar pressure to become pain. It settled instead, acknowledging what he had signed without releasing him. Beside him, Hermione let out the breath she had been holding.

"It accepted the interpretation."

"It still expects an honest attempt," Bill warned. "But it recognises a safety pause now."

Cedric laid his quill beside Harry's. "Then we make one."

Sirius crossed to Harry's chair and crouched beside it. "We couldn't remove the contact condition," he said. "I wanted to. You know that."

Harry nodded. He had watched his godfather argue over every word until even Hermione ran out of alternatives.

"If something fails, you stop," Sirius continued. "No finishing the task to spare us trouble. Use the whistle. If that fails, use the Portkey. We will come for you."

Flitwick touched the bracelet. "If forced transport overcomes this, the counter-charm will try to impress Hogwarts upon whatever carries you. That may leave a return route even if it cannot hold you here."

"May," Sirius repeated.

"A final contingency," Flitwick said. "Not a reason to trust an object which tries to take him."

Harry looked at the narrow band. He would prefer it to do its first job.

"And if I can't signal?"

"We have watchers following all four tracking charms," Amelia said. "If a signal disappears, or an emergency charm fails its next check, that is sufficient authority to enter. The school and the Ministry have both signed it."

They tested the pause before leaving the office. Under Bill's supervision, Harry declared an equipment failure and turned away from the Cup's marked position on the model maze. For once, the binding allowed the movement without pain. Cedric repeated it, then Fleur and Viktor. Sirius remained beside Harry throughout, watching his breathing more closely than the parchment. When the last test ended, Harry realised he had lowered the hand he usually kept near his chest.

Amos Diggory's hand tightened on his son's shoulder. "You don't need to prove anything in there."

Cedric met his father's eyes with a surprise he did not quite manage to hide. Amos saw it, and something changed in his face. Harry wondered whether he had realised how often telling Cedric what he could achieve had sounded like telling him what he must.

"You never did," he added.

Cedric covered his father's hand with his own. Across the table, Sirius's attention settled upon Harry. Harry pretended to examine the contract until the moment passed.

Rita Skeeter chose that afternoon to publish an article entitled HARRY POTTER'S ARMY OF ANXIOUS GUARDIANS. The accompanying photograph showed Sirius leaving Dumbledore's office with one hand upon Harry's shoulder. Lucius walked on Harry's other side. Hermione and Draconia followed close behind, their expressions serious enough for Rita to describe them as rival young witches united by their determination to control the troubled champion. Harry read the first two paragraphs, decided they were quite enough, and folded the newspaper into a narrow strip. He pushed it under the wobbling leg of the library table until his ink bottle stopped rocking.

"Excellent," he said. "Useful journalism."

Hermione did not answer. She sat opposite him with three earlier articles arranged in chronological order. Green ink circled particular details, with arrows linking one clipping to another. Draconia leaned over her shoulder, one hand braced on the table to avoid disturbing the arrangement.

"She knew Sirius had arrived through the Floo before the Ministry announced it," she said.

"And that Harry called Crouch's field manual contradictory," Hermione replied.

Harry lifted his head. "I said that in the hospital wing."

"Behind privacy curtains."

"Privacy is a generous description. Madam Pomfrey hears people breathe incorrectly."

Hermione placed another clipping beside the first. "She knew about the argument after the Yule Ball."

Draconia's expression became carefully blank. "Half the school knew about that."

"Half the school knew we argued. Rita knew Harry said we could have asked him."

Harry lowered his voice. "Are we revisiting that?"

"No," both girls said.

"Comforting."

Hermione ignored him.

"She printed Sirius's private explanation for agreeing to the lake task. Harry heard him say it inside the medical tent, where there weren't any reporters. She knew Winky admitted delivering the gillyweed, and she described Moody's hospital bed when no reporter had even been allowed into that corridor." Hermione pressed a finger to the final clipping. "We keep finding things she shouldn't have been able to hear."

"Someone is speaking to her," Harry said.

"Someone small," Luna observed.

Harry turned to find Luna in the chair beside him, although he had not seen her arrive. She placed a book about carnivorous clouds on the table and leaned sideways to examine the folded newspaper supporting its corner.

"How small?" Hermione asked.

"Beetle-sized."

Draconia's eyes narrowed. The beetle again. Luna continued with the same mild interest she might have brought to a change in the weather.

"The bright green one I told you about, on the railing during the Second Task. It had markings around its eyes like spectacles. I did think it might be a Journalist Beetle, but Daddy says those were hunted to extinction after they began correcting headlines."

Hermione went very still, her finger resting on the clipping. Harry recognised the look: after weeks of collecting details, she had found somewhere to put them.

"The revealing charms," she said, gathering the articles together.

"Flitwick's charms produce a violet halo around concealed human transformation. Binns glowed because the spell mistook death for disguise."

"An Animagus is a transformation," Draconia said.

"Registered Animagi carry Ministry exemptions which identify them to the charm. An unregistered one—"

"Would glow," Harry finished.

Luna opened her book.

"The beetle near the hospital wing was purple."

Hermione stood so quickly that her chair fell backwards, its crash making three nearby readers jump. Madam Pince appeared from behind a shelf with the speed of a hunting bird.

"Out."

"Gladly," Draconia said.

Harry rescued the folded newspaper from beneath the table, which resumed wobbling indignantly as they left. Hermione was explaining the trap before they had reached the hospital corridor. They prepared it inside an empty classroom beside the ward. Hermione supplied parchment covered with false notes about Harry's final-task strategy, and Draconia added a sealed letter apparently written by Lucius. Harry's contribution was a loudly delivered argument with Sirius about refusing the anchor charm. They left the desk close enough to the window for an interested beetle to read.

"You're enjoying this," Hermione accused from behind the half-closed classroom door.

Harry raised his voice towards the empty corridor. "I don't know what you mean. I often reveal secret plans beside open windows."

"Less commentary."

Sirius stood out of sight near the wall, wand ready. His part required him to look furious and leave in a manner impossible to overlook, which proved well within his abilities.

"If you remove that bracelet," he said, loud enough to carry towards the hospital wing, "I will personally bind it to your arm."

"You'd have to catch me first."

"I caught Pettigrew after twelve years in prison."

"Remus helped."

"Betrayal," Sirius said.

He swept away around the corner. Harry entered the classroom and left the false notes upon the teacher's desk. Draconia waited behind a tapestry. Hermione crouched beneath an Invisibility Cloak with Luna, whose inclusion Hermione claimed was necessary because she had identified their quarry. Harry suspected Luna had simply refused to leave. They settled in to wait, with Harry on the windowsill and the others hidden from view. Sunlight crept across the floorboards while a clock beyond the wall marked five minutes, then ten. Harry watched dust turning in the light, resisting the temptation to look towards the desk. At twelve minutes, the revealing charm above the corridor door flickered, and a violet spark no larger than a fingernail passed beneath it. Harry kept still as a green beetle landed on the outer sill, close enough for him to see its legs find purchase on the stone.

Its shell was an unpleasant shade of green. Black markings surrounded the compound eyes in shapes remarkably like jewelled spectacles. It cleaned one foreleg, apparently satisfied that the room was empty, and flew towards the desk. The jar descended from what looked like empty air. Glass struck wood; the beetle shot upwards and hit the lid with a small, sharp click.

"Got you," Hermione whispered.

Draconia stepped out from behind the tapestry as Hermione and Luna emerged from the Cloak. Sirius returned through the door with Remus, Professor McGonagall and Amelia Bones behind him. At the sight of the gathering audience, the beetle became extremely still.

"That," McGonagall said, "is either the insect Miss Lovegood described or the most comprehensively witnessed beetle in Britain."

Hermione held the jar to the light. "Rita Skeeter is not listed in the Animagus Registry."

The insect struck the glass twice.

"Can she understand us?" Harry asked.

"Yes," McGonagall said.

Harry leaned closer to the glass. "Your article ruined a good newspaper."

The beetle's wings buzzed furiously.

"That upset her more than the prison risk," Draconia observed.

Amelia placed a legal evidence seal around the jar and touched it with her wand. Green light passed through the beetle; as the transformation broke, the jar's sides stretched into a cylinder of transparent magic. Rita Skeeter appeared seated on the desk inside it, with just enough room to move and nowhere to go. Her blonde curls had flattened on one side. The jewelled spectacles remained on her face, slightly crooked. She clutched a tiny green handbag against her chest. For once, no Quick-Quotes Quill hovered at her shoulder. Her eyes travelled from face to face, pausing longest on Amelia's wand.

"Surely," she said, "this is an overreaction to rigorous investigative reporting."

Hermione placed the collected articles against the barrier. "You entered private hospital rooms in animal form."

"I observed matters of public interest."

"You listened to children while they slept," Draconia said.

Rita's attention shifted. "Miss Malfoy. Your father understands the value of a controlled narrative."

"My father understands blackmail. He made sure I could recognise it."

Rita recovered a careful smile. "And what do you believe you have?"

Hermione produced a book from her bag. The title read The Animagus Registry and Associated Criminal Penalties: Revised Edition. Coloured markers protruded from forty pages, and several passages had been underlined twice. Rita's smile weakened as she took in the title.

"Preparation," Harry said. "It's how she shows affection."

"Harry," Hermione warned.

"See?"

Remus concealed a smile behind one hand. Amelia waited for Rita to look at her before addressing her through the barrier.

"Unregistered transformation carries a mandatory fine, supervised registration and a possible sentence of up to six months. Use of that transformation to bypass Ministry security, enter a protected school and access restricted medical areas increases the possible charges considerably."

"I am a member of the press."

"Beetles possess no press exemption."

"A regrettable oversight in the legislation."

"You may raise it with your solicitor. Registration remains compulsory."

Rita's fingers tightened around the clasp of her handbag. It was the first movement she had not appeared to choose.

Amelia continued. "The intrusion charges require statements from Hogwarts and the people whose privacy you violated. Your cooperation may be entered in mitigation. I will not suppress evidence, Miss Skeeter, and no agreement in this room will leave you unregistered."

Rita's attention returned to Harry. All year she had made his face serve her paragraphs: the pitiable orphan, the dangerous attention-seeker, the romantic prize caught between girls whose friendship she treated as a spectacle. Without her quill, she looked less certain of which expression to give him. He could almost see her weighing what each person in the room might cost her.

"What do you want?" she asked.

Hermione answered at once. "For twelve months, you print nothing about Harry, Draconia, Luna or me without written permission."

"Impossible."

"Then Madam Bones can continue."

"The public has a right—"

"The public does not have a right to Harry's nightmares."

Even the rustle of Remus's sleeve stopped. Rita's gaze sharpened, and Harry felt Draconia shift beside him.

"That line would make an excellent—"

Draconia struck the barrier with her wand, making the transparent wall flash white. Rita recoiled against the far side of the desk, her handbag clutched to her chest.

"Finish that sentence," Draconia said, very quietly, "and you can give your next interview through Azkaban glass."

Harry touched Draconia's wrist and left his hand there until she looked at him. Her wand lowered a fraction. Hermione placed a second sheet on the desk, careful to keep her own hand outside the barrier.

"You also hand over every note you obtained by entering Hogwarts unlawfully. And you agree not to use your Animagus form to enter the school, a private home or a protected medical space without explicit permission." Hermione glanced at Amelia, who nodded. "In return, we decline to pursue a private complaint, and Madam Bones records your cooperation."

"Decline?" Rita repeated.

"Continued compliance preserves that decision."

"That is extortion."

Lucius entered before Hermione could answer. Harry had not heard him approach, although he suspected Narcissa, appearing in the doorway behind him, had spent some time listening.

"No," he said. "Extortion would require them to seek unlawful gain. This is a conditional settlement concerning whether injured parties pursue additional remedies after the state fulfils its own obligations."

Rita stared at him. Lucius adjusted one cuff and handed Hermione a contract. "I have prepared a cleaner version."

Hermione read the first page, then turned to the second with increasing interest.

"There are nineteen clauses."

"Twenty-three. Four are folded into definitions."

Draconia looked offended. "You drafted this without me?"

"You were baiting a journalist."

"I can do two things."

"You inherited that confidence from your mother."

Narcissa lifted one eyebrow. Rita took the contract, smoothing the first page against the desk. By the third paragraph, the confidence had gone out of the hand holding it.

"One interview," she said at last.

Hermione frowned. "What?"

"At a future date of your choosing. Exclusive. My questions, your conditions. In exchange, the restriction lasts eighteen months rather than twelve."

Harry turned to Hermione. Her eyes had sharpened at the word exclusive, and he recognised the look with which she set a useful fact aside. Whatever she had seen in the offer, Rita had not.

"The interview must be printed accurately," Hermione said. "No invented dialogue, no changes of meaning and no accompanying speculation presented as fact."

Rita looked physically pained. "Fourteen months."

"Eighteen."

"Sixteen."

"Eighteen, and you correct the article claiming Luna was romantically involved with Harry."

Luna tilted her head. "I didn't mind."

"I did," Harry said, and found everyone looking at him.

"Not because it was Luna," he added quickly. "Because she made being friends sound like something we ought to be embarrassed about."

Luna smiled. Hermione's expression gentled. Rita sighed.

"Eighteen."

She signed with a sharp flourish, and the contract flashed green. Amelia then placed a separate registration notice beside it. Rita's gaze travelled down the form as though each line contained a fresh personal insult.

"Before you leave," Amelia said.

Rita signed that too, pressing hard enough to leave an impression on the sheet beneath it. When Amelia dissolved the barrier, she stood, restored her hair with one furious movement of her wand and collected her handbag. At the door, she stopped beside Harry.

"You understand this will not make the press kinder."

Harry thought of headlines arriving before he understood the events they claimed to explain.

"No," he said. "But it makes you quieter."

Rita's smile contained the smallest trace of respect. Then she left with Amelia beside her and the evidence jar floating at their heels. Hermione watched the empty doorway, her fingers resting on their copy of the agreement. "One interview," she murmured.

"Planning ahead?" Harry asked.

"Always."

She folded the agreement carefully and put it in her bag. Harry did not ask what future she had imagined for Rita's single honest interview; for the moment, it was enough that she was imagining one.

Four days before the task, the real Moody conducted his first lesson since being removed from the trunk. Madam Pomfrey called it supervised rehabilitation; Moody called it proof that nobody competent remained in charge of his recovery. At six that morning, Harry followed Sirius across the grounds towards a circle of temporary walls erected between the greenhouses and the Forest. Apparently, rehabilitation did not require a classroom. Mist lay ankle-deep over the grass, soaking the hems of their robes as they walked. Behind them, the castle rose in grey layers, its upper windows catching the morning light while the lower courtyards remained in shadow. The maze made a dark mass against the sky. Moody waited beside the practice enclosure, tapping his staff impatiently against the ground.

Madam Pomfrey had replaced his temporary leg with a sturdier wooden one, though it lacked the old leg's hidden compartments and defensive enchantments. A black patch covered the empty eye socket. He stood with one hand around a staff and the other holding a steaming metal cup. Harry's body still recognised the outline before it recognised the man: the scarred face, the wooden leg, the staff striking the ground. His shoulders tightened, preparing for the magical eye to swivel towards him through solid walls. The black patch stayed still, and he had to remind himself why.

Moody noticed the hesitation. He shifted the staff into his other hand, leaving his wand arm visible, and waited without calling Harry forward. That small courtesy was so unlike Crouch's careful performance of roughness that Harry felt his shoulders loosen. He crossed the last stretch of grass in his own time. Remus occupied a folding chair nearby with blankets around his shoulders. The last full moon had been three nights earlier. Exhaustion still showed beneath his eyes, but he held the clipboard Hermione had given him with an air of irritating preparedness. Hermione and Draconia stood inside the first line of walls. Harry looked around for Cedric and found no sign of him.

"Where is he?" Harry asked.

"Sleeping," Moody replied.

"Why?"

"Because this is your test."

Harry surveyed the enclosure. Its twelve-foot canvas walls had been charmed to resist ordinary cutting spells, and no entrance was visible from where he stood. He could feel the hum of further enchantments through the damp air. Someone had put a great deal of effort into making this unpleasant.

"I already have a test."

"Then fail this one and sleep through that."

Sirius took Moody's cup and drank from it. Moody struck his hand with the staff. "Poisoned."

Sirius spat the mouthful into the grass and held the cup away from him.

"You said the old flask was poisoned whenever I touched it."

"Eventually I'd be right."

Remus made a mark on the clipboard.

"What are you writing?" Sirius asked, looking at it suspiciously.

"Excellent vigilance from both instructors."

Harry smiled before he could help it. He had grown used to starting the day with a tight stomach and a list of protections to check; smiling before breakfast felt almost extravagant. Moody pointed towards the blank canvas wall.

"Your objective is the silver bell in the centre. Ring it. You have fifteen minutes."

"And the walls?"

"Present."

"The entrance?"

"A useful concept."

"Are Hermione and Draco helping me?"

"Define helping," Hermione called from somewhere beyond the canvas.

Draconia added, "Poorly."

Moody removed a heavy watch from his pocket and pressed its crown. "Begin."

Harry did not cast immediately. The false Moody had made every hesitation feel like a failure of courage; the real one watched without comment while he walked the perimeter and examined the ground. Near one corner, a trail of small, bare footprints disturbed the mist. They were too narrow to belong to either girl. Harry followed them for three paces, then stopped with his wand raised.

"House-elf?" he called.

Nothing answered. The footprints continued straight through the canvas, and when Harry touched the wall with his wand, the tip vanished into it. An illusion. He stepped towards the gap just as a curse struck his shoulder from behind, sending numbness down his arm. Harry dropped, rolled through the wet grass and threw up a Shield Charm as he turned towards the nearest tree. Through the flash of red light, he saw Sirius half-concealed behind the trunk, protected by a shield of his own.

"You found the obvious entrance," Sirius said.

"There are footprints."

"Exactly."

Harry threw a Stunner before Sirius finished the word. His godfather's image dissolved where the curse touched it, leaving nothing behind the tree. Remus made another mark on the clipboard. Harry was beginning to dislike that clipboard.

"You're all very supportive," Harry said.

Moody checked the watch. "Fourteen minutes."

Harry tested the wall two feet to the left and found it solid. Keeping his shield raised, he stepped through the false section into darkness. The canvas had become stone corridors beneath a starless sky, cutting off the castle and the sound of birds outside. Moist air pressed against his face. Three passages sloped away from him, and his attempt at a Four-Point Spell proved no help at all: the wand pointed north, spun east, then settled on what appeared to be Sirius's location.

"Traitor," Harry told it.

Hermione's laugh travelled along the right-hand passage, followed by a sharp cry and the sound of something falling. Harry ran two steps before the cry came again, with precisely the same catch of breath. He stopped, listened to the sequence repeat a third time, and turned left. A recording. He would have preferred to realise that before he started running.

"Heartless," Hermione's real voice observed from behind a wall.

"You make a very repetitive victim."

"I was working with limited time."

The floor opened beneath him. Harry caught the edge with both hands, his fingers scraping over rough stone as his feet swung into darkness. The smell of wet earth rose from below. His numbed shoulder gave first; before his grip could slip entirely, he drove the tip of his wand into the side of the pit.

"Spongify!"

Stone softened around the wand, gripping it firmly enough for Harry to swing against the wall. His shoe found a narrow ledge. He climbed awkwardly, one shoulder protesting, and hauled himself over the edge to find Draconia standing above him with her wand pointed at his forehead.

"Dead," she said.

Harry's gaze dropped to her shoes. Dust lay undisturbed beneath them. "Not you."

He cast through the illusion, scattering it. The spell continued into the darkness and struck something solid, prompting a furious oath from the real Draconia. That, at least, sounded convincing.

"Alive," Harry corrected.

Green light came at him from the next corridor. Harry threw himself sideways. The curse clipped his sleeve and turned it into a swarm of moths.

"That was one of my better shirts."

"It was one of Sirius's shirts," Draconia called.

"That's how I know."

He swept dust down the passage with his wand, revealing the outline of an invisible barrier. Draconia waited beyond it, her pale hair bright in the darkness and her wand already raised. Three passages offered convenient ways around the wall. Harry distrusted all of them and aimed at the barrier itself.

"Reducto."

Runes flashed across the air as the barrier resisted the spell. Draconia used the light to aim; Harry dropped beneath her curse and changed tactics.

"Finite."

The barrier flickered, holding long enough to reveal a seam. Harry drove his shoulder against it. Pain flared down his numbed arm, and the magic tore with the sound of ripping cloth. He stumbled through, collided with Draconia and brought them both to the ground. Her wand rolled out of reach; Harry caught her wrists before she could draw the second one from her sleeve.

For a moment, neither moved. Draconia's hair had come loose against the dark floor, and her breath touched Harry's cheek. The surprise in her face made him forget the corridors, the bell and the ache in his shoulder. He was suddenly aware of how close they were, of her pulse beneath his fingers. Then Draconia drove her knee into his stomach.

Harry rolled away, coughing, while she recovered her wand.

"Dead," she said again, sounding rather less composed.

"Unnecessarily."

"You hesitated."

"I was winded."

"Before that."

Harry's face warmed.

"Eight minutes!" Moody shouted from somewhere impossibly distant.

Draconia held out her hand. Harry took it, allowing her to pull him upright. She kept hold of his fingers while he caught his breath.

"The bell is north-west," she whispered.

"Are you allowed to tell me?"

"You defeated me."

"You kneed me."

"I allowed you to survive."

"Generous."

Her fingers tightened around his once before she disappeared through the wall. Harry followed the direction she had given him, though he distrusted every turn along it. As the passage narrowed, thorny branches pushed through cracks in the stone. He severed one that curled around his ankle before it reached his skin; another recoiled from his wand, then crept towards him again as soon as he turned away. At the final junction, the silver bell waited beneath a shaft of false moonlight. There was no visible guard, no curse and nothing blocking the short walk towards it. Harry stopped six feet away. It was altogether too inviting.

"Of course," he murmured.

He turned to find Sirius behind him, solid enough to stir the dust beneath his boots. His hair was tied at the nape, his sleeves rolled above the forearms, and his wand rested loosely beside one thigh. Harry recognised the way he held it from their earlier duels and adjusted his own grip.

"Bell's there," Sirius said.

"I noticed."

"Seven minutes left."

"Moody said eight a moment ago."

"I'm better with numbers."

Harry kept his wand at his side. "Are you going to stop me?"

"Do you think I am?"

"Yes."

Sirius smiled and cast without another warning. His first curse came low; Harry blocked it, angling the shield to send the spell into the branches. As the thorns recoiled, Sirius advanced, transfiguring two fallen pieces into black ropes. Harry cut the first, but the second wound around his wrist. He let Sirius's pull carry him forward, slid beneath the next spell and came up close enough to make his godfather's longer reach awkward. Their wands locked, throwing sparks between them.

"Crouch teach you that?" Sirius asked.

"No." Harry stamped on his foot. "That was Dudley."

Sirius swore and broke away, and Harry laughed before he could contain it. His godfather heard and grinned. A sweep of his wand turned the floor to ice, sending Harry sliding past him towards the bell. Harry windmilled, caught his balance and struck the silver dome with his bound wrist. Its clear note carried through the corridors as the walls vanished and morning returned around them. Harry stood in the middle of the grass with one sleeve made of moths, a black rope around his wrist and Sirius's curse halfway towards him. As the enclosure dissolved, the curse veered off course and struck Moody's metal cup. It exploded with a crack loud enough to scatter birds from the nearest tree. Moody stared at the empty handle in his hand.

"Poisoned," Sirius reminded him.

Remus checked the watch. "Completed in eight minutes and forty-one seconds."

Harry bent with both hands on his knees. Hermione came out from behind the remaining canvas, dabbing at a narrow cut on her chin from one of her own obstacles. Draconia followed, brushing dust from her robes with unnecessary concentration. She did not look at Harry, which made it harder for him to stop looking at her.

"Result?" Harry asked.

Moody examined him.

"You entered through a marked ambush, followed false distress for two paces, fell into the first pit and got distracted by an opponent."

Draconia turned towards the Forest.

"I rang the bell."

"You adapted," Moody said. "That's different."

From him, it sounded enough like praise for Harry to straighten. His shoulder still ached, and one sleeve had wings, but he had reached the bell.

"Will the maze be worse?"

"Yes."

"Thank you for preserving morale."

"Morale is what people carry when facts are insufficient."

Sirius unwound the rope from Harry's wrist, checking the skin beneath it. "You did well."

The simple words mattered more than Harry had expected. He met Sirius's gaze and saw his godfather's smile soften. With the morning sun turning the damp grass gold and laughter still warm in Harry's chest, Harry could almost see the boy who stood beside James in the photographs. Harry wondered what his father would have said after the duel. Something competitive, probably. James would have claimed Harry's footwork came from him and the stamping from Lily. The thought hurt, but he could hold it alongside the pleasure of the duel without losing the whole morning to it. That was new.

After breakfast, Sirius suggested a walk around the lake, claiming they needed to discuss security somewhere the castle portraits could not report their conversation to six departments. Harry suspected his godfather simply disliked leaving before they had spent a little time alone. He was grateful for the excuse. The day had warmed. Sunlight lay broad upon the water, broken by ripples where the giant squid moved near the surface. Students occupied the grass in scattered groups, revising beneath trees or abandoning revision whenever professors disappeared from sight. Sirius carried his shoes. Harry kept his on because the lakeshore contained stones, mud and at least one creature Hagrid had described as harmless.

"You trust the bracelet?" Sirius asked.

"I trust Flitwick."

"Not the same question."

"No."

Sirius nodded, accepting the distinction. They walked for a while without speaking, their footsteps keeping pace along the shore.

"The emergency Portkey is stronger," he said. "It's keyed to your blood. Press your thumb to the centre and say my name, and it takes you to me."

"Even from the maze?"

"In theory."

"Everyone's favourite kind of magic."

"The practical version required Dumbledore to set fire to the Department of Magical Transport."

"That sounds useful too."

Sirius nudged a flat stone loose from the path, picked it up and sent it skipping four times across the lake. "There's something else."

Harry waited as his godfather glanced towards his collar.

"You still wear the key."

Harry reached beneath his shirt and drew it out. The brass had darkened where his thumb rubbed over it whenever he needed something familiar to hold. He had caught himself doing it during lessons often enough that the movement no longer surprised him. Fine silver lines followed the shaft, answering the wards at Hawthorn Cottage. The tooth which opened the upper lock still leaned faintly to the left.

"It has never tried to murder me," Harry said. "That puts it above most of my possessions."

Sirius took the key between two fingers without removing the cord from Harry's neck. A faint warmth stirred across the brass as its magic recognised them both.

"I gave you this before I knew whether Dumbledore would let you spend a full summer with me," he said. "Before your school records named Hawthorn as your residence. I meant what I told you then."

"That the door opens after we argue?"

"Especially then." Sirius released the key. "Your room is waiting, Harry. That has nothing to do with how the Tournament ends."

Harry gazed across the water. Sunlight made it impossible to see the castle reflected clearly. The towers broke and reformed with every small wave.

"I still have to go back to Privet Drive."

The words came automatically. Dumbledore had explained the blood protection and its conditions often enough for Harry to hear the argument without anyone making it: Lily's sacrifice, the relative who shared her blood, another stretch of summer beneath that roof. His grip tightened on the key.

"For part of the summer," Sirius said. "Not all of it."

Harry looked at him. "Still three weeks?"

"Dumbledore won't agree to less."

Harry looked down at the key.

"I wanted three hours. Remus suggested we negotiate before I threatened to turn your aunt's house into a memorial crater."

"What would it memorialise?"

"My restraint."

Harry laughed, though the familiar number still sat heavily in his stomach. Three weeks, and afterwards Hawthorn Cottage: his godfather leaving tea unfinished beside the fire, Remus reading beneath the window, the bedroom that would remain his even when he was away. He could open the front door without knocking. He already had the key, had worn it for most of a year, yet he ran his thumb over its crooked tooth as though he needed to check that it was still there.

"What if you change your mind?"

Sirius stopped walking. Harry wished he could take the question back. It belonged to a younger version of him, the child who kept his few possessions inside a cupboard because anything visible could be removed. That child had never quite gone away, however much Harry had grown around him. Sirius stepped in front of him, leaving room at the edge of the path but making it clear that they were not walking past this.

"I spent twelve years staying alive partly because I believed there was a chance I would see you again."

Harry lowered his eyes. "That isn't an answer."

"It is, but I'll make it clearer." Sirius waited until Harry raised his eyes. "Nothing you do in that maze will make the key stop working. Lose, freeze, run away from the Cup—I want you back. Come out furious or frightened or carried by an entire team of healers. Spend your first week home refusing to speak to me because I've become unbearable." His voice stayed steady. "It's still your home."

Harry tried to imagine arriving at Hawthorn in a temper and being allowed to stay that way. His chest tightened. Sirius saw it. "You will return," he said.

"You just said—"

"I know what I said. I'm allowed to hold two thoughts. Remus assures me it's common."

Harry smiled without looking away and closed his hand around the key. "It's the only magic I own that doesn't try to decide where I go."

Sirius considered the wards threaded through the brass. "It does become unpleasant if someone else attempts to use it."

"That's different. It still lets me choose whether to open the door."

He tucked the key beneath his shirt again. It rested warmly against his chest, promising no rescue and pulling him nowhere. The door at Hawthorn would open when he chose to come home.

Sirius began walking again. "Have you chosen a colour for the walls?"

"Green is fine."

"You have mistaken my willingness to house you for permission to commit decorative crimes."

"It's my room."

"That argument remains annoyingly effective."

They made it another four paces before Harry stopped. For once, he did not wait for Sirius to guess what he needed. Harry stepped into him, and Sirius's arms closed around him at once. His godfather smelled of grass, smoke and the bitter coffee he drank when afraid to sleep. Harry held on, his forehead against Sirius's shoulder, while a hand settled at the back of his head. He did not need to explain the hug, and Sirius did not ask. The giant squid rolled in the water. Voices travelled from the castle lawn. Somewhere behind them, a Ravenclaw shouted that an essay had blown into the lake. The world continued being ordinary around Harry.

He had never understood how much happiness could hurt before he possessed enough to lose.

Hermione found him that evening in the Charms classroom. Harry had taken apart both antidote kits and arranged their contents across a desk. Small bottles stood in three precise rows. Silver labels identified poisons, venoms, confusion draughts and compounds intended to slow the heart. A folded card listed symptoms in Flitwick's handwriting. Harry was trying to memorise them, matching each symptom to the bottle he would need. He had reached blue lips, delayed blinking and sudden affection for authority figures when Hermione pulled out the chair beside him.

"That one may be difficult to distinguish from Percy," he said.

She did not smile, and Harry lowered the card. Her hair was escaping the plait she wore during revision, with loose strands catching against the collar of her robes. Ink marked two fingers and one cheek. She looked tired in a way sleep did not seem to be helping, as though she had brought every book she had read that day into the room with her.

"What happened?" he asked.

"Nothing."

"All right. What happened after all this nothing?"

The corner of her mouth moved. Harry felt absurdly pleased that Sirius's line had worked twice. Hermione reached for the nearest antidote bottle and turned it between her fingers, studying a label she had already checked that afternoon.

"You shouldn't carry both kits in the same pocket."

"I wasn't planning to."

"If something damages one side of your robes—"

"I can still reach the other."

"And the bezoar should be somewhere reachable without opening a case."

Harry drew the small stone from his left sleeve. "Here."

"Your wand arm."

He produced a second from his right pocket. "And here."

Hermione stared at the two stones.

"Snape gave me three," Harry explained.

"Why?"

"He said my survival has developed an offensive dependence upon improvisation."

"That sounds like him."

"The third is in my boot."

She put down the bottle, lining it up with the others. For several seconds, they listened to the low hum of stored charms. Evening light spread across the windows and the empty desks beyond them. Beyond them, the mountains stood dark against a sky fading through peach and violet.

"You've thought of everything," Hermione said.

"You thought of most of it."

"That doesn't make me feel better."

Harry leaned back, giving her room to put down the answer as well as the bottle. "What would?"

"Knowing what's going to happen."

"That seems unlikely."

"I know."

The answer wavered, and Hermione lowered her gaze before Harry could be certain he had heard it. He looked at the bottles, the labels and the folded chart, and understood her lists a little differently. To most people, Hermione's preparation looked like confidence: she gathered books, made notes and broke impossible questions into smaller ones she could answer. Sitting beside her now, he could see how frightened she was of reaching the last question and finding nothing there.

After the Chamber, she had wanted every detail she had missed while Petrified. During the search for Sirius, she had read until her eyes reddened, reaching for another book whenever the story became too frightening. Now she had a schedule for the Tournament, covered with amendments and folded soft at the corners. Harry wondered how often she had held it simply because she needed something to hold.

"You don't have to solve this," he said.

Hermione lifted her head. "Someone does."

"Dumbledore, maybe."

"He was fooled by Crouch."

"Then Moody."

"He spent nine months in a trunk."

"Not his best year."

"Harry."

"I'm not asking you to stop helping."

"It sounds like you are."

He reached carefully between the bottles and took her ink-stained hand. Hermione went still, her fingers resting loosely in his before they closed around them.

"I'm asking you not to blame yourself for whatever happens. It won't mean you missed something you should have found."

"What if I do?"

"Then you'll be wrong."

"You can't know that."

"I can. You miss things constantly."

Her eyebrows rose. "You have a very peculiar idea of comfort."

"You spent weeks catching ordinary beetles before we caught Rita. You didn't notice me hiding the Hogsmeade permission form. And in third year you carried so many books around that you barely noticed what using the Time-Turner was doing to you."

"This is getting less comforting."

Harry tightened his fingers around hers. "And you're still the person I want beside me when everything goes wrong."

Hermione stopped objecting. The remaining light caught in her eyes.

"You won't be able to see me inside the maze," she said.

"That isn't what beside me means."

Hermione's thumb moved across his knuckles, and Harry felt the familiar uncertainty between them become something harder to ignore. It had followed them through unfinished conversations all year: on a staircase after the ball, beside the lake when he returned with Sirius and Gabrielle, across tables when one of them looked away too late. Whenever he tried to name it, he thought of Draconia too, and stopped. He had hoped that would spare them pain. He was beginning to see that it had only left them guessing.

"What did Sirius want to talk about this morning?" she asked.

Harry touched the cord beneath his shirt with his free hand. "Summer. I can spend most of it at Hawthorn."

"Harry." Her whole face brightened, and she squeezed his hand.

"After three weeks at the Dursleys."

Her smile faltered, but the happiness did not quite leave her face.

"Three weeks is still too long."

"Sirius agrees."

"Could the blood protection be renewed through brief visits?"

"Hermione."

"Or perhaps it depends on intent rather than how long you stay. If your aunt still recognises the house as—"

"Hermione."

She stopped, and Harry smiled. "I knew you'd do that."

"Someone should."

"You've already started a list."

She had. A folded corner protruded from beneath the antidote chart, and she made an unsuccessful attempt to cover it with her wrist. Harry drew the parchment out and read the heading.

ALTERNATIVES TO RESIDENTIAL BLOOD-WARD RENEWAL

Seven numbered possibilities followed.

"I wrote it at lunch," she said.

"I only told you about summer just now."

"I began before."

"Why?"

Hermione's cheeks coloured. "Because I thought you should be able to go home to him."

Harry looked down at the list. There were careful footnotes, references to three different books, and a smear where her hand had moved across the ink before it dried. She had been trying to find him a way home before she knew Sirius had managed to shorten the wait. For a moment, he could not work out what to say.

"Thank you."

She shrugged as though the work had cost nothing. Harry let go of her hand and reached up to rub the ink from her cheek with his thumb. Hermione drew a quick breath. He should have lowered his hand then, but she leaned slightly into it, and he found himself close enough to see the darker flecks around her pupils. Her gaze dropped towards his mouth. His heart stumbled as she moved closer.

The classroom door opened. Professor Flitwick entered carrying a box of silver instruments and stopped on the threshold. Harry and Hermione moved apart so quickly that two antidote bottles overturned, rolling towards the edge of the desk. Flitwick's gaze travelled from one to the other.

"I can return."

"No," Hermione said.

"Yes," Harry said at the same time.

In the silence that followed, Flitwick took a careful step backwards. "I shall inspect the anchoring bracelet tomorrow."

The door closed. Harry stared at it while Hermione caught the bottles and set them upright.

"We should finish packing these," she said.

"Probably."

Neither moved.

"After the task," Harry said at last.

Hermione met his eyes. He could feel a joke forming, something that would let them both look away, and made himself leave it unsaid.

"We need to talk. Properly. About the ball. And everything after it."

Hermione glanced down at their hands on the desk, no longer touching, then back at him. "Yes," she said.

The questions between them remained, but she reached for his hand again before they began packing. For now, knowing they would talk was enough.

Draconia avoided being alone with him the next day. She sat beside Pansy at breakfast, worked with Daphne in Potions and never once corrected the angle of Harry's knife. Her revised maze notes arrived through Hermione, and she left training before he finished packing his equipment. At first Harry was offended. By midnight, after several attempts to catch her had come to nothing, he was worried. Harry found her in the empty classroom where they first tested the Malfoy alarm coins. Rain traced silver lines down the tall windows, hiding the grounds beyond them. A single lamp burned beside the teacher's desk, where Draconia stood holding a new coin beneath her wand. She was so intent on it that she did not look up when Harry stopped in the doorway.

Her satchel lay open beside a scatter of failed versions: silver discs cracked through their centres, warped by heat or stained black around the Malfoy crest. A faint smell of scorched metal hung over the desk.

"You missed one," Harry said.

She spun, the spell leaving her wand before she recognised him. Harry raised his shield in time for pale light to break against it and scatter across the room. One of the ruined coins rattled off the desk.

"Hello would have been nice."

Draconia lowered her wand, breathing rather quickly. "So would knocking."

"The door was open."

"Since when have you needed anything more than that?"

"It's how doors work."

"Not Slytherin doors."

Harry approached the desk, keeping clear of her wand. "What are those?"

"Unsuccessful."

"I noticed."

She placed the newest coin beside the others with excessive care. It looked intact until Harry leaned closer and saw a thin fracture crossing the serpent on its face.

"The Ministry tracking charm stops working outside Britain," she said. "The bracelet responds to transport magic, but a ritual could displace you without triggering it. And you have to speak to activate the emergency Portkey. Someone only has to silence you."

"You've been talking to Moody."

"I have been reading."

"More dangerous."

Draconia ignored that.

"The family alarms cross most wards. Blood connects them, so distance matters very little. I'm trying to make one you can activate without having to crush it."

Harry picked up a ruined coin and felt heat lingering in the metal. "Why without crushing?"

"You might not be able to use your hands."

The answer came too quickly, as though she had been turning that possibility over for hours. Harry put the coin down.

"Draco."

"I have nearly solved it."

"Look at me."

"That will not improve the runes."

"It might."

"Your understanding of runes is offensive."

"Still not looking."

She made an impatient sound and faced him. Her eyes were red, though her lashes were dry and she had composed her face before turning. Someone who knew her less well might have blamed the late hour or the smoke from the coins. Harry knew how much effort she put into hiding tears. He reached tentatively for her wand, and she drew it back.

"I am working."

"You're burning holes through money."

"We can afford it."

"That may be the most Malfoy thing you've ever said."

"Go to bed, Harry."

He stopped at the sound of his first name. When she was angry with him, it was usually Potter. This sounded different.

"No."

Draconia straightened, drawing her composure around her again. "You need sleep."

"So do you."

"I'm not the one entering the maze."

"I know."

"Do you?"

The question came out sharply enough to make them both pause. Harry waited while she turned away and braced her hands against the desk, her shoulders rigid beneath her robes.

"In the first task, we had to watch a dragon drive you into the ground," she said. "Everyone stayed in the stands. No one could reach you, because apparently disqualification mattered more."

Rain struck the windows harder.

"Then the lake. The hour was over, Harry. A whole hour, and then another minute, and people were still discussing scores while Hermione and I stared at the water."

Harry moved closer. Draconia's fingers curled against the wood.

"Now they've built walls around you." Her voice broke, and she pressed her lips together before trying again. "Every time, we have to watch you disappear."

Harry had no answer that would make her stop being right. He had survived the dragon and returned from the lake, but Crouch had needed him to survive both. Getting this far did not mean the plan had failed. Somewhere beyond all their preparations, someone still expected the final task to put Harry within reach.

"I'll have the bracelet," he said.

"Seventy-eight per cent."

"The Portkey."

"You have to be able to speak."

"The Aurors."

"Waiting outside walls they aren't allowed to enter."

"You've found a problem with everything."

"Yes."

"That isn't the same as nothing working."

"It still doesn't mean you're safe."

She swept one hand across the desk, sending coins clattering over the edge. At the sound, her face crumpled. Draconia covered her mouth, her shoulders folding inwards as though she could still keep the rest of it hidden. Then she sank into the chair behind her and began to cry. Harry had seen Hermione cry in anger, exhaustion and relief. He had watched Sirius grieve without tears and Lucius struggle to keep them hidden. Draconia made almost no sound. Even now, with her hands pressed to her face, she was trying to contain it. Harry knelt before her, careful not to touch her until she could see him.

"I don't know what to say."

"Then don't."

"I'm bad at that."

A broken laugh escaped behind her hand. Harry waited until she lowered it, then opened his arms a little. "Can I?"

Draconia met his gaze and nodded. When Harry drew her forward, she came out of the chair and held him with sudden force, her arms locked around his shoulders. He settled onto the floor with her as she pressed her face against his neck. The fear she had kept busy with instructions, research and ruined silver shook through them both. Harry held her without telling her he would be fine. He was beginning to understand how much work that promise asked of someone who wanted to believe it.

Instead, he held the back of her head and said, "I'm frightened too."

Draconia's fingers tightened in his robes.

"You don't seem frightened."

"I make jokes at inappropriate times."

"You do that when you're happy too."

"It's a very versatile habit."

She breathed something between a laugh and a sob against his shoulder. Harry rested his cheek on her hair, feeling her grip loosen just enough for her to take a fuller breath.

"I don't want to go into the maze," he admitted. "I don't want to touch the Cup. And I don't want Voldemort waiting somewhere, knowing what happens next when I haven't got a clue."

The name made her flinch. She did not pull away.

"Then how are you so calm?"

"I'm not."

"You signed the contract."

"Because Hermione found a useful paragraph and you bullied an Unspeakable."

"I did not bully him."

"He may need time before agreeing."

Draconia finally lifted her head. Her face was damp, and she made no immediate attempt to compose it. Harry had never seen her look so young, or felt so trusted by the fact that she let him see.

"You shouldn't have to comfort me about the possibility of you dying," she said.

"I'm not planning to die."

"That's never stopped you getting close."

"It's very approachable."

"Harry."

"Sorry."

Neither let go. Draconia studied his face as her fingers shifted near his collar, finding the cord from which Sirius's key hung. She held it lightly, as though checking that he still had it.

"What if you don't come back?" she whispered.

Harry could not imagine what happened to any of them then, and he would not give her another promise he could not keep. There was something else he could say, if he could find the courage.

"There are things I haven't said."

Her breath caught.

"To you," Harry continued. "And Hermione."

Something painful crossed her face before she looked down. "I know."

"No, I mean—"

"I know what you mean."

Harry was not certain he knew what he meant himself. Draconia touched his cheek, and for a heartbeat he thought she might close the distance between them. He wanted her to. At the same time, he remembered Hermione in the Charms classroom, the way she had leaned towards him and their promise to talk after the task. He hesitated, and Draconia saw it. Her hand fell. As she eased back, Harry felt the cold air where she had been.

"After," she said.

Harry swallowed. "After."

Draconia wiped her face with both hands and took a moment before speaking again. Her voice was steadier, though Harry could still hear the effort behind it.

"Help me collect the coins."

They gathered the ruined silver together, their shoulders occasionally brushing as they reached beneath the desk. Near one leg, Harry found a coin that had not cracked. Its serpent crest remained whole, surrounded by a narrow circle of runes that became visible when his fingers touched the metal. The coin warmed. Every other piece of silver on the floor answered with a pulse of green light, and Draconia went still. Harry held it up.

"Was it meant to do that?"

She took the coin from him, angling it beneath the lamp to examine the runes. "No."

"Encouraging."

"It responded to you without blood."

"Less encouraging."

Draconia cast three diagnostic charms, watching each result closely. The coin stayed warm in her palm, its runes clear and unbroken.

"The original alarm worked because the Manor's household wards included you over the summer," she said slowly. "This one may be recognising the same connection."

Harry watched the green light move over the silver. The Malfoys' family magic had recognised him without waiting for anyone to decide what to call him. He turned the coin gently in her palm.

"Can I use it?"

"Not inside the maze. Private communication with anyone outside is prohibited."

"Then after."

Draconia closed his fingers around the coin and left her hand over his for a moment. "After," she agreed.

Early the next morning, Draconia sent Hermione a note. There was no greeting, only four words written with unusual care.

I require your honesty.

Hermione found her in the Astronomy Tower shortly after six. The rain had passed, leaving the sky scrubbed pale and the stones dark with moisture. Far below, mist drifted over the grounds. The maze appeared through it in fragments—one black wall, then another, with empty whiteness between. Draconia stood against the parapet, her swollen eyes turned towards the grounds. Hermione closed the door quietly behind her.

"Was it Harry?"

Draconia raised her eyebrows. "You'll have to be more specific."

"Did he upset you?"

"No."

Hermione approached, studying her friend's face. "Did you upset him?"

"Not intentionally."

"Draco."

"I nearly kissed him."

Hermione stopped beside the nearest telescope, her hand coming to rest on its cold brass stand. Jealousy arrived before anything kinder could. She imagined Draconia close to Harry in some private room, Harry looking at her as he had looked at Hermione before Flitwick opened the door. The memory must have shown in her face, because Draconia turned fully towards her.

"You too."

It was not a question. Hermione folded her arms, trying to recover a little privacy. "Nearly."

"When?"

"That isn't relevant."

"It became relevant when you made me answer."

"Last night."

Draconia's expression went still. "Before or after midnight?"

"Before."

"Then you were first."

"We are not keeping score."

"I wanted to know when it happened."

Despite everything, Hermione laughed. They looked at one another, briefly startled by it, before Draconia turned back to the maze and curled her hand over the wet edge of the parapet.

"I didn't," she said.

"Neither did I."

"Did he stop you?"

"Professor Flitwick did."

Draconia stared at her. Hermione felt her face warm. "He came into the classroom."

"I assumed so. I was appreciating the image."

"You may stop."

"Not yet."

Hermione stood beside her. Morning brightened by slow degrees. The mist thinned near the Forest, exposing the tops of trees and the dark line of the task enclosure where Harry had trained with Sirius.

"He thought of you," Draconia said.

Hermione turned to her.

"Last night. When I almost—" Draconia stopped, then tried again. "He thought of you. I could see it."

Hermione wanted to be pleased, but Draconia's face made that difficult. "I thought of you too," she admitted.

"How romantic."

"You know what I mean."

"Unfortunately."

Their agreement after the Second Task had given them rules, and neither had intended to abandon the competition. But they had made those rules sitting safely in an alcove, without knowing they would both nearly kiss him in the same evening. Hermione had found it easier to promise fairness before she had to imagine Harry looking at Draconia that way. Beside her, Draconia pressed both palms against the stone.

"What if there is no after?"

Hermione's chest tightened. "There will be."

"That's what Harry says when he wants us to stop looking frightened."

"I know."

"Do you believe it?"

Hermione watched another wall of the maze emerge beneath the lifting fog. "Not every minute."

It was the first time she had said it aloud. Draconia moved closer until their shoulders touched.

"I keep thinking I can make it true if I find the right spell," Hermione continued. "If I read every law, check every charm and think of everything that could go wrong, he'll come back. Because I did enough."

"And if he does not?"

"Then I'll spend the rest of my life looking for the line I missed."

Draconia closed her eyes. "I keep making coins."

Hermione reached for her hand. Draconia looked down at their joined fingers, surprised, then turned her palm into Hermione's.

"We can't promise for him," Hermione said. "But we can decide what we do."

"Which is?"

"When he comes back, we don't use any of this to make him choose. He doesn't owe us an answer because we were frightened."

Draconia's fingers tightened around hers. "We don't even know that he wants either of us."

"I think he does."

"Which?"

Hermione looked at her. "That's the problem."

Draconia's mouth twisted, caught between a smile and something less bearable.

"Potter is exceptionally inconvenient."

"He always has been."

"When he returns," Draconia said, lingering carefully on the first word, "we stop pretending nothing nearly happened."

Hermione's courage faltered at the prospect. "Immediately?"

"No. He may be injured."

"Or exhausted."

"And we each speak for ourselves," Draconia continued. "No interference, no strategic timing, and no arranging to find him first."

Hermione stared at her. "You were already planning to reach him before breakfast."

"I thought it might help my chances."

"You're getting a head start."

"Then we need an amendment process."

Hermione nearly smiled. "You added the original amendment process without consulting anyone."

"And prevented the problem from recurring."

Hermione could almost see the train compartment again: their emergency list, Draconia adding terms, Harry objecting to half of them. They had argued so seriously then, with so little idea of how much they would come to mean to one another. She looked at Draconia and knew she was remembering too.

"Before summer ends," Hermione said.

Draconia considered the compromise, then nodded. "Agreed."

They stayed at the parapet as sunlight spread over Hogwarts, their hands still joined on the damp stone.

The families came to Hogwarts on the day of the task. Harry had seen Sirius and Remus so often that week that it had not occurred to him the school would make an occasion of the visit. He only learned about the tradition when Professor McGonagall collected the champions after breakfast and led them towards the small chamber beside the Great Hall. Fleur smiled as soon as she understood. Viktor's guarded expression loosened. Cedric looked unsurprised, which suggested he had at least been told what to expect. Harry slowed outside the door. He knew Sirius was nearby, knew there were people waiting for him, yet the words the champions' families had brought the Dursleys to mind before he could stop them. They would not come.

He did not want them to. The idea of Vernon walking through Hogwarts felt like dirt tracked across a clean floor. Petunia would stare at the moving portraits as though magic had been invented to offend her, and Dudley would eat until a serving dish defended itself. Even knowing he did not want them there, Harry felt the old unease of standing outside a room where other children's parents had come for them. He had done it often enough for his body to remember. McGonagall touched the door handle.

"Professor," Harry said, "I can wait outside."

Her expression softened a fraction. "You may," she said. "But I believe several people would object."

She opened the door to reveal Sirius standing nearest it, dressed in dark blue robes with his hair bearing the marks of an unsuccessful attempt to tame it. Remus waited beside him, one hand tucked into a worn coat pocket. Both were looking towards the doorway. Narcissa sat upon a sofa near the window while Mrs Granger unpacked enough food for a minor expedition. Lucius and Mr Granger stood over a model of the maze, engaged in an argument about whether electric alarms were more reliable than Sneakoscopes. Draconia and Hermione occupied the rug between them. Harry stopped just inside the room, his uncertainty disappearing beneath the sight of them all. Mrs Granger reached him first and hugged him with the same lack of warning she employed at King's Cross. He had just enough time to put his arms around her before she began inspecting him.

"You're too thin."

"Madam Pomfrey says I'm within range."

"Which range?"

"Alive."

"Harry."

Narcissa approached as Mrs Granger let him go and adjusted his collar. "Have you eaten?"

"Breakfast ended twelve minutes ago."

"That was not the question."

Harry turned towards Sirius, taking in the occupied sofa, the picnic and the model maze. "Did you organise this?"

"Dumbledore asked me whom you considered family. I gave him the list."

Whom he considered family. Sirius had known, without Harry having to explain which relatives might be persuaded to come or who possessed the right paperwork. Harry looked around the room again, his throat tightening at the sight of people who had simply wanted to be here.

"This seems excessive."

"We edited the list," Sirius said.

"Who was removed?"

"Aunt Marge."

"Cruel."

"To the furniture."

McGonagall left them together. Beyond the chamber, other reunions filled the Great Hall. Gabrielle ran into Fleur hard enough to make both stumble. Viktor's mother cupped his face between her hands while his father stood beside them, broad and silent. Amos Diggory had already begun telling Bagman that Cedric led the scoring if one interpreted the Second Task correctly. Inside the smaller chamber, nobody discussed scores. Mrs Granger gave Harry sandwiches while Narcissa added fruit to his plate. Remus asked about the final lesson schedule, only for Sirius to interrupt each answer with an increasingly implausible account of Harry defeating him during training. Harry found himself correcting the story with his mouth full.

"He transformed three walls," Sirius said.

"One barrier," Harry corrected.

"Disarmed Moody."

"Broke his cup."

"Then rescued a village."

"There was no village."

"Because he rescued it pre-emptively."

Mr Granger laughed. Across the room, Lucius regarded Sirius with faint interest. "Does accuracy become physically painful if maintained too long?"

"You'd know better than I would."

Narcissa intervened before the conversation could improve further, and shortly afterwards they set out to tour the grounds before lunch. The champions' families moved in separate groups beside the lake, escorted by teachers and watched from a distance by Ministry officials. Harry's party had both McGonagall and Snape as escorts. He suspected two teachers were needed to keep Sirius and Lucius from testing one another's defensive theories within range of passing students. The day shone brilliantly, and the breeze carried the scent of warm grass and water. Hogwarts rose above them with banners hanging from the towers, every coloured length snapping against a cloudless sky. For an hour, Harry let himself enjoy it. There was warm grass beneath his feet, sunlight on the water and no meeting he had to reach until lunch.

He walked between Sirius and Remus. Hermione and Draconia argued behind him about whether his left pocket contained too many potions. The Grangers stopped to ask Hagrid about the pumpkin patch. Further along the path, Narcissa fell into quiet conversation with Mrs Granger, while Lucius listened to Mr Granger explain how a burglar alarm identified broken glass. No one expected him to find words large enough for what the day meant. He could listen, walk and occasionally steal a biscuit from Draconia without explaining how grateful he was. Near the beech tree, Sirius threw a stone into the lake. It sank without bouncing, and Harry raised one eyebrow.

"Four the other day."

"Wind changed."

"There's hardly any wind."

"Sabotage."

Harry chose a flat stone and sent it over the surface. Five skips. Sirius looked betrayed.

"James taught you that."

"No."

"Lily?"

"Mrs Figg's garden pond."

For a moment, sadness crossed Sirius's face at another small part of Harry's life he had missed. Then he crouched to collect three more stones. "Best of five."

Harry won, although Sirius lodged a formal complaint about interference from the giant squid. Remus declined to investigate.

Cedric found Harry shortly before the early supper. They stood beneath the covered walkway overlooking the Quidditch pitch, where the maze had claimed every inch of grass. Its outer walls seemed to shift with the afternoon breeze, though Harry could never pick out a single leaf moving. He kept his eyes on the same corner until Cedric spoke.

"My father thinks I should take the Cup before you," Cedric said.

Harry leaned against a stone pillar. "Sirius thinks I should set it on fire."

"Can it burn?"

"Apparently the champions catch fire first."

"Right."

They watched two curse-breakers complete another circuit of the hedges, stopping at intervals to test the wards. Neither looked particularly satisfied when they moved on.

"If we both reach it," Cedric said, "we inspect it together."

"Agreed."

"No touching it while the other person's back is turned."

"You don't trust me?"

"I don't trust either of us when there's a chance to do something nobly stupid."

Harry considered objecting, then remembered Cedric sharing the first-task clue, helping with training and agreeing not to ignore screams inside the maze. "Fair."

"If the Cup shows foreign magic, we signal. If the equipment fails or the maze starts doing something the officials never approved, we stop."

"And if the contract starts punishing us?"

"Then something has gone wrong with the binding as well." Cedric glanced towards the watching Aurors. "They tested the pause. It worked. We try the Portkey and let the adults open a way in."

Harry turned towards him. Cedric had stopped watching the curse-breakers and was looking directly at him.

"If I reach it first, the equipment is working and the tests are clear, I'm taking it," he said.

"You should."

"If you reach it first, you do the same."

"Cedric—"

"Don't decide I deserve it because I'm older, or that Hogwarts needs a champion nobody's arguing about. And don't stand aside because you've decided everyone else matters more."

Harry returned his attention to the maze, giving himself a moment before answering. "Hermione helped with that speech."

"She provided notes."

"Draco?"

"Threats."

"Efficient."

Cedric held out his hand. "We compete honestly, and we help if the other's in danger. We inspect the Cup. If everything is working, whoever reaches it takes it. If it isn't, we wait."

Harry clasped his hand. "And if we reach it together?"

Cedric smiled faintly. "We'll argue about it there."

Neither released the handshake immediately.

"All four back," Cedric said.

Harry remembered black water, failing breath and Cedric coming back from the bank to help bring him ashore. Their agreement had meant more than the scores, then. He wanted it to mean the same thing now.

"All four," Harry agreed.

Cedric let go but remained beside the pillar. He watched the upper branches of the maze for a moment, then drew a breath as though choosing a different subject deliberately.

"Cho wants us to go somewhere after this," he said. "Somewhere without judges, photographers or my father explaining the scoring system to strangers."

"Does such a place exist?"

"She has promised to investigate."

Harry smiled, picturing it. "Sirius will want a rematch. Flying, at Hawthorn."

Cedric glanced at him. "Is that an invitation?"

"Probably. He'll pretend it was his idea."

"Your godfather spent twenty minutes explaining to Dad that dangerous enthusiasm is hereditary."

"That was him being friendly."

"I was afraid of that."

It felt good to talk about flying, even with the maze in front of them. Making a plan would not force the future to cooperate; Harry knew that. But refusing to imagine anything beyond tonight had begun to feel like giving up the summer before it arrived. Cedric turned back to him.

"No debts in there," he said. "If I help you, you don't owe me. Same if you help me. We come out friends or not at all."

"That last part sounds dramatic."

"Hermione edited the first version."

"Explains it."

Cedric laughed and pushed away from the pillar.

"See you at supper, Harry."

"See you there."

Harry watched him cross the sunlit walkway towards the castle. See you there was an easy promise for once. Supper was waiting just inside the doors.

Lucius stopped Harry outside the Great Hall after the others had gone ahead. Through the doors came the scrape of benches and the low conversation of students gathering for the early meal. Harry could hear Hermione asking someone to leave the seat beside her free. Sunlight from the high windows turned dust into gold between them.

"A word," Lucius said.

Harry waited while Lucius glanced at the Ministry officials stationed at either end of the corridor. When he spoke again, his voice was low enough for Harry alone.

"Mr Nott built his reputation upon understanding what other people would refuse to do," he said quietly. "He mistook predictability for principle."

"What does that mean?"

"He believes affection makes people controllable. A parent will obey to protect a child. A servant will betray one master to save another. A friend will enter danger because someone else is already inside it."

Harry remembered Crouch's voice in the corridor, and the certainty with which he had spoken.

He told me friendship would make you predictable.

"Sometimes he's right."

"Yes." Lucius did not pretend otherwise. "His error is believing a person possesses only one possible loyalty."

Lucius had chosen the words carefully. Harry studied his face, trying to work out why he had chosen this moment to say them.

"Are you expecting to be in the maze?"

"No."

"That sounded evasive."

"It was precise."

Lucius's gaze shifted towards the doors. Draconia's voice travelled faintly from beyond them.

"If you encounter anyone connected to his circle," he continued, "do not assume they will make the choice he expects."

"Including you?"

Lucius met his eyes and held them. Behind the doors, a burst of laughter rose above the conversation, startlingly distant.

"Especially me."

Before Harry could ask more, Lucius adjusted his collar in a gesture so like Narcissa's that Harry almost smiled. "Eat," he said, and entered the Hall.

Harry scarcely tasted the final meal, but he ate every bite, chewing carefully when his stomach threatened to object. Hermione sat on his right at the Gryffindor table. Draconia occupied the place on his left with Snape's permission and everyone else's considerable interest. Their parents and guardians sat at the staff table beneath the Tournament banners. Nobody tried to pretend the seating was ordinary, which made it easier for Harry to settle between them. As the evening drew on, warm light spread across the enchanted ceiling and long shadows reached over the flagstones. Each time a door opened, conversation faltered. At half past seven, Bagman rose.

"Champions, please report to the chamber adjoining the Hall."

A strange calm settled over Harry. The waiting was over, and he knew what he had to do next. He set down his cup as Hermione's hand found his beneath the table. Draconia took the other, and for several seconds they kept him seated between them.

"I do need those," he said.

"Debatable," Draconia replied.

Hermione did not smile. Harry looked from one girl to the other, squeezing their hands once. "After," he said.

The girls looked at one another across him, sharing something Harry could not read.

"After," Hermione agreed.

They released him, and he stood before he could lose the calm that had made standing possible. Inside the adjoining chamber, the champions changed into task robes behind four painted screens. Harry checked each protection in order, following the sequence he and Hermione had practised. Silver anchoring bracelet upon his left wrist. Emergency Portkey beneath his shirt. Sirius's key beside it. Antidote cases divided between his inner pockets. Bezoars in both sleeves and one boot. Wand holster secured. He tucked Draconia's coin against his chest. Its alarm would remain dormant inside the maze under the Tournament's rules, but he wanted it with him. She had said after, and he meant to have it when after came. When he emerged, Sirius waited with Remus near the outer doors. Narcissa embraced him first.

"Return," she whispered.

Her arms tightened for a moment before she let him go. Lucius placed a hand briefly against the back of Harry's neck, while Mr Granger shook his hand, appeared to reconsider, and drew him into a short, awkward hug. Mrs Granger kissed his forehead. Remus waited until Harry reached him to examine every buckle, taking the same care he might have taken before a school trip.

"You remember the emergency signal?"

"Red sparks."

"And if your wand is lost?"

"Silver whistle."

"If you cannot reach it?"

"Scream."

"Sensibly."

Sirius waited until the others stepped away. Rather than giving Harry another instruction, he touched the place beneath his robes where the house key rested. "Door's yours," he said.

Harry nodded. "I know."

Sirius pulled him into a hug, and Harry held on immediately, pressing his face into the familiar shoulder for a moment longer than he meant to. When they separated, his godfather's eyes shone. His smile remained steady.

"Make Moody proud."

"Lose a body part?"

"Make me proud instead."

"Break school rules?"

"You've already managed it."

Harry smiled.

"Come home," Sirius said.

Harry carried the words through the door, across the Entrance Hall and onto the grounds, repeating them silently until they settled beside the key against his chest. Hermione and Draconia caught him halfway to the pitch. The champions walked ahead with Bagman. Teachers, families and officials followed behind. Students poured from the Entrance Hall towards temporary stands built around the maze, their voices spreading across the summer evening. Hermione caught Harry's sleeve and drew him into the shadow of a stone arch. Draconia followed at once, positioning herself between him and the view from the path.

"You have ninety seconds," McGonagall warned from the path.

McGonagall continued walking, turning once to ensure no one else followed. For a few seconds, Hermione had no list. She watched Harry as though trying to memorise his face, while Draconia reached for his collar and straightened a fold that did not need straightening. Hermione brushed his fringe away from the scar, then left her hand hovering for a moment before she lowered it. The familiar gestures brought King's Cross back to Harry: three children facing an unknown school, with no idea of how much they would one day be afraid to lose.

"The bracelet?" Hermione asked.

Harry raised his wrist.

"Portkey?"

He touched his chest.

"Coin?" Draconia said.

"Dormant and there."

"Bezoars?"

"I could survive being poisoned by a brewery."

Neither laughed. Harry lowered his hands. "I'm coming back."

Draconia's face tightened. "Don't promise something you can't control."

"All right. I intend to come back."

"Better," Hermione said.

"And when I do—"

"We talk," she finished.

Harry turned to Draconia.

"All of us," she said, holding his gaze.

Harry's pulse quickened. Before he could ask what agreement they had reached, Hermione stepped forward and put her arms around him. He held her close, feeling her cheek press against his. When she spoke, her voice was too quiet for Draconia to hear.

"You do not have to win."

Harry closed his eyes. "I know."

When Hermione released him, Draconia took her place. She held him firmly, making no attempt to disguise the tremor in her arms. Harry rested his hand between her shoulder blades while she steadied her breathing.

"If there's an easy path," she murmured—

"It belongs to Crouch."

"If you hear either of us calling—"

"Verify."

"If the Cup looks safe—"

"Check anyway."

Draconia drew back far enough to look at him. "If you're frightened?"

Harry thought of the mirror beneath his pillow at three in the morning. "I admit it."

Her eyes closed briefly. "Good."

Hermione took his right hand and Draconia held his left. For a moment they stayed beneath the arch, listening to footsteps and voices pass along the path. Harry wanted to remain there. He could admit that now, without despising himself for it. He wanted to be safe with the people he loved; he wanted another ordinary day with them after this one. Their hands stayed warm in his until McGonagall's voice carried from the path.

"Time."

Hermione and Draconia released him slowly. Harry looked at them once more, then walked towards the maze.

The stands surrounded the Quidditch pitch on every side. Thousands of faces rose above walls of living green. Banners snapped overhead. Ministry lanterns floated at each entrance, their white flames bright against the gathering shadows beneath the hedges. Somewhere inside, the Cup was waiting. Harry could not see it, and he found that harder than he had expected. Bagman led the champions to four separate openings. Cedric stood at the northern entrance, Harry at the south. Fleur and Viktor waited east and west. Aurors examined each champion one final time, checking their equipment and confirming the identity charms on their skin. Professor Flitwick touched his wand to Harry's bracelet, and its runes shone blue against his wrist.

"Seventy-eight?" Harry asked.

"Eighty-four," Flitwick said.

"What changed?"

"I found the flour."

Harry smiled, grateful for the absurdity of the answer. From the judges' platform, Dumbledore raised his wand, and the crowd fell quiet by degrees until Harry could hear the rustle of leaves beside him. Harry found Sirius in the front row. Remus sat beside him. The Malfoys and Grangers occupied the same section, Hermione and Draconia between both families. Draconia's hand closed around Hermione's. Harry wondered whether either had noticed. Across the pitch, Cedric met his eyes and gave a small nod. Inspect together. Help if the other was in danger. Compete honestly.

The first horn sounded, and Cedric disappeared through the northern entrance. Harry waited out the two minutes between their starts, keeping his eyes on the gap in front of him. At the second horn, he stepped into the maze. Leaves closed behind him, and the cheering vanished as though a door had shut upon the world. He stood alone between walls too high to see over, with only a narrow strip of sky above them.

For one final second, Sirius's key remained warm against his chest.
