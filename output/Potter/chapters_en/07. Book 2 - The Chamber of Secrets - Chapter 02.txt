2

Freedom, Harry discovered, came with a bowl of soup and six people pretending not to watch him eat it.

They had returned to the private room at the Leaky Cauldron because nobody had been willing to discuss what happened next on the pavement outside number four. Tom had taken one look at Harry, one look at the adults accompanying him, and led them upstairs without asking questions.

Now Harry sat at the end of a long table with clean clothes, damp hair and a bowl of chicken soup steaming before him.

Mrs Granger had insisted upon examining the cut above his eyebrow. Narcissa had vanished through the Floo and returned with several bottles of potions, a bundle of clothing and an expression which discouraged questions. Between them, they had washed the blood from Harry's face, treated the bruising around his shoulder and established that nothing appeared to be broken.

Harry had tried to explain that he felt fine. This had not helped.

Hermione sat on his right. Draconia sat on his left. Neither had moved more than a few feet from him since leaving Privet Drive.

Across the table, Lucius Malfoy was studying a copy of the Daily Prophet without turning a page. Mr Granger was examining the grain of the table with unusual concentration, while Mrs Granger reorganised the medical supplies she had already organised twice.

Harry lifted another spoonful of soup. Six pairs of eyes immediately looked elsewhere.

"This is going to become unsettling quite quickly," he said.

Hermione turned back first. "What is?"

"Nothing."

"Harry."

"You're all watching me eat."

"We are not," Draconia said.

Harry looked at her. She had been staring directly at his spoon.

"I was watching the soup."

"Was it doing anything suspicious?"

"It might."

"It has carrots in it," Hermione said. "That is hardly suspicious."

"You have misplaced confidence in vegetables."

Harry smiled despite himself.

The expression felt strange upon his face, as though those muscles had not been required in some time. Hermione noticed. Her shoulders relaxed slightly.

Harry finished the spoonful. Mrs Granger reached towards the bread basket, hesitated, and then withdrew her hand.

Harry knew she wanted to place more food on his plate. He also knew she was trying not to make him feel watched. The effort somehow made him feel more watched than if she had simply handed him the bread.

He took a piece himself. Everyone relaxed. This was worse.

"We need to talk about the creature Harry saw," Lucius said, folding the newspaper at last.

Draconia straightened. Harry looked between her parents. Narcissa was seated beside her husband, one hand resting around an untouched cup of tea.

"The house-elf," Hermione said.

It was the first time she had been told exactly what had visited Harry's bedroom. Her expression had changed twice during the explanation: first to disbelief, then to the particular intensity which meant she had discovered a new subject about which she intended to learn everything.

"Dobby," Draconia said quietly.

"You're certain?" Hermione asked.

"He has served our family since before I was born."

"He wasn't wearing very much."

Draconia winced. "House-elves don't."

"He was wearing a pillowcase."

"Yes."

Hermione stared at her.

"We can discuss the peculiarities of wizarding domestic arrangements at another time," Lucius said.

"We certainly will," Hermione replied.

Mr Granger rubbed his mouth, concealing a smile. Lucius chose not to notice.

Lucius questioned Harry with the precision of someone conducting an official inquiry. Harry repeated the essentials: Dobby believed something terrible would happen at Hogwarts, had intercepted every letter sent after the beginning of July and had become frightened whenever Harry mentioned Draconia. He had refused—or had been unable—to explain why.

Narcissa explained that house-elves were bound against revealing certain family matters. Dobby might have overheard something at the Manor, she said, or sensed magic which the family had overlooked.

"The visitor," Draconia said.

Lucius's expression closed.

After a moment, he admitted that a man had brought an old magical object to the Manor and proposed using it for political retaliation. Lucius had refused, and the object had apparently left with its owner.

"Apparently?" Hermione repeated.

"I watched him carry it through the front door."

That satisfied the Grangers more than it satisfied Draconia. Harry saw the doubt in her face, but Lucius offered nothing further.

Hermione reached for her notebook.

Draconia intercepted her hand. "We are not making a list yet."

"The fact that you added 'yet' weakened your position," Harry observed.

Narcissa allowed them a moment before returning to the matter.

"We will notify Professor Dumbledore," she said.

Lucius turned towards her. "Cissa—"

"A house-elf crossed every order binding him to this family, interfered with several weeks of correspondence and exposed himself to a wizard outside the household because he believed Harry would be in danger at Hogwarts. We will notify the headmaster."

"We have no proof that the two matters are connected."

"We have three children returning to that school."

Lucius glanced towards Draconia. That ended the argument.

"I will write to Dumbledore tonight," he said. "The details will remain limited."

Hermione opened her mouth.

"He will be given enough to investigate," Narcissa added, before she could object. "He will not be given enough to begin making public accusations which we cannot substantiate."

Hermione considered that and reluctantly nodded.

Harry continued tearing the bread apart.

"And Dobby?" he asked.

Four adults turned towards him.

"If he comes back," Harry said, "you won't punish him, will you?"

Lucius's brows rose. "He imprisoned your owl, stole your correspondence, endangered the International Statute of Secrecy and caused you to be confined for more than a week."

"He thought he was helping."

"He was catastrophically mistaken."

"That doesn't mean he should hurt himself."

Lucius studied him.

Harry wondered whether asking had been a mistake. Dobby belonged to the Malfoys. Harry did not understand the rules governing house-elves, but he understood the expression Uncle Vernon wore before deciding someone needed to be taught a lesson.

Narcissa looked at the bruises visible above Harry's borrowed collar.

"No," she said. "Dobby will not be punished for trying to prevent harm to a child."

Lucius turned towards his wife.

"He will be questioned," she amended.

"Extensively," Lucius said.

"But not punished," Harry persisted.

Narcissa met his eyes. "Not punished."

Harry nodded and returned to his soup.

He was aware of Hermione watching him again, though this time there was no relief in her expression. There was something sadder there, and Harry did not know what to do with it.

He took another piece of bread so she would look away.

It worked.

For a few minutes.

The question of where Harry would spend the remainder of the summer proved more difficult than the rescue itself.

"He can stay with us," Mrs Granger said.

They were still gathered around the table, although Tom had removed the empty dishes and replaced them with tea. Harry had been given hot chocolate without anyone consulting him. He did not object.

"Our guest room is ready," Mrs Granger continued. "It isn't large, but it has a proper bed, and we can arrange for a doctor to examine him tomorrow."

"The Manor is more securely protected," Lucius said. "If Dobby returns, he is more likely to appear there."

Harry was uncertain whether this was an argument in the Manor's favour.

"Harry should be somewhere familiar," Mr Granger said.

"He has never visited your home either," Lucius pointed out.

"He knows us."

"He knows my daughter."

"He knows Hermione."

"Yes," Lucius said. "That is what I meant."

Hermione and Draconia exchanged an offended look. Narcissa ignored both men and addressed Harry directly.

"Where would you prefer to stay?"

The room became quiet again. Harry stared into his hot chocolate.

Nobody had asked him that at Privet Drive. The Dursleys decided where he slept, when he ate, which rooms he could enter and whether he was permitted outside. Even at Hogwarts, his place had been decided by staircases, timetables and a hat which could read his thoughts.

Now six people were waiting as though his preference mattered. He did not want to disappoint the Grangers. He did not want to insult the Malfoys. More importantly, he did not want to choose between Hermione and Draconia.

"Could I do both?"

Hermione blinked.

Draconia tilted her head. "Both?"

"I could stay at the Manor until we know whether Dobby comes back. Then I could visit Hermione's house. Or the other way around. Unless that's inconvenient."

"It isn't," Hermione said immediately.

Draconia spoke at the same time. "Obviously."

Their parents looked at one another. Harry braced himself for an argument. Narcissa said, "That seems sensible."

Mrs Granger nodded. "The Manor first, then. On the condition that we visit tomorrow."

"You may visit whenever you wish," Narcissa said.

Lucius glanced at her. She met the look without expression.

"You may visit whenever it has been arranged in advance," he corrected.

"We'll arrange it now," Hermione said.

Lucius closed his eyes for a moment. Harry held the warm cup between both hands.

"What about Uncle Vernon?"

Nobody answered immediately. His stomach tightened.

"He signed the temporary consent," Mr Granger said carefully. "You will not be expected back there before school."

"Will he get in trouble?"

Mrs Granger looked astonished. "Harry, he locked you in a room."

"I know. I only meant—"

"You are not responsible for what happens to him," Hermione said.

"I didn't say I was."

"You made the face."

Harry looked at her. "You cannot keep inventing faces."

"That one existed last year," Draconia said.

"You're encouraging her."

"I'm confirming independent findings."

Harry looked towards the adults. "Do either of you want them?"

"Desperately," Lucius said.

It took Harry a second to realise he was joking. The discovery was so unexpected that he laughed into his hot chocolate. Lucius looked faintly surprised with himself.

The tension around the table eased.

"Your relatives will be spoken to," Mr Granger said when Harry quietened. "Properly. But nothing will happen tonight, and no one will make a decision concerning where you live without telling you."

Harry nodded. It was not precisely a promise that he would never have to return. He suspected the adults knew that. For the moment, however, it was enough.

- - - - - - -

Malfoy Manor did not look like a place in which children were expected to touch anything.

The entrance hall rose two storeys above them, its pale stone floor polished until the black marble reflected the chandeliers overhead. Portraits watched from dark frames along the walls. A pair of silver peacocks crossed the lawn beyond the windows, their tails trailing through the evening mist.

Harry stood just inside the doors with everything he owned contained in one school trunk.

He had packed quickly at Privet Drive. Most of his belongings had already been inside it because Uncle Vernon disliked seeing evidence that Harry attended Hogwarts. There had been no time to decide what mattered.

The loose floorboard beneath his bed had contained two Chocolate Frog cards, a broken quill, several photographs and all the letters he had managed to hide before Dobby began intercepting them.

Harry had taken everything.

A house-elf appeared beside the trunk with a crack.

Harry jumped.

The elf bowed low. "Mipsy is taking Master Harry Potter's belongings to the east guest room."

She vanished with the trunk.

Harry stared at the space where she had been.

Draconia came to stand beside him. "She won't drop a pudding."

"That's reassuring."

"Usually."

"Less reassuring."

Narcissa led them upstairs.

The east guest room was larger than Harry's bedroom at Privet Drive and Dudley's second bedroom combined. Tall windows overlooked the gardens. A fire burned despite the mild evening, and clean green curtains hung around a bed wide enough for Harry to sleep across instead of along.

His trunk waited at its foot.

"There is a bathroom through there," Narcissa said. "Draconia's room is opposite. Ours is at the end of the corridor."

Harry glanced at the door.

There was no lock upon the outside.

Narcissa followed his gaze.

"The door locks from within," she said. "No one will enter without knocking."

"Oh."

"You are not required to lock it."

"I know."

She waited.

Harry looked away from the door.

"Thank you."

Narcissa crossed to the wardrobe and opened it. Inside hung several sets of clothing in Harry's size: trousers, shirts, jumpers and a pair of dark blue pyjamas.

Harry stared at them.

"You didn't need to buy all that."

"I did not."

"Did Draconia have them?"

Draconia leaned against the bedpost. "Why would I own boys' clothing several inches too short for me?"

"Strange hobby?"

"I'll add it to the list beneath collecting unpleasant Gryffindors."

Narcissa ran one finger along a sleeve.

"The clothes belonged to a cousin. They were placed in storage after he outgrew them. You may use them until something suitable can be purchased."

Harry felt an immediate, shameful relief that nobody had spent money on him.

Narcissa saw that too.

"We will still purchase anything you need," she said.

"I don't need anything."

"You own three shirts."

"Four."

"One has no buttons."

"Buttons are mostly decorative."

Draconia looked at her mother. "He has been spending far too much time with me."

"Not nearly enough," Harry said.

The answer came more easily than he expected.

Draconia's expression softened.

Narcissa moved towards the door. "Dinner will be served in an hour. You may rest until then."

Harry looked towards the corridor.

"Are the Grangers staying?"

"They are returning tomorrow."

"Hermione too?"

"Yes."

"And Draconia is across there?"

Draconia raised one eyebrow. "You have asked that twice."

"I was checking."

Something passed over Narcissa's face.

"She is across the corridor," she said. "Neither of us is going anywhere."

Harry nodded.

When they left, he sat on the edge of the bed and listened to their footsteps fade.

The room was quiet.

Not the held-breath quiet of Privet Drive, in which every sound from downstairs might mean Uncle Vernon approaching. This quiet was deeper. The Manor was too large for every movement to reach him.

Harry looked at the door again.

He crossed the room and tested the handle.

It opened.

He shut it.

Then he opened it once more.

The corridor remained empty. Draconia's door stood ajar opposite his.

Harry returned to the bed.

He told himself he was checking how the latch worked.

He did not lock it.

- - - - - - -

Dinner was served in a room almost as large as the Great Hall at Hogwarts, though only four places had been set at one end of the table.

Harry stopped in the doorway.

"We don't have to sit all the way apart, do we?" he asked.

Draconia glanced towards her mother. "I've been saying this for years."

Narcissa gestured, and the place settings slid closer together.

Lucius arrived moments later carrying two envelopes and looking as though the distance between the chairs had personally offended him.

One envelope bore the Hogwarts crest.

"Already?" Draconia asked.

"Dumbledore replied immediately."

Harry sat up straighter.

Lucius did not open the letter at once. Instead, he took his place and waited until food appeared upon the table.

Harry watched roast chicken, potatoes and vegetables materialise in silver dishes.

A plate appeared before him already filled.

He waited.

Draconia reached for her fork, stopped and looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're doing the face now."

"I do not make faces."

"You've been staring at your potatoes for nearly a minute."

Harry lowered his voice. "Are we waiting for something?"

Draconia's confusion lasted only a moment.

"No. You can eat."

"Oh."

He picked up his fork.

Across the table, Narcissa looked down at her own plate. Lucius carefully unfolded his napkin.

Nobody mentioned it.

Harry ate slowly at first, partly because his stomach had become unaccustomed to full meals and partly because the food seemed too carefully prepared to disappear quickly. When his plate was half-empty, another potato rolled onto it by itself.

Harry stared.

"Mipsy," Narcissa said mildly.

The potato stopped moving.

"I can serve myself."

A small voice emerged from somewhere beneath the table. "Mipsy is sorry, Master Harry Potter."

"You don't have to be sorry. I only meant—thank you."

The elf did not answer. Draconia pushed the vegetable dish towards him instead. "Try those. They are less aggressive."

"You've been speaking to Hermione too much."

"She has corrupted my relationship with dinner."

Lucius broke the seal upon Dumbledore's letter.

"The headmaster is reviewing the school's protections and alerting the staff to indications of Dark magic," he said. "Without a description of the artefact or evidence that it will enter Hogwarts, there is little more he can do."

"That's all?" Draconia asked.

"That is all anyone can do without searching the belongings of every student."

Harry thought of the Stone beneath the third floor and Dumbledore's habit of allowing danger to become obvious before explaining that he had known about it all along. The letter was reassuring in theory. In practice, it felt rather thin.

Harry returned to his dinner.

The second envelope lay unopened beside Lucius's hand.

"What's that one?" Draconia asked.

"School supply lists."

"Those usually come at the end of August."

"Apparently your new Defence professor requires every student to purchase his complete published works."

Draconia groaned.

Lucius opened the envelope.

His expression worsened with every title.

"Gadding with Ghouls," he read. "Travels with Trolls. Voyages with Vampires. Is the man teaching Defence or promoting a bookshop?"

"Who is he?" Harry asked.

"Gilderoy Lockhart," Narcissa said. "He is frequently photographed."

"That is not a qualification."

"It may be his only one," Lucius said.

Harry accepted another potato.

This one did not move by itself.

Hermione arrived at nine o'clock the following morning carrying a suitcase, three notebooks and enough books to suggest she planned to remain until Christmas.

"My parents said I could stay for two nights," she explained as Mipsy struggled beneath the weight of the suitcase. "We're taking you back to Oxford with us afterwards."

"You packed textbooks for two nights?" Draconia asked.

"I didn't know what we might need."

"We are recovering from a rescue, not sitting examinations."

"We need to reconstruct everything Dobby said."

"We did that yesterday."

"You reconstructed it from memory. I intend to write it down properly."

Harry looked at Draconia. "I did warn you about the list."

"You did."

Hermione had barely removed her coat before establishing herself in a smaller morning room overlooking the gardens. She arranged the intercepted letters across a table in chronological order, separated by sender and intended date of arrival.

Harry sat opposite her.

Draconia occupied the chair beside him, one leg tucked beneath her. The letters looked harmless laid out together.

Hermione's began neatly and grew increasingly worried. Draconia's began with complaints about her summer schedule and ended with threats against unreliable owls. Harry's replies were shorter. Some contained jokes. Others contained assurances that everything was fine.

The final reply had been written on the sixth of July. After that, there was nothing. Hermione touched the corner of the page.

"You never received the one about France."

"No."

"Or the photograph of the cat which kept sitting outside my window?"

"No."

"I sent that photograph."

"I didn't receive it either," Harry said.

Hermione looked genuinely aggrieved by this particular theft.

"It was an excellent photograph."

"The cat was blurred," Draconia said.

"It was moving."

"That is generally how living cats behave."

"It looked a little like Professor McGonagall," Hermione told Harry.

"Did it?"

"No," Draconia said.

Harry lifted another letter.

This one was from Draconia. He recognised the decisive slope of her handwriting.

Mother says you may visit in August, provided your relatives are informed properly. Father says this is not an invitation until he has agreed it is an invitation. I have chosen to interpret that as progress.

Harry remembered receiving it.

At the time, he had read the sentence about visiting until the parchment softened beneath his fingers.

He had begun imagining Malfoy Manor long before he saw it. In his imagination the corridors had been colder, Lucius taller and Narcissa almost entirely silent. Draconia had been exactly the same.

"You kept all of them," she said.

Harry looked up.

"I keep letters."

"All of them?"

"Yes."

Draconia examined the stacks again.

Hermione had gone quiet too.

Harry shifted in his chair. "Was I not supposed to?"

"No," Hermione said. "Of course you were."

Harry knew that tone. It was the careful one people had begun using whenever he revealed something about the Dursleys without understanding why it mattered.

He put the letter down.

"It was only because I hadn't had any before."

Draconia's hand stilled on the table.

"None?" she asked.

"Who would've written?"

Neither girl answered.

Outside, something pale moved between the hedges. Harry watched until he realised it was one of the peacocks.

"At first I thought the owls were delayed," he said.

Hermione looked back at him.

"Then I thought Uncle Vernon had frightened them off. Then Hedwig wouldn't leave, and the letters I tried sending through the post came back."

He traced the edge of the parchment.

"After a while, I wondered if maybe you'd stopped writing because I hadn't answered something properly."

"Harry," Hermione said.

"I didn't think it for long."

"How long?"

He shrugged. "Mostly at night."

Draconia sat very still beside him.

Harry wished he had not said it.

"I knew it was stupid."

"It was not stupid," Hermione said.

"It was incorrect," Draconia added. "That is different."

Harry gave her a small smile.

She did not return it.

"If I stop writing to you," Draconia said, "it will be because I am dead, unconscious or being held somewhere without access to an owl."

Hermione frowned. "Those are terrible examples."

"They are the only circumstances likely to prevent me."

"You could be ill."

"I would dictate."

"Your parents might forbid it."

"I would lie."

Lucius, passing the open doorway, stopped.

Draconia looked directly at him.

"I would appeal the decision through appropriate channels," she amended.

Lucius continued walking.

Harry waited until his footsteps faded. "You've done that before."

"Frequently."

Hermione pulled a clean sheet of parchment towards her.

Between them, they devised seven increasingly elaborate procedures for future correspondence. Hermione proposed paired parchment which would mark itself whenever a letter was opened. Draconia wanted to connect the Grangers' fireplace to the Floo Network. Harry suggested that any plan which depended upon Uncle Vernon cooperating should be discarded immediately.

By the time Hermione numbered the final procedure, Draconia had accused her of organising their friendship like a military supply line. Harry listened to them argue, feeling the tightness beneath his ribs slowly loosen.

"We need one more rule," Hermione said eventually.

Draconia eyed the growing list. "There are already seven."

"This one matters."

"They all apparently matter."

"No one investigates anything dangerous alone."

Harry looked up.

Hermione was watching him.

"Why are you looking at me?"

"Because it is primarily your rule."

"I don't investigate things alone."

"You followed Snape into the dungeons by yourself."

"You were both busy."

"That is the definition of alone."

Draconia took the quill from Hermione and added a line beneath the others.

"No one investigates anything dangerous without telling the other two," she read.

"That's different," Harry said.

"It's more achievable," Draconia replied.

Hermione looked dissatisfied but accepted it.

Harry leaned across the table.

"Add that nobody is allowed to decide they're protecting the others by refusing to explain something."

Draconia's quill paused.

Hermione looked between them.

Harry thought of Dobby withholding the truth, Dumbledore leaving them with half-answers, and Draconia retreating behind her surname whenever she believed a problem belonged to her family.

"Fine," Draconia said.

She wrote the eighth rule.

Draconia stared at the final rule for a moment longer than necessary before putting down the quill.  Recovery from more than a week of too little food was not immediate.

For the first few days, Harry woke before dawn and needed several seconds to remember which ceiling hung above him. Sometimes he woke with his heart beating hard, convinced he had heard Uncle Vernon fitting the metal bar across the window.

There was never anyone there.

The bedroom door remained unlocked. Breakfast arrived whether Harry had done anything to deserve it or not. No one shouted when he left a glass upon a table. When he accidentally knocked a porcelain ornament from a shelf, Narcissa repaired it with one movement of her wand and asked whether he had cut himself.

Harry had not known how to answer. He began hiding bread in the drawer beside his bed.

He did not plan to. His hand simply closed around a roll at breakfast, and later he discovered it wrapped in a napkin beneath two pairs of socks.

The following morning, it was gone.

Harry searched the drawer twice before Mipsy appeared at his elbow.

"Mipsy removed the stale food, Master Harry Potter."

Harry's stomach dropped.

"I'm sorry."

The elf's enormous eyes widened. "Master Harry Potter is not being sorry."

"I won't do it again."

"Mipsy is bringing fresh bread."

She vanished and returned with three rolls upon a small plate.

Harry stared at them.

"Mistress Narcissa says food is staying wherever Master Harry Potter wishes until Master Harry Potter believes it will return."

Harry looked towards the open bedroom door. Narcissa was nowhere in sight.

"Did she tell you to say it like that?"

"Mistress Narcissa was using more words."

"That sounds likely."

The bread remained beside his bed. After a week, Harry stopped checking whether it was there. After two, he stopped needing it to be.

- - - - - - -

The Grangers' house in Oxford was smaller, warmer and filled with objects which looked as though people actually used them. Books lay open upon chairs. Mr Granger left his newspaper folded beside the kettle. Hermione's mother kept appointment cards attached to the refrigerator with brightly coloured magnets.

Harry was given a room overlooking the back garden.

Mrs Granger showed him how the window opened, where the towels were kept and which cupboard contained food he could take whenever he wished.

Harry waited for the rules which would follow.

They did not.

Hermione noticed him standing uncertainly in the kitchen that first afternoon.

"You can have anything," she said.

"I know."

"You've been looking at the biscuits for five minutes."

"I was wondering what kind they were."

"Chocolate."

"That took five minutes to determine?"

"The label was facing away."

Hermione turned the packet around. Harry took one. The next day, he took one without waiting for her.

Draconia visited through the Floo and emerged into the Grangers' sitting room covered in soot, looking personally betrayed by the fireplace.

"This method of transport is barbaric."

"You travelled here in less than a minute," Hermione said.

"I inhaled part of Birmingham."

Mr Granger offered her a handkerchief.

Draconia accepted it with grave dignity.

The three of them spent the afternoon walking through Oxford. Hermione tried to explain the history of every building they passed. Draconia pretended not to be interested while asking questions which forced Hermione to explain more.

Harry walked between them.

No one stared at his scar. No one knew his name. The people passing on the pavement saw only three children arguing about whether a medieval tower would be improved by moving staircases.

For several hours, Harry almost forgot that anything waited for them at Hogwarts.

Almost.

No news came from the missing house-elf. Dumbledore wrote once more to assure them that the school's protections were being examined, though Harry had developed a healthy distrust of assurances with no specifics.

Even so, the days continued. His bruises faded. His appetite returned. His school robes had to be adjusted because he grew nearly an inch before the end of August. Mrs Granger said this was what happened when children were fed properly.

Harry thought she was joking until he saw her face. He did not know whether to feel pleased or angry.

Mostly, he felt tired.

- - - - - - -

Diagon Alley was smaller than Harry remembered.

The previous summer, it had seemed endless. He had followed Hagrid through the brick archway and into a world so crowded with impossible things that he had hardly known where to look first. Every window had offered proof that his life was larger than the cupboard beneath the stairs: broomsticks suspended in mid-air, books which muttered upon their shelves, cauldrons stacked in shining towers and owls staring solemnly through brass cage bars. Harry had gone home that evening believing there would always be another corner to turn, another marvel waiting beyond it.

Now he could see from Gringotts to the distant bend near the apothecary.

The alley had not changed. Harry had.

It was still crowded enough that families spilled from the pavements into the cobbled street. Parents called after children who had disappeared into shop doorways, cages rocked precariously atop luggage trolleys, and several new students stood motionless beneath the bank's white columns while trying to take in everything at once. Cauldrons rang from an open workshop. A tawny owl objected loudly to being carried upside down. Further along, a shopkeeper was demonstrating a set of self-folding dress robes which had folded themselves around him and showed no inclination to let go.

Yet Diagon Alley no longer felt like a place Harry might wake from if he looked away.

He knew which shops sold parchment cheaply, which alley led towards the owl emporium and where not to stand when the apothecary received a delivery of dragon liver. He knew that the goblins inside Gringotts were not decorative and that ice cream could melt even when purchased with wizarding money.

More importantly, he was not following anyone.

Hermione walked on his right with their combined school lists held against her chest. Draconia walked on his left, occasionally moving closer whenever the crowd pressed around them. Behind them came four parents who had spent the morning discovering that rescuing a child together did not make agreeing upon a shopping order any easier.

"We should collect the books first," Hermione said. "Flourish and Blotts will only become busier."

"It is already busy," Draconia observed.

"It will become more busy."

"Busier."

Hermione turned her head slowly. "That is what I said."

"It is what you should have said."

"I am attempting to keep six people organised. Forgive me if I don't prioritise impressing you with my grammar."

"You have never needed to prioritise it. You attempt that constantly."

Harry stepped between them before Hermione could begin explaining the difference between correct speech and showing off.

"We could get our robes first."

Both girls looked at him.

Harry reconsidered.

"Or not."

Hermione consulted the list. Draconia consulted the queue outside Flourish and Blotts as though judging whether it could be compelled to move through intimidation alone.

"Books," they decided together.

They looked at one another with matching irritation and immediately began walking in the same direction.

Harry followed, smiling.

They had already visited Gringotts. Hermione's parents had exchanged Muggle money while attempting not to stare at the goblins, and Lucius had withdrawn enough gold to make Harry uncomfortable merely by looking at it. Harry had opened his own vault and felt the familiar, uneasy disbelief upon seeing the heaps of coins inside. A summer spent worrying about the cost of every slice of bread had left him reluctant to touch any of it.

When he purchased his cauldron, he had counted the coins three times.

The shopkeeper had waited patiently through the first count, less patiently through the second and not at all patiently through the third.

Narcissa had noticed.

She had not offered to pay for him. Harry was grateful for that. Instead, she had handed him the correct coins from his own purse and asked whether he trusted her arithmetic.

"I trust it more than mine," Harry had admitted.

"Then we have identified the problem."

Afterwards, she had shown him how to separate the denominations between the pockets of his money bag, making it easier to see what he was spending. She had done it without lowering her voice or behaving as though Harry's confusion were something shameful.

It was a very different kind of kindness from Mrs Granger's, but Harry was beginning to recognise both.

The Grangers now remained near Narcissa, who was explaining why wizarding school trunks were charmed against water but not, apparently, against students spilling ink inside them. Mr Granger had fallen several paces behind with Lucius and was asking increasingly detailed questions about goblin banking.

"So they provide secure storage," Mr Granger said, "but no interest?"

"They preserve wealth," Lucius replied.

"But the value of the money diminishes over time."

"Gold does not diminish."

"Its purchasing power might."

Lucius glanced at him. "Why?"

"Inflation."

"What is inflating?"

Harry heard Draconia release a quiet breath.

"Father will either learn Muggle economics," she said, "or kill Mr Granger before lunch."

"Hermione's father survived asking a goblin whether they offered mortgages," Harry said. "I think he'll be all right."

"He asked what?"

Behind them, Mr Granger had begun explaining central banks.

Lucius's answers grew noticeably shorter.

They passed Quality Quidditch Supplies, where a crowd had gathered around the newly released Nimbus Two Thousand and One. Draconia slowed just enough to examine it.

The broom was displayed upon deep purple velvet, its polished handle gleaming beneath the window lights. A gold inscription advertised improved speed, finer control and protections against minor jinxes.

"Father intends to purchase them for the Slytherin team," Draconia said.

"All of them?" Hermione asked.

"Only seven. Any more would be excessive."

Hermione stared at her. "Seven is excessive."

"It is the minimum number required for a team."

"You already have brooms."

"We have inferior brooms."

"They worked perfectly well last year."

"They worked adequately. Adequacy is not the same as quality."

Harry leaned nearer the glass. The broom was beautiful, but its price tag contained more digits than he thought any broom ought to require.

"Are you joining the team?" he asked.

"I'm considering it."

"You have been considering it since June," Hermione said.

"I prefer to make informed decisions."

"You practised every morning at the Manor."

"That was research."

Harry caught her reflection in the glass. "You want to play."

Draconia gave him a cool look which failed to conceal that he was right.

Before she could answer, Narcissa called them away from the window.

Flourish and Blotts was visible further down the street, though its entrance had nearly vanished behind a queue which stretched onto the pavement and bent around the neighbouring shop. Witches in brightly coloured robes stood shoulder to shoulder beneath the awning. Children clutched piles of books and tried to see through the front windows, while an exhausted shop assistant moved along the line explaining that signed copies were limited to three per person.

Harry stopped.

"Do we have to go in?"

Hermione studied the crowd. "We need our Defence books."

"Today?"

"We are already here."

"We could return when he isn't."

"When who isn't?"

Draconia pointed towards the enormous banner stretched across the upper half of the shopfront.

GILDEROY LOCKHART Signing Copies of His Autobiography MAGICAL ME Today, 12:30–4:30

Lockhart's smiling face occupied both ends of the banner. The portraits occasionally turned towards one another and winked, as if even two copies of him could not resist their mutual charm.

Mrs Granger shaded her eyes against the sunlight.

"He has excellent teeth."

Mr Granger, briefly released from his economic discussion, examined the moving photograph. "They move."

"Everything in our world moves," Draconia said. "It does not make it intelligent."

A nearby witch wearing lilac robes turned and frowned at her.

Draconia smiled politely.

The witch faced forward again.

Hermione opened the school list and ran one finger down the required texts. "Every Defence Against the Dark Arts book is his."

Harry looked over her shoulder.

Their list included Break with a Banshee, Gadding with Ghouls, Gilderoy Lockhart's Guide to Household Pests, Holidays with Hags, Travels with Trolls, Voyages with Vampires, Wandering with Werewolves and Year with the Yeti.

"Perhaps he couldn't decide which one was useful," Harry said.

"Or the school receives a percentage," Draconia suggested.

"Hogwarts would not choose textbooks for financial reasons."

"You have touching faith in institutions."

Hermione ignored that. She had turned the list over and was reading the short biography printed beneath Lockhart's name.

"He has received the Order of Merlin, Third Class," she said. "Honorary membership in the Dark Force Defence League, five-time winner of Witch Weekly's Most-Charming-Smile Award—"

"That last one seems especially relevant to teaching," Draconia said.

"He has also apparently defeated a werewolf, subdued a vampire and cured a village of some form of possession."

"Apparently?"

"That is what his books claim."

Draconia looked faintly pleased by Hermione's scepticism. "My father has an Order of Merlin."

Lucius glanced towards her. "Second Class."

"That was not an invitation to participate."

Mr Granger coughed into his hand.

Narcissa's eyes lowered briefly towards the pavement.

Lucius looked from one of them to the other and appeared to decide that asking would only make matters worse.

The line shifted. They moved forward several feet and were swallowed by the crowd near the entrance.

Inside, Flourish and Blotts was hotter than the street. Every available surface had been covered in copies of Lockhart's books. They rose in towers beside the windows, occupied the ends of shelves and formed a glittering blue-and-gold wall behind the signing table. Purple banners hung from the ceiling. Lockhart's face beamed down from each one.

The man himself sat beneath them in robes of forget-me-not blue.

He appeared even more polished than his photographs. His golden hair curled with improbable precision, and his smile remained fixed while he signed books, shook hands and turned towards the photographer in seemingly effortless sequence. Every few moments, the camera released a cloud of violet smoke and a flash bright enough to illuminate the shop.

Hermione watched him sign a witch's copy of Magical Me.

"He writes without looking at the page," she said.

"Perhaps his signature is more practised than his spells," Draconia murmured.

"You haven't read any of his books."

"Neither have you."

"I read the first three chapters of Break with a Banshee while we were waiting."

"When?"

"In the queue."

"You were holding the school list."

"The book was beneath it."

Draconia looked at Harry. "She conceals reading material about her person."

"You knew this."

"I had suspected."

Harry was still smiling when a witch near the register heard his name.

It had been spoken quietly. Mrs Granger had merely asked whether Harry wanted help carrying his cauldron. Yet the witch turned so quickly that the feathers upon her hat struck the man beside her.

"Harry Potter?"

The name travelled faster than any sound had a right to travel.

Heads turned. The whisper passed between shelves and through the queue, changing slightly with every repetition but never becoming less recognisable.

Harry Potter.

Is that really him?

Look at the scar.

Harry felt the space around him contract.

For one hopeful moment, he considered denying it. Unfortunately, denying his identity had become considerably more difficult since entering a shop containing several hundred people who knew what his forehead looked like.

Hermione moved closer.

Draconia's posture changed almost imperceptibly. Her shoulders drew back, her chin lifted and the expression she normally reserved for other Slytherins appeared upon her face.

Harry tried to retreat towards the shelves.

The photographer spotted him first.

His camera rose.

The flash exploded.

Harry flinched.

Lockhart looked away from the witch whose book he was signing and saw where the camera had turned. His smile somehow widened.

"It can't be!"

Before Harry understood what was happening, Lockhart had risen from his chair and pushed through the delighted crowd. A hand closed around Harry's shoulder.

The grip was firm enough to steer him.

"There he is!" Lockhart announced. "Harry Potter himself!"

The crowd applauded.

Harry stumbled against the edge of the signing table. Lockhart pulled him upright and placed him beside the chair, one arm settling heavily around his shoulders.

Up close, he smelled overwhelmingly of cologne and mint.

"What an extraordinary surprise," Lockhart said, though the photographer was already moving into position as if the surprise had been rehearsed. "The Boy Who Lived, here to purchase the works of the man who has lived through practically everything else!"

Several people laughed.

Harry attempted to step away.

Lockhart's fingers tightened against his shoulder.

"Just here, Harry. Face the camera."

"I'd rather not."

The photographer lifted the camera.

Lockhart either failed to hear him or chose not to.

"Together, Mr Potter! A smile for the Prophet!"

The first purple flash burst directly before Harry's eyes.

For half a second, the shop vanished.

He was back in the hallway at Privet Drive with Uncle Vernon's hand fastened around the back of his neck. The fingers were thicker, the smell different, but the certainty was the same: an adult had decided where Harry would stand, and whatever Harry wanted no longer mattered.

Noise pressed in from every direction.

Lockhart was saying something about fame.

The photographer called for Harry to lift his chin.

Harry tried to pull his shoulder free.

"I said I don't—"

Another flash swallowed the words.

A tower of Lockhart's books collapsed.

The crash was magnificent.

Copies of Year with the Yeti struck the floor one after another, taking Holidays with Hags with them. Several slid beneath the signing table. One landed against the photographer's boot, and another knocked over a display advertising commemorative quills.

Hermione stood behind the ruins with her hand still resting against the shelf.

"Oh," she said.

She did not sound sorry.

The photographer lowered his camera. Two shop assistants rushed forward, one calling for customers to step back and the other attempting to rescue an avalanche of autobiographies.

Lockhart's attention moved towards the disaster.

Draconia stepped directly between Harry and the photographer.

"You do not have permission to publish his image."

The photographer blinked. "Who're you?"

"Draconia Malfoy."

Recognition crossed his face.

It was different from the recognition Harry received. There was no delight in it, only sudden calculation.

"The Daily Prophet doesn't require permission for photographs taken at public events," he said, though with less certainty.

"This is a bookshop, not a Ministry press conference, and he has twice attempted to leave."

"Now, see here—"

"My father's solicitors greatly enjoy copyright disputes. They enjoy privacy disputes even more."

The photographer looked past her.

Lucius was making his way through the crowd.

He did not hurry. Somehow that made his approach more alarming.

Narcissa followed close behind, her expression composed except for the brightness of her eyes.

The photographer covered the front of his camera with one hand.

Lockhart finally released Harry.

"No need for all that," he said, smoothing the front of his robes as though the entire incident had resulted from other people's poor manners. "Merely welcoming a fellow celebrity."

"I'm not a celebrity," Harry said.

Lockhart laughed warmly.

"No, no, modesty. I understand entirely. I was much the same at your age."

Harry doubted this with surprising intensity.

Lucius stopped beside Draconia.

"Mr Potter said no," he said.

The noise immediately around them diminished.

Lockhart's smile remained, though it seemed to require more effort. "A harmless photograph, Malfoy. Surely we needn't become dramatic."

"I find that people who describe their own behaviour as harmless have usually noticed that no one else intends to."

Narcissa reached Harry and looked at the handprint Lockhart's grip had left upon the shoulder of his robes.

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Did he frighten you?"

Harry became aware of several strangers listening.

"I'm fine."

The answer was out before he had decided to give it.

Hermione's mouth tightened.

Narcissa did not challenge him in front of the crowd. She merely touched his shoulder where Lockhart had held it, light enough that Harry could have moved away.

"He will not touch you again," she said.

Draconia took Harry's arm.

Hermione claimed the other.

Neither asked permission, but Harry found that this did not feel the same. Their hands were not forcing him to stay. They were giving him a direction in which to leave.

They pulled him between two shelves before Lockhart could compare their departures to an episode from one of his books.

The Transfiguration section was cooler and nearly empty. The sounds of the signing became muffled behind the tall shelves, though Lockhart's voice still rose above the crowd whenever he remembered he had an audience.

Harry leaned against a bookcase.

Hermione released his arm and immediately began examining him.

"Did he hurt your shoulder?"

"No."

"Can you move it?"

Harry lifted his arm.

"There."

"That does not prove anything."

"It proves I have an arm."

Draconia remained near the end of the aisle, watching to ensure no photographer followed them. "He was warned."

"By you or your father?" Harry asked.

"Both."

"Which one should frighten him more?"

"Father has lawyers."

"And you?"

"I have fewer restrictions."

Harry laughed.

The sound escaped more sharply than expected, carrying some of the strain with it. Hermione's shoulders loosened.

"Did you knock those books over deliberately?" he asked her.

"They were stacked dangerously."

"They were not," Draconia said without turning.

"They became dangerous when I pushed them."

Harry looked at Hermione.

She straightened the edge of her sleeve and tried to appear innocent.

"You could've hit someone."

"I checked where they would fall."

"You calculated the collapse?"

"It was not particularly complicated."

Draconia finally turned from the aisle. "You deliberately destroyed a commercial display to create a distraction."

"I did not destroy it. Books are remarkably resilient."

"You are becoming a terrible influence."

"On whom?"

Draconia nodded towards Harry.

"He climbed onto a broomstick to catch a flying key before I pushed over one display."

"Then perhaps he is a terrible influence on me."

"That seems more plausible."

Harry smiled again, but Hermione continued watching him.

"What?" he asked.

"You looked frightened."

The humour faded.

Harry glanced towards the opening of the aisle. "There were a lot of people."

"It wasn't the people."

"I didn't like him grabbing me."

"That is not the same as being fine."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Harry met her eyes.

The immediate answer would have been yes. It would also have been a lie, or at least the sort of answer which prevented anyone from asking further questions.

He looked down at the shoulder of his robe.

"I'm fine now," he said.

Hermione considered the correction.

"All right."

She did not sound entirely satisfied, but she accepted it.

Draconia studied Harry for another moment, then looked back towards Lockhart. "I dislike him."

"You dislike most people," Hermione said.

"Yes, but I generally require longer than thirty seconds to reach a conclusion."

"You disliked me within thirty seconds."

"I was correct more quickly then."

Hermione folded her arms.

Harry said, "You two became friends very strangely."

"We are not—" Draconia began.

"Harry?"

The voice came from behind them.

A small red-haired girl stood at the entrance to the aisle, clutching a new pewter cauldron against her chest. She looked as though she had gathered a considerable amount of courage simply to say his name and had now exhausted the supply.

Harry recognised her. He had seen her at King's Cross the year before, mostly hidden behind her mother while the twins teased her about not being old enough for Hogwarts.

"Hello," he said.

Colour flooded her face.

She opened her mouth.

No sound emerged.

Her eyes moved from Harry to Hermione, then to Draconia, whose calm examination seemed to make matters worse.

The girl retreated so quickly that the cauldron struck the shelf behind her with a hollow clang.

Harry stared after her.

"Did I do something?"

Draconia examined him. "You spoke."

"That usually works better."

"Not for everyone."

Hermione peered around the end of the bookcase. "Her name is Ginny. She's Ron Weasley's sister."

"I remember."

"She's beginning Hogwarts this year."

Harry looked towards the adjoining aisle.

Ginny had retreated to where the rest of her family were gathered around a pile of second-hand books. She stood behind one of her brothers, pretending to inspect the bottom of her cauldron.

"She looked terrified."

"Not terrified," Hermione said.

"What, then?"

Hermione opened her mouth and glanced towards Draconia.

Draconia's expression became warningly pleasant.

"Overwhelmed," Hermione decided.

"By me saying hello?"

"You can be unsettling," Draconia said.

"You threatened a photographer with lawyers."

"I was not the one unsettling her."

Before Harry could ask what that meant, Mrs Weasley noticed him.

Her face brightened. She abandoned the argument she had been having with one of the twins and crossed the shop with surprising speed.

"Harry, dear!"

Harry had just enough warning to brace himself before she hugged him.

Unlike Lockhart, she released him the moment she felt him stiffen.

"Oh, I'm sorry," she said. "I should have asked."

"It's all right."

"No, I should have. Are you well? Arthur heard there had been some trouble, but nobody would tell us anything beyond—" Her eyes travelled over him, taking in the new robes, the fading mark above his eyebrow and the weight he had not yet regained. "Have you been eating?"

Harry glanced towards Draconia. "Apparently not enough to satisfy anyone."

Mrs Weasley touched his cheek with the backs of her fingers, then seemed to remember herself and lowered her hand. "You're welcome with us any time. You know that, don't you?"

Harry did not know that.

He nodded anyway.

Mr Weasley approached more slowly, accompanied by the rest of the family. Ron gave Harry an awkward wave from behind a tower of books. The twins greeted him cheerfully and attempted to convince Mrs Weasley that Lockhart's collected works counted as a single item and therefore only one of them needed to own the set.

Ginny remained near the back.

"Hello again," Harry said.

Her face reddened, but this time she managed a small, "Hello."

Harry smiled at her.

She looked at the floor.

Mr Weasley's attention had moved beyond Harry.

Lucius stood at the end of the aisle with Narcissa and the Grangers.

The openness in Mr Weasley's face disappeared.

"Malfoy."

Lucius stopped.

"Weasley."

The warmth drained from the space so quickly that Harry almost expected frost to form upon the shelves.

Draconia's shoulders stiffened beside him.

Narcissa moved half a step closer to her daughter. Mr and Mrs Granger looked between the two men, recognising hostility without understanding its history.

Mr Weasley's gaze travelled over the group: Lucius and Narcissa, the Grangers, Draconia beside Hermione, and finally Harry standing among them all.

"I heard there was trouble at Privet Drive," he said.

Harry's stomach tightened.

Several people nearby turned.

Mr Weasley seemed to realise his mistake at once.

"Dumbledore contacted the Ministry," he explained quickly. "Only the department responsible for Muggle welfare concerns. He needed assistance confirming certain details."

Lucius's expression cooled.

"Apparently half of Diagon Alley belongs to the appropriate department."

Mr Weasley's ears reddened. "I haven't told anyone."

"Then there was no reason to announce it in a crowded shop."

"I was asking whether the boy was all right."

"The boy is standing in front of you."

Harry wished they would both stop referring to him as though he were not.

"I'm all right," he said.

Neither man appeared to hear.

"Father," Draconia said.

She did not raise her voice.

Lucius stopped speaking.

Mr Weasley looked from him to Draconia, plainly surprised that a single word had accomplished what several Ministry officials apparently could not.

"So," he said after a moment, "you're helping Harry now?"

The question held more suspicion than curiosity.

Harry felt Draconia go still.

Lucius did not react visibly, but Narcissa's expression sharpened.

"Is there a reason I should not?" Lucius asked.

"There are several reasons I would have expected you not to."

"Molly," Mrs Granger said quietly, "they were the ones who helped us reach him."

Mrs Weasley looked towards her.

"My husband and I could not have entered that house safely without them," Mr Granger added. "Nor could we have removed Harry as quickly."

Lucius glanced at him, perhaps surprised by the defence.

Mr Weasley's suspicion did not vanish. It changed direction.

"Why?" he asked.

Lucius looked towards Harry before answering.

"My daughter asked."

Draconia's head turned slightly.

"And," Lucius continued, "the situation required it."

Harry understood what that answer cost him. Lucius disliked providing explanations at all, especially to Arthur Weasley, and he had just offered one in front of half the shop.

Mr Weasley seemed to understand it too.

Some of the accusation left his face.

Not all of it.

"You've spent much of the summer attempting to prevent my department from conducting its work," he said.

"You have spent much of the summer encouraging your colleagues to search my home."

"For illegal Dark artefacts."

"None were found."

"That does not prove none existed."

"It proves your department failed to find them."

"It proves you know how to hide things."

Lucius took one deliberate step forward.

Harry saw Mrs Weasley reach for her husband's arm. Narcissa did not move at all, which somehow seemed more dangerous.

Draconia caught the edge of her father's sleeve.

It was a small gesture, almost lost among the crowded aisle. She did not pull or speak.

Lucius looked down at her hand.

Then he stopped.

"No," he said, returning his attention to Mr Weasley. "It means that none were found."

Mr Weasley's eyes flicked towards Draconia.

Something altered in his expression. The distrust remained, but he had seen her stop the argument. He had also seen Lucius allow it.

The difference mattered.

Mrs Weasley seized the silence before either man could ruin it.

"Ginny still needs her books," she announced.

She began separating Lockhart's works into several uneven stacks. Most of the Weasley copies were second-hand, their corners softened and their covers scratched. Ginny attempted to lift hers along with the cauldron she was already carrying.

The top book began to slide.

Harry stepped forward automatically. "Here, I can carry some."

Ginny looked up at him.

Her fingers opened.

The entire stack struck the floor.

Harry crouched at once. Ginny did the same, and their foreheads collided above a copy of Break with a Banshee.

"Ow," Harry said.

Ginny made a strangled sound.

Then she stood and fled behind the twins.

Harry remained kneeling among Lockhart's smiling faces.

Draconia looked down at him.

"I believe speaking may not be the problem."

"Thank you for solving it."

Hermione crouched beside him and began gathering the books. Her mouth twitched.

"You could try being less famous," she suggested.

"I've been trying that for years."

Mrs Weasley apologised repeatedly. Harry assured her that he was fine, returned the books and stood before anyone could begin examining his forehead.

Lucius broke the silence by addressing Mr Weasley.

"A word."

Arthur looked suspicious again, but he followed Lucius towards the end of the aisle. Narcissa went with them, though she remained several paces away and pretended to examine a display of magical atlases.

Harry, Hermione and Draconia drifted closer under the pretence of gathering books. Lucius lowered his voice, but the crowded shop forced them near enough to hear.

"You should be wary this year."

Mr Weasley frowned. "Of what?"

"If I knew, I would be more specific."

"Is this some kind of threat?"

"No. It is advice."

"You will forgive me if I find advice from you difficult to distinguish from a threat."

"I am not asking for your trust."

"Then what are you asking?"

Lucius glanced towards the Weasley children. His gaze rested briefly upon Ginny.

"If an unfamiliar object enters your home, do not assume that it is harmless because it appears worthless."

Mr Weasley's expression changed.

"What do you know?"

"Not enough."

"Who has threatened my family?"

"I did not say anyone had."

"You crossed half this shop to warn me about an object, Malfoy. Don't insult me by pretending this is a general concern."

Lucius's jaw tightened.

For a moment, Harry thought he would walk away.

"A man approached me with a proposal intended to damage your reputation," he said. "I refused him."

"What sort of proposal?"

"One involving an old magical artefact."

"Dark?"

"He believed so."

"Who?"

Lucius looked towards the listening crowd, then back at Mr Weasley. "Not here."

Arthur's eyes narrowed.

"You expect me to believe that you refused an opportunity to undermine me?"

"No. I expect your pride to obstruct you in whatever manner it finds most satisfying. I am warning you despite it."

Mr Weasley stared at him.

"Why?"

Lucius's gaze moved once more towards the children. This time, it settled upon Harry and Draconia.

"Because political disagreements cease to be entertaining when someone places a child in the middle of them."

Mr Weasley followed his gaze.

The anger in his face did not disappear, but something within it yielded.

"Was the object meant for one of my children?"

"I believe your daughter was the intended route."

Across the aisle, Ginny was helping Mrs Weasley rearrange the books, unaware that either man was looking at her.

Mr Weasley's face drained of colour.

"Where is it now?"

"The man who brought it to my home left with it."

"You watched him leave?"

"Yes."

"And you're certain it was still in his possession?"

Lucius hesitated only slightly.

"I saw it in his hand."

That was not precisely an answer.

Mr Weasley noticed.

"So it could still be intended for Ginny."

"It could be intended for no one. The threat may have ended when I refused to participate."

"But you don't believe that."

"I would not be having this conversation if I did."

Mr Weasley drew a slow breath.

"Tell me who brought it."

"Somewhere private."

Arthur nodded once.

The movement did not signal trust. It signalled that, for the moment, protecting Ginny mattered more than hating Lucius.

"That may be the first useful thing you've ever said to me," he muttered.

"Do not become sentimental."

Lucius left him there and returned to Narcissa. Of the three children, Hermione pretended they had not overheard.

Hermione looked towards Ginny. "Should we tell her?"

"Her parents know," Harry said.

Ginny stood among her brothers with a battered stack of textbooks held against her chest. Nothing among her possessions appeared unusual: second-hand books, folded robes, a cauldron and a narrow wand box.

Harry remembered that the most dangerous object at Hogwarts the previous year had been small enough to carry beneath one arm and ordinary enough to wrap in brown paper.

"We keep an eye on her," Draconia decided.

Hermione nodded. "Discreetly."

Harry considered Hermione's usual standard of discretion but wisely said nothing.

- - - - - - -

The final days of August passed too quickly.

Harry divided them between the Grangers' home and Malfoy Manor. Neither place became entirely familiar, but both began to acquire small points of certainty.

At the Grangers', the third stair creaked, breakfast was eaten in the kitchen and Hermione's father fell asleep halfway through the evening news.

At the Manor, the west corridor was colder than the rest of the house, the silver peacocks attacked anyone carrying berries, and Narcissa drank tea in the smaller drawing room every evening at precisely half past eight.

Lucius continued treating Harry with formal courtesy.

He did not ask about the cupboard. He did not discuss the bars or the empty tins upon the windowsill. He arranged for Harry's school trunk to be repaired, purchased a proper winter cloak and informed him that both were practical necessities when Harry attempted to object.

Once, Harry found him standing in the entrance hall with a letter from the Ministry.

"Am I going back?" Harry asked.

The question escaped before he could stop it.

Lucius looked up. "To Privet Drive?"

Harry nodded.

"Not before Hogwarts."

"That isn't what I meant."

"I know."

Lucius folded the letter.

"There are protections surrounding your mother's relatives which cannot be dismissed without examination. Dumbledore insists that the house remains important to your safety."

Harry felt the old weight settle in his stomach.

"So I do have to go back."

"Perhaps."

Harry looked away.

"I will not lie to you simply because the truth is unpleasant," Lucius said. "It is possible you will be required to return for part of future summers."

"Right."

"It is not possible," Lucius continued, "that you will be left there without contact, observation or a means of leaving."

Harry looked back at him.

Lucius's face remained composed, but his voice had become very quiet.

"Your relatives mistook isolation for permission," he said. "They will not make that mistake again."

Harry swallowed.

"Thank you."

Lucius inclined his head.

It was not affection. It was not a promise that Harry would never be afraid again.

But it was solid.

Harry found that he trusted solid things.

- - - - - - -

King's Cross Station was crowded with Muggles who had no idea that half the luggage trolleys passing them contained cauldrons.

They hurried beneath the great iron-and-glass roof with newspapers tucked under their arms, briefcases swinging beside their knees and expressions of determined annoyance whenever an owl hooted beneath a travelling cloak. Announcements echoed overhead. Trains screamed against their brakes. Somewhere near the ticket barriers, a child was crying because his balloon had escaped into the rafters.

Harry stood beneath the clock between platforms nine and ten with one hand resting upon his trolley.

Hedwig's cage occupied the top. His repaired school trunk sat beneath it, its brass corners newly polished and the broken latch replaced with one that no longer opened whenever the trunk was lifted. Narcissa had also placed a dark green blanket over the Gryffindor scarf visible through the narrow gap beneath the lid, claiming that properly packed luggage did not display its contents to an entire railway station.

Harry suspected Lucius had objected to carrying Gryffindor colours through Muggle London.

The Grangers had arrived twenty-three minutes early.

Mr Granger said this was because one could never predict London traffic. Hermione said it was because her father distrusted any journey without a printed timetable. Mrs Granger said nothing, though Harry had seen her checking her watch every few minutes since they left Oxford.

The Malfoys arrived precisely on time and behaved as if this were the same thing.

They emerged through the crowd in a group which Muggles unconsciously moved aside to avoid. Lucius wore dark travelling robes beneath a long black coat and looked faintly displeased by everything from the station architecture to a nearby vending machine. Narcissa appeared perfectly composed despite steering Draconia's trolley with one hand while preventing a concealed owl cage from sliding off it with the other.

Draconia walked beside them carrying nothing.

Hermione looked at the trolley, then at her.

"Did you pack any of that yourself?"

"I supervised."

"That means no."

"It means I ensured it was done correctly."

"You told a house-elf what to put in your trunk."

"I produced a written inventory."

Hermione stared at her. "How long was it?"

"Three pages."

"That is worse."

Draconia's gaze travelled towards Hermione's own luggage. There were two trunks upon her trolley, a canvas satchel hanging from the handle and a small tower of books strapped precariously beside it.

"You appear to have packed the contents of a library."

"They're reference books."

"We are going to a school containing an actual library."

"The Hogwarts library does not permit students to remove certain books, and these are my own annotated copies."

"Three pages," Draconia repeated. "Written inventory."

"That is completely different."

"How?"

"I carried my own."

Mr Granger was bent behind the trolley, attempting to prevent one wheel from turning permanently towards platform eight.

"Hermione," he said with some strain, "what exactly did you put in the second trunk?"

"Books."

"Yes, I had reached that conclusion."

Harry moved to help him straighten the wheel.

Draconia glanced at her immaculately packed luggage, then towards Hermione's father.

"I could ask our driver to—"

"No," Hermione said immediately. "Muggle-born students have managed railway stations before."

"I was going to say that I could ask him to carry it."

"Oh."

Draconia's expression became unbearably satisfied. "You're very defensive this morning."

"I am not."

"You assumed an insult before I had finished speaking."

"Experience has made that efficient."

Harry took hold of Hermione's trolley alongside Mr Granger and pushed. The stubborn wheel gave a sharp squeak before reluctantly facing the correct direction.

"There," he said. "Now it only sounds as though we're strangling a mouse."

"I told you not to pack the encyclopaedia set," Mrs Granger said.

"It might be useful."

"At what point during your second year do you expect to require four volumes on the history of European magical transport?"

Hermione hesitated. "I don't know yet."

"Then it is fortunate you brought them," Draconia said solemnly.

Mr Granger laughed.

Hermione glared at all three of them.

By then, the adults had reached one another.

Mr Granger offered Lucius his hand without hesitation. Lucius looked at it only briefly before accepting.

"Malfoy."

"Granger."

The formality remained, but it no longer possessed the sharp edge it had carried at their first meeting. During the final weeks of August, Lucius had endured a dinner at the Grangers' home, two extended discussions about magical finance and an afternoon in which Mr Granger attempted to explain electricity. Mr Granger, in return, had survived Malfoy Manor, Floo travel and Lucius's opinions about Muggle tax law.

Harry suspected each man now considered the other an exhausting but interesting problem.

"Traffic was good?" Mr Granger asked.

"We do not use the roads."

"Right. Of course."

"Your journey?"

"Forty-seven minutes."

Lucius nodded as if this answered something important.

Mrs Granger embraced Narcissa with far less uncertainty than she had shown a month earlier. Narcissa stiffened for only an instant before returning the gesture.

"I put a small medical kit in Hermione's trunk," Mrs Granger said after releasing her. "There's one for Harry as well."

"Narcissa has already packed enough potion to treat a small Quidditch team," Lucius remarked.

"Two small Quidditch teams," Narcissa corrected.

Harry looked towards his trunk.

"That explains why it became heavier."

"You will be grateful if you need it."

"I'm beginning to think the trunk itself is the greater threat."

Narcissa's lips curved faintly.

Around them, more Hogwarts families were arriving. Their attempts at concealment varied considerably. One wizard had thrown a blanket over three broomsticks, leaving their handles protruding over the edge of his trolley. A large spotted toad escaped from beneath a woman's hat and began hopping towards the Underground entrance. Two boys with identical red hair pursued it while blaming one another loudly.

Harry recognised the Weasley twins. They disappeared behind a newsstand before either noticed him.

Hermione consulted the clock.

"We should go through. It's nearly quarter past ten."

"The train does not depart until eleven," Draconia said.

"We still need to find a compartment."

"There are dozens."

"And hundreds of students."

"Most of whom are capable of sitting."

Harry looked from one to the other. "I'm beginning to understand why your parents arrived at different times."

Neither girl heard him.

Mr Granger examined the apparently solid barrier separating platforms nine and ten.

"We walk directly through that?"

"Yes," Hermione said.

"In front of everyone?"

"Not everyone at once."

"That was going to be my next question."

Mrs Granger peered at the brickwork. "And it definitely is not solid?"

"It is to Muggles who aren't meant to cross," Harry explained.

Mr Granger pressed one finger experimentally against a brick.

It did not move.

"Apparently I'm not meant to cross."

"You have to walk," Hermione said. "You can't test it first."

"Why not?"

"Because magic is frequently offended by sensible precautions," Draconia said.

Lucius regarded the steady stream of Muggles passing behind them. "We are becoming conspicuous."

He placed one hand upon Draconia's trolley.

"We will go first."

Draconia stepped beside him. Narcissa joined them on the other side, and the three Malfoys moved towards the barrier without breaking stride. A family wheeling two large suitcases crossed between Harry and them for a moment.

When it passed, the Malfoys were gone.

Mr Granger stared.

"That is deeply unsettling."

"It's perfectly safe," Hermione assured him.

"You disappeared through a pub wall the first time you entered Diagon Alley," Harry reminded him.

"Yes, but I had not yet developed standards."

Mrs Granger took one side of Hermione's trolley. "We'll go together."

"Mum, you don't have to—"

"I am not allowing my daughter to vanish into a railway wall without seeing where she goes."

Hermione looked as though she might argue, then smiled instead.

They began moving.

At the last second, Mr Granger shut his eyes.

The three of them disappeared.

Harry remained alone on the Muggle side of the barrier.

Only for a moment.

He knew precisely where everyone had gone. He knew they would be waiting for him on the other side. Even so, something cold and instinctive moved beneath his ribs when he saw the empty space where they had been.

Harry tightened his hands around the trolley.

Then Hedwig hooted impatiently above him.

"Yes, all right."

He pushed forward.

The barrier gave way without resistance.

Steam swept across his glasses.

Platform Nine and Three-Quarters stretched beneath a hazy canopy, louder and brighter than the station he had left behind. The scarlet Hogwarts Express stood beside the platform, its engine breathing great clouds of white vapour which curled around trunks and ankles. Cats slipped between the wheels of trolleys. Owls called to one another from their cages, and students leaned from open carriage windows to shout greetings across the crowd.

Harry stopped just beyond the barrier.

The sight struck him with the same force it had the previous year.

Not wonder this time. Recognition.

He knew the smell of coal smoke and hot metal. He knew the shrill whistle would sound once at ten to eleven and again before the doors closed. He knew the prefects would begin pretending to organise people while the younger students ignored them. He knew the train would carry them past London, through fields and villages, then north beneath a sky which always seemed to grow larger the closer they came to Hogwarts.

For weeks, Harry had been afraid something would prevent him from seeing it again.

Now the engine waited exactly where it should.

Hermione waved from several yards away. The Grangers stood beside her, both wearing the pleased, slightly dazed expressions they always acquired when confronted by obvious magic.

Draconia had already found a porter and was directing the loading of her trunk.

Harry pushed his trolley towards them.

"You took your time," she said.

"I was gone for six seconds."

"Eight."

"You counted?"

"You were the last one through."

Hermione looked at him more carefully. "Were you all right?"

"Yes. Hedwig was objecting to the delay."

Hedwig gave a dignified hoot, as if confirming this.

Draconia stepped closer to the cage. "At least one of you understands punctuality."

Hedwig clicked her beak at her.

Harry grinned. "She says you arrived twenty-three minutes after Hermione."

"She does not."

"You don't speak owl."

"Neither do you."

"I've known her longer."

The argument followed them towards the train.

For several minutes, the platform dissolved into organised confusion. Their trunks had to be labelled, surrendered to the guard and then reclaimed when Hermione realised one of hers had been placed beside luggage bound for the rear of the train. Narcissa checked Draconia's ticket twice. Mr Granger attempted to help a wizard lift a cage containing something which hissed beneath a blanket and wisely abandoned the effort when the cage hissed his name.

Harry remained near the open carriage door with Hedwig.

He watched families say goodbye.

Some parents issued instructions. Others pressed money into their children's hands or attempted to repair uniforms which would be untidy again before the train left London. A very small first-year was clinging to his father's robes, while an older girl promised to show him where to sit.

Harry had left for Hogwarts the previous year with Hagrid's instructions folded inside his pocket and the Dursleys already driving away.

He had not known enough then to understand that the platform could be part of leaving too.

Mrs Granger reached Hermione first.

She straightened her daughter's jumper, tucked a loose curl behind her ear and kissed her forehead.

"Mum," Hermione protested, glancing at the surrounding students.

"You will survive."

"I know."

"That was directed at the embarrassment, not the school."

Mr Granger hugged Hermione next. "Try not to read during every meal."

"I don't."

"You read the marmalade jar yesterday."

"It contained nutritional information."

"It has contained the same information for six months."

Hermione's face became pink.

Harry looked away to help her retain some dignity.

Mrs Granger turned to him.

"Write," she said.

"I will."

"More than two lines."

"I wrote three once."

"One of them was your name."

"That still counts."

She gave him a look which was unmistakably Hermione's.

Harry smiled.

Mrs Granger hesitated just long enough for him to understand what she intended before she hugged him. He still did not react quickly enough to return it properly, but his arms managed to move before she released him.

"You have our address," she said. "You have the telephone number, even if you cannot use it from Hogwarts, and you know you're welcome during the holidays."

Harry nodded.

"And if your letters stop—"

"We have procedures," Hermione said.

"Eight of them," Harry added.

"Nine," Draconia corrected. "I added one."

Hermione turned. "When?"

"Last night."

"You cannot add rules without consulting us."

"The ninth rule establishes a process for adding rules."

"That is ridiculous."

"It was necessary."

"You created a rule giving yourself permission to create rules?"

"I identified a weakness in the existing system."

"You are the weakness in the existing system."

Mr Granger laughed.

Mrs Granger looked towards Draconia, who was still defending the constitutional validity of rule nine.

"May I hug you?" she asked.

Draconia stopped.

For perhaps the first time that morning, she had no immediate answer.

Her eyes flicked towards Narcissa.

Narcissa's expression did not change, but she gave the smallest nod.

"If you wish," Draconia said.

Mrs Granger embraced her.

Draconia's arms remained stiff at her sides for one startled moment. Then, carefully, she returned the hug.

"Look after one another," Mrs Granger said when she let go.

"Hermione has written that into the rules."

"Rule one," Hermione said.

"Rule two," Harry corrected. "Rule one is to check whether letters arrive."

"The order is wrong," Mrs Granger decided.

Draconia nodded gravely. "I can amend it."

Hermione made an outraged sound.

Narcissa rescued them by drawing Draconia aside.

She checked the fastening of her daughter's cloak and brushed a speck of soot from one shoulder.

"You have everything?"

"Yes."

"Your winter robes?"

"Yes."

"Gloves?"

"In the front compartment."

"The warming potion?"

"Mother."

"Answer."

"Yes."

Narcissa studied her daughter's face as if committing each feature to memory. Then she pressed a kiss to Draconia's forehead.

Draconia tolerated this with considerably more grace than Hermione had.

Lucius stood beside them, one hand resting upon the silver head of his cane. He waited until Narcissa stepped back before placing his palm briefly against Draconia's shoulder.

"Write every Sunday."

"I know."

"Not merely when you require something."

"I wrote throughout last year."

"You sent requests for books."

"They were letters containing requests for books."

"They contained no other information."

"I assumed you preferred efficiency."

Lucius gave her a look.

Draconia's composure slipped enough for Harry to see the affection beneath it.

"I'll write," she promised.

Lucius nodded.

Narcissa turned to Harry next.

He had come to recognise the particular expression she wore before deciding that his clothes required interference. He attempted to take one step backwards.

She caught his collar.

"It is crooked."

"It was fine before."

"It was crooked before."

She folded it into place, smoothed the front of his coat and then adjusted his glasses, although Harry was fairly certain they had not moved.

"There is food in Draconia's basket," she said. "You will eat."

"I was already planning to."

"Something containing more substance than sugar."

"That has made the plan considerably less appealing."

A smile touched her face. "You may have sugar as well."

"Then we have an agreement."

Her hands came to rest upon his shoulders.

For an instant, Harry thought she might hug him. Narcissa seemed to consider it too. Then, perhaps remembering how uncertain he remained when adults touched him without warning, she allowed him to decide.

Harry hesitated.

He stepped forward.

The embrace was brief. Narcissa held him carefully, one hand against the back of his head, and released him before he could become uncomfortable.

"When you write to Draconia," she said, "you may include a separate page for me."

"Oh."

"Only if you wish."

"I will."

She gave his shoulder one last squeeze before stepping away.

Lucius approached Harry after her.

He did not attempt a hug, for which Harry suspected they were both grateful.

"If something at school appears wrong," Lucius said, "write before attempting to investigate it."

"The order of those instructions seems very restrictive."

"Harry."

"I'll write."

"Before investigating?"

Harry hesitated. "I'll write quickly."

Lucius looked unconvinced, but the approaching whistle prevented further negotiation.

The first whistle rang across the platform.

Steam surged around them. Doors began slamming further down the train, and the crowd shifted as parents urged reluctant children aboard.

"We should go," Hermione said.

"You said we had forty-five minutes," Draconia reminded her.

"We did. Time passed."

"A design flaw."

They climbed into the carriage.

Harry lifted Hedwig's cage through the doorway, then returned to help with Hermione's satchel. It was much heavier than it appeared.

"What is in this?"

"Reference books."

"You have reference books in both trunks."

"These are the ones I might need during the journey."

"It's a train, Hermione, not an expedition across uncharted territory."

"The route takes nearly all day."

"It follows rails."

"That does not make knowledge unnecessary."

Harry managed to carry the bag six compartments before his arms began to ache.

Draconia noticed first.

"There," she said, indicating an empty compartment.

Hermione looked inside. "We could find one nearer the front."

"This one has seats."

"They all have seats."

"This one has empty seats."

"There might be another—"

Draconia took the satchel from Harry and placed it inside.

Hermione looked at Harry, then at the bag.

"This one is fine," she decided.

Neither mentioned that Harry had grown tired.

They loaded their smaller bags overhead. Hedwig's cage went near the window, where she immediately tucked her head beneath one wing with the long-suffering air of a creature accustomed to travelling with disorganised children.

Hermione claimed the seat facing the direction of travel.

Draconia stopped in the doorway. "Why?"

"I become ill reading backwards."

"You read for half of last year's journey while facing backwards."

"I had a headache afterwards."

"You read four hundred pages."

"That was unrelated."

"How could it possibly be unrelated?"

"The book was badly printed."

Draconia sat opposite her. "I want it recorded that I surrendered the superior seat under protest."

Hermione pulled a notebook from her bag. "Would you like me to write that down?"

"You would enjoy it too much."

Harry took the seat beside the window.

Outside, their parents stood together in an unlikely group.

Mr Granger had one hand upon Mrs Granger's shoulder. Narcissa stood beside them, pale hair bright against the steam. Lucius remained slightly apart, though not so far that the separation seemed deliberate.

For the first time in Harry's life, he had more than one person waiting to see him leave.

The thought caught him strangely.

He raised his hand.

Mrs Granger waved at once, energetic enough for several nearby parents to glance at her. Mr Granger lifted an arm above the crowd. Narcissa smiled and raised her fingers in a smaller farewell.

Lucius inclined his head.

The gesture was restrained, but it held a warmth which had not been there at the beginning of the summer.

Draconia opened the window.

"You could wave properly," she called to her father.

"I am waving."

"You are moving your head."

"It is sufficient."

"It is barely visible!"

Lucius lifted one gloved hand with immense dignity.

Draconia smiled and withdrew from the window.

"I knew he could do it."

The train gave a sudden lurch.

Hermione fell backwards into her seat. Draconia, who had not yet sat down, caught the luggage rack to steady herself.

Harry leaned from the window one last time as the platform began to move.

Or rather, the train moved. For several seconds, however, it looked as though the parents, trolleys and clouds of steam were all being drawn backwards while their compartment remained still.

Mrs Granger continued waving.

Narcissa walked beside the carriage until the train gathered speed. Lucius followed at a slower pace, one hand held aloft. The three adults blurred into the crowd, then into the steam, and finally disappeared behind the curve of the station.

Harry remained at the window until the platform was gone.

He sat back.

Hermione was watching him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You do that badly."

"You looked happy."

Harry glanced towards the corridor, where students were still searching for compartments and calling to one another.

"I am."

Draconia settled into the seat opposite. "Try not to sound so surprised."

Harry smiled.

London slipped past the window in pieces: soot-darkened walls, rows of narrow houses, washing strung between upper windows and streets crowded with cars. Gradually the buildings spread apart. Patches of green appeared between them, widening until the city gave way to fields.

For a while, the three of them were content simply to watch.

Then Hermione placed her satchel upon her lap.

Draconia eyed it with immediate suspicion.

"If you produce the communication rules, I am leaving."

"The train is moving."

"I will find another compartment."

"You would return within ten minutes."

"Why?"

"You left your lunch here."

Draconia glanced at the luggage rack.

"That is a compelling point."

Hermione reached inside the bag and withdrew a thick paperback.

Draconia relaxed. "What is that?"

"Break with a Banshee."

"You brought Lockhart onto the train?"

"We are expected to have read it."

"We are expected to own it."

"Books are generally more useful after being read."

"That depends upon the book."

Harry looked over. Hermione had inserted slips of parchment between dozens of pages. Notes covered every visible margin.

"You've finished it already?"

"I finished four of them."

Draconia stared. "There were only eight days between Diagon Alley and today."

"Yes."

"You read four entire books in eight days?"

"Five," Hermione corrected. "I began Voyages with Vampires last night."

Harry leaned closer. "Are they any good?"

Hermione's expression became complicated.

"The accounts are interesting."

"That was not his question," Draconia said.

"Some of the defensive techniques appear sound."

"Appear?"

"There are inconsistencies."

Draconia became interested despite herself. "What sort?"

"In Break with a Banshee, Lockhart claims he developed a counter-charm after three days without sleep. But the same charm appears in Gadding with Ghouls, where he says he learnt it from a warlock in Cork five years earlier."

"Perhaps he forgot."

"He also describes the moon as full on two evenings which were supposedly nine days apart."

Harry considered this. "Perhaps it was a very persistent moon."

Hermione gave him a patient look.

Draconia held out her hand. "Give me the book."

"I thought it was useless."

"I wish to assess the inconsistencies."

"You want to prove he's a fraud."

"That is a form of assessment."

Hermione surrendered it.

Draconia opened at the first marked page and began reading. Within two minutes, she had found a sentence which appeared word for word in another chapter.

Hermione immediately retrieved the second book for comparison.

Harry watched the investigation begin.

"You realise school hasn't started yet?"

Neither girl answered.

He reached into his pocket and took out a packet of Bertie Bott's Every-Flavour Beans.

Narcissa had bought it for him while insisting he eat something nutritious. Harry thought the beans counted as educational.

He selected a pale yellow one and examined it.

"What flavour?" he asked.

Hermione looked up from Lockhart's conflicting accounts. "You cannot tell by looking."

"I know. Guess."

"Lemon."

"Soap," Draconia said.

"Why soap?"

"You selected it. Your luck is inconsistent."

Harry bit into the bean.

His face tightened.

"Well?" Hermione asked.

"Wax."

Draconia looked satisfied. "Closer to soap."

"That's not how guessing works."

"It is how superior judgement works."

Harry held out the packet.

Hermione chose a green bean and received apple. Draconia selected a dark red one with the confidence of someone who expected confectionery to obey her.

She bit down.

Her expression did not change.

Harry watched her.

Hermione watched her.

Draconia swallowed with great care.

"What was it?" Harry asked.

"Cherry."

"You're lying."

"I am not."

"You look offended."

"I often look offended."

Hermione leaned across the compartment. "Let me smell it."

Draconia moved the remaining half away.

"That is an extraordinary invasion of privacy."

"It was chilli," Harry decided.

"It was not."

"Pepper."

"No."

"Beetroot," Hermione suggested.

Draconia's composure broke.

She coughed and reached for the water bottle.

Harry began laughing.

"It was chilli!"

"It was unexpectedly warm."

"Your eyes are watering."

"The train is poorly ventilated."

Hermione handed her the water while trying not to smile.

"I thought Malfoys possessed superior judgement."

"We do. The sweet was incorrectly coloured."

She drank half the bottle and refused all further beans.

The countryside unrolled beyond the glass. Sunlight moved in and out behind the clouds, turning streams briefly silver and throwing the shadows of hedgerows across the fields. They passed villages gathered around narrow church towers, farmhouses with smoke rising from their chimneys and distant hills softened by summer haze.

Students moved continually through the corridor.

A group of third-years passed while arguing about Quidditch. Two Hufflepuffs stopped to greet Hermione, then became awkward when they noticed Draconia and left more quickly than necessary. A nervous first-year opened the compartment door, apologised and closed it again before anyone could invite him inside.

When the trolley witch arrived, Narcissa's lunch basket was already open across the middle seat. It contained neatly wrapped sandwiches, cold chicken, small fruit tarts, three bottles of pumpkin juice and enough food for several additional passengers.

Hermione stared.

"How many people did your mother think would be in this compartment?"

"She believes Harry requires feeding at regular intervals."

Harry looked inside the basket. "She packed twelve sandwiches."

"There are three of us."

"I know how division works."

"She does not trust you to apply it to meals," Draconia said.

Hermione's mother had contributed cheese sandwiches, two apples, a paper packet of biscuits and a note reminding Hermione not to live entirely upon sweets.

Harry purchased three Cauldron Cakes anyway.

He did not buy half the trolley as he had the previous year. He no longer needed to prove that food could be his simply because he possessed the coins to purchase it. He chose what he wanted, handed over the correct amount without counting twice and returned the purse to his pocket.

The simplicity of it pleased him more than it should have.

They spread the food between them.

Hermione unwrapped her sandwiches and exchanged one for a slice of Narcissa's treacle tart. Draconia claimed she did not want an apple, then ate half of Harry's after he cut it. A Chocolate Frog escaped from its packet, leapt onto the window and attached itself to the glass.

Harry tried to catch it.

The frog jumped onto his head.

Hermione laughed so abruptly that pumpkin juice nearly came through her nose.

Draconia removed the frog from Harry's hair and examined it.

"It has poor survival instincts."

"It chose the person least likely to eat it," Harry said.

Draconia bit off its head.

"Incorrect."

When the wrappers had been cleared away, they played Exploding Snap upon the floor between the seats. Hermione attempted to calculate the safest placement for every card, which Draconia argued defeated the purpose of a game based upon explosions. Harry won one round by accident and lost the next when his entire hand detonated at once.

They stopped only after a card singed the end of Draconia's hair and Hedwig began screeching at them through the smoke.

The warmth of the afternoon settled over the compartment. Conversation wandered without anyone directing it. They discussed the new timetable, whether Professor Snape would be worse now that Harry had survived another summer, and which of Lockhart's portraits was most likely to fall in love with itself.

Draconia admitted she intended to try out for the Slytherin Quidditch team.

"Seeker?" Harry asked.

"Obviously."

"So we'll be playing against each other."

"I believe that is the usual arrangement."

"You haven't made the team yet."

"Neither had you before your first match."

"I caught the Snitch."

"After nearly swallowing it."

"That still counts."

"It would not have counted if you had choked."

Hermione closed her book. "Is every Quidditch conversation going to become this?"

"Yes," Harry and Draconia said together.

Hermione sighed.

Harry looked across at Draconia. "Gryffindor will win."

"Slytherin will have seven Nimbus Two Thousand and Ones."

"You said expensive equipment did not guarantee skill."

"I have never said that."

"You implied it."

"When?"

"At the shop."

"I implied that I possessed both."

Hermione reopened her book. "I hope Hufflepuff wins."

They turned towards her in mutual outrage.

The conversation became sufficiently heated that a passing prefect opened the door and asked them to keep their voices down.

Draconia waited until he left.

"Ravenclaw might be acceptable," she conceded.

Harry threw a cushion at her.

She caught it and threw it back.

Hermione attempted to stop them, received the cushion against the side of her head and joined in.

Ten minutes later, the compartment was disordered, the cushions were on the wrong seats and Harry's glasses had somehow ended up upon Hermione's face.

She pushed them higher on her nose.

"Your eyesight is dreadful."

"Thank you."

"How do you walk without these?"

"Poorly."

Draconia was attempting to smooth her hair back into place. "Both of you look ridiculous."

Harry squinted at her. Without his glasses, she was a pale blur opposite him.

"You might as well. I can't tell."

A cushion struck his chest.

Eventually the excitement wore itself out.

Hermione returned Harry's glasses and resumed reading, though she had to begin the same page three times because Draconia kept interrupting to point out another inconsistency. Draconia stretched across the opposite seat with her shoes carefully positioned so they did not touch Hermione's robes.

Harry rested his head against the window.

The steady rhythm of the train settled beneath him. Warm sunlight crossed his knees. Hermione and Draconia's voices drifted in and out around the rattle of the wheels.

He did not mean to fall asleep.

When he woke, the light had changed. The fields beyond the window were greener, the hills steeper and the sky crowded with long grey clouds.

A folded jumper had been placed between his head and the glass.

Harry lifted it.

The silver-and-green trim identified it as Draconia's.

She was pretending not to notice.

Hermione looked up from her book. "You were asleep for forty minutes."

"I gathered that."

"You snore," Draconia said.

"I do not."

"You do."

Hermione shook her head. "He doesn't."

Draconia looked betrayed. "We had agreed."

"We did not agree to lie."

Harry put his glasses straight. "What else happened while I was unconscious?"

Hermione reorganised the emergency procedures," Draconia said.

"I corrected the numbering."

"She also rejected my offer of academic cooperation."

"You attempted to bribe me with Chocolate Frog cards."

Outside, the daylight had begun to fade.

The journey's final hours passed more quietly.

They changed into their school robes in turns. Harry fastened the clasp of his new cloak and caught sight of himself in the darkening window: untidy black hair, round glasses and Gryffindor red visible at his throat. He looked older than he had on the journey north the year before.

Not greatly older.

Enough.

Hermione emerged from the corridor carrying her folded Muggle clothes and immediately corrected Harry's tie.

"It was fine."

"It was crooked."

"Narcissa fixed my collar at the station."

"She fixed your collar, not your tie."

Draconia returned moments later in Slytherin robes. She stopped when she saw Hermione's hand near Harry's neck.

Draconia crossed the compartment and flattened a section of his hair instead.

"What are you doing?"

"Completing the repair."

"My hair doesn't stay flat."

"That is because you surrender too easily."

Hermione stepped back to inspect him. "He looks presentable."

Draconia considered Harry with unnecessary seriousness. "Temporarily."

Harry looked between them. "Do I get an opinion?"

"No," they said together.

The train began to slow.

Students filled the corridor, already calling to friends and dragging robes over their clothes. Rain touched the window in scattered drops. Beyond the glass, mountains rose as dark shapes against the evening, and somewhere beyond them waited the lake, the boats and the castle Harry could not yet see.

Excitement stirred through the carriage.

Harry felt it too.

Whatever worries had followed him through the final weeks of summer had not vanished. They remained somewhere at the edge of thought, waiting for quiet.

For now, there was no quiet.

There was Hermione attempting to fit three books into a satchel already too full to close. There was Draconia insisting that her hair had become unacceptable after the cushion fight. There was Hedwig objecting to the movement of her cage and younger students calling to one another in the corridor.

Harry helped Hermione force the buckle of her bag closed. Draconia collected the remaining Chocolate Frogs before they could be crushed beneath the seats. There was a castle ahead of them and an entire year they had not yet managed to complicate.
