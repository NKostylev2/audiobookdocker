1

By morning, Harry had managed forty-seven minutes of sleep.

He had watched the luminous hands of Dudley's discarded alarm clock throughout the night as they crossed two, then three, then four. At some point after half past five, exhaustion dragged him under without permission. At seventeen minutes past six, Aunt Petunia struck the bedroom door with the flat of her hand.

"Up."

Harry opened his eyes.

Daylight filled the narrow gap between the curtains. It was thin and colourless, nothing like the green glare which still lingered at the edge of his dream, yet his heart had already begun beating too quickly. He lay without moving while the room arranged itself around him.

His splintering wardrobe. Dudley's old desk. The window without its bars, and the closed door. No damp stone. No carved serpents. Nothing breathing beneath the floor.

"Did you hear me?"

A second blow rattled the frame.

Harry cleared his throat. "The first time was surprisingly effective."

Silence followed.

He could picture Petunia on the landing, thin lips compressed and one hand still raised against the wood. She had never decided what to do with politeness when it carried an insult.

"You have five minutes."

"That seems generous."

"Four."

Harry pushed himself upright. "There it is."

Her footsteps retreated towards the stairs. He remained seated until they disappeared.

The letter he had been reading during the night rested beside his pillow. Draconia's handwriting crossed the page in narrow black lines, the last paragraph silvered by the light entering through the curtains.

I dreamt about the Chamber again.

Harry folded the letter before the words could draw him back towards the cold beneath Hogwarts. He returned it to the bundle under his mattress, then stood carefully.

The room tilted. He caught the bedpost and waited for the dizziness to pass.

That had begun three days ago. Harry hadn't decided whether it came from exhaustion, heat or the reduced portions Aunt Petunia served whenever Uncle Vernon believed Harry had been insufficiently grateful. None of the possibilities seemed urgent enough to mention in a letter.

He dressed in one of Dudley's old shirts and a pair of jeans which had become nearly short enough to fit. His trainers waited beneath the desk, their soles separating near the toes. When he bent to tie them, pain pulled briefly through his right forearm.

Harry stopped.

The beatings had stopped but his arm still ached some mornings. Harry rubbed the place once and pulled down his sleeve.

The landing smelled of furniture polish. Petunia had already opened every curtain, and sunlight lay across the carpet in clean, rectangular sections. Family photographs occupied the walls in carefully spaced frames: Dudley wearing his first school uniform, Dudley holding a football, Dudley seated upon a pony which appeared seconds from collapse.

Harry was not included. He no longer paused to look.

The cupboard beneath the stairs stood locked. Vernon had placed Harry's trunk, broomstick, cauldron and schoolbooks inside it on the first evening of the holidays. His wand had followed, despite three separate letters explaining that Harry could not legally perform magic outside school and therefore presented no threat unless someone attempted to murder him in the kitchen.

Vernon had read none of them. He had held the wand at arm's length, dropped it into Harry's trunk and slammed the lid.

"You won't be needing that unnatural thing here."

"I didn't need bars on my window last summer either," Harry had replied. "Life can be unpredictable."

Vernon had gone purple. The argument ended with a lock placed upon the cupboard and Harry assigned two days of garden work. Considering the possible alternatives, Harry counted it as a draw.

The only concession the Dursleys had maintained concerned Hedwig. She was allowed out twice a week, provided she left after dark, returned before morning and made no noise that might attract the neighbours. The arrangement had not emerged from a sudden respect for Harry's correspondence.

It had emerged from Lucius Malfoy standing in the Dursleys' sitting room at the end of June.

Lucius had not raised his voice. He had not drawn his wand or threatened anyone in language the Grangers could object to. He had merely stood beside the fireplace, silver-headed cane resting between both hands, and explained that preventing Harry from communicating with the magical world would be regarded as an act of deliberate endangerment.

Narcissa had watched Petunia throughout the conversation with such cold attention that Petunia eventually stopped offering tea she had never intended anyone to drink.

Mr Granger had been less elegant.

"You locked a twelve-year-old behind bars," he had said. "I don't particularly care which world deals with it next time."

Mrs Granger had touched his arm, but she had not asked him to withdraw the statement. The Dursleys had agreed to the letters. They had not agreed pleasantly.

Harry descended to the kitchen.

Dudley occupied his usual chair, filling it with the stubborn completeness of an object designed for a larger purpose. He wore a red football shirt stretched tightly across his stomach and was eating bacon directly from the serving plate while Petunia stood at the stove. Vernon sat behind the morning newspaper, one hand curled around a mug of tea.

A single piece of toast waited at Harry's place. He sat and reached for it.

"Hands," Petunia said.

Harry examined them. "Still attached."

"Wash them."

"I did."

"You have dirt beneath your nails."

"That's probably because you had me digging yesterday."

Petunia turned from the stove. "Do not use that tone."

Harry considered asking which tone she meant. Experience suggested she would identify whichever one he used next. He went to the sink.

Cold water ran over his fingers. The soap smelled sharply of lemons and left the skin around his knuckles tight. Behind him, Dudley took a fourth rasher of bacon.

"Mum, he's staring at me."

Harry dried his hands. "I was wondering whether you can chew."

"See?"

"Harry," Petunia warned.

"I've stopped wondering."

Dudley scowled. His face had grown broader during the year, though not noticeably older. Without the school uniform and the gang of boys who followed him around Little Whinging, he sometimes looked younger than Harry remembered: not gentler, precisely, but less certain of the size he was meant to occupy in the world.

He recovered quickly.

"At least I'm allowed bacon."

Harry sat again. "A devastating argument."

"What did you say?"

"I said you've won."

Dudley narrowed his eyes, sensing mockery but unable to locate it.

Vernon lowered one corner of the newspaper. "What's this about winning?"

"Nothing, Dad."

"Good."

The paper rose.

Harry ate his toast. It had been spread with margarine so thinly that the centre remained dry. He chewed slowly, partly to make it last and partly because his stomach had not yet accepted that morning required food.

Petunia slid eggs onto Vernon's plate, added bacon and then placed two sausages beside Dudley.

Harry looked at the serving dish. One sausage remained. Petunia carried it to the counter and tipped it into the bin. The movement was casual. She did not look at him.

Harry lowered his eyes to the crust in his hand. He had spent years learning not to react to food being removed. Hunger annoyed the Dursleys only when it became visible. Asking for more proved greed. Looking disappointed proved ingratitude. Eating quickly suggested poor manners, while eating slowly allowed Vernon to accuse him of wasting everyone's time.

The safest response was none at all.

Harry swallowed the final bite and wiped the crumbs from his fingers.

"Finished?" Petunia asked.

"Apparently."

Her eyes sharpened, but Vernon turned a page loudly before she could answer.

"Front path needs sweeping," he said. "Then the hedge."

"I did the hedge on Monday."

"It's grown."

"In three days?"

"It's untidy."

Harry looked through the window. The hedge stood in a rigid green line outside, every surface clipped flat enough to qualify as architecture.

"I'll speak to it."

Vernon's newspaper lowered.

"Boy."

"Politely."

"You will cut the hedge, wash the car and clear the weeds beside the shed. Your aunt is not going to arrive here and find the place looking neglected."

Harry's fingers stopped against the table.

"Aunt Marge is coming?"

Vernon's face brightened with the particular pride he reserved for his sister, expensive drills and stories in which he had defeated a parking attendant.

"She'll be staying for a full week."

Dudley grinned.

Petunia placed a bowl of scrambled eggs on the table. "Her train arrives tomorrow afternoon."

Tomorrow.

Harry had known Marge intended to visit at some point during the summer. Vernon had mentioned it in June, then again in early July, without providing dates. Harry had allowed himself to hope the visit might happen after his return to Hogwarts.

A week.

Aunt Marge could accomplish a remarkable amount of cruelty in seven days.

She disliked Harry with the wholehearted confidence of someone who knew almost nothing about him. During previous visits, she had blamed his size, his manners and his parents upon a defective bloodline. She spoke about breeding constantly, having built her understanding of human behaviour from bulldogs and the conviction that both species required frequent discipline.

"When was anyone going to tell me?" Harry asked.

"We're telling you now," Petunia replied.

"Less than thirty-six hours beforehand."

"You don't have plans."

"That isn't the point."

Vernon folded the newspaper with dangerous care. "You will be respectful while Marge is under my roof."

Harry looked towards the ceiling.

"Oh thanks for letting me know we still had a roof."

Dudley snorted into his eggs.

Vernon's moustache moved.

"There will be no cheek. No unnatural behaviour. No mention of that school."

"Hogwarts."

"I know what it is called."

"That must have be painful."

Petunia set the spatula down.

"Harry."

The warning contained more fear than anger.

He noticed because he had become good at noticing fear in adults. Petunia's gaze had flicked towards the kitchen window and then the ceiling, as if expecting an owl, a Malfoy or an entire delegation of furious magical parents to materialise whenever Harry was mistreated.

The knowledge should have felt comforting. Instead, it made Harry tired.

Nothing the Grangers or Malfoys said could change what the Dursleys were when the doors closed. It could only change how carefully they concealed it.

Harry pushed back his chair.

"I'll do the hedge."

"You'll do everything your uncle said."

"I was hoping to leave one weed as a memorial."

"Out."

He carried his plate towards the sink.

Petunia intercepted it before he reached the counter. "Leave that. You'll chip it."

Harry looked at the plain white plate. He had washed it hundreds of times without incident.

"All right."

He walked into the hall.

"Boy."

Vernon's voice stopped him beside the cupboard. Harry turned.

"No funny business while your aunt is here."

"I wasn't planning a comedy show."

"You know what I mean."

Unfortunately, Harry did.

His magic had become unpredictable since the Chamber. It didn't happen often. Just a cupboard door opening when he woke from a nightmare. A glass cracking beneath Dudley's hand after he called Draconia a name Harry refused to repeat. Once, every light in the house went out when a lorry changed gears outside and the sound became stone grinding open.

Harry had repaired or concealed what he could. The Ministry had not written. No owl had arrived accusing him of underage sorcery, perhaps because none of the incidents involved a wand. That did not mean another episode would go unnoticed.

"I can't promise anything," he said.

Vernon stood.

The chair legs scraped against the tiles.

"You had better promise."

"Magic doesn't always ask me first."

"Control it."

"I'll explain your policy."

Vernon advanced two steps. His face had begun reddening around the neck. Harry's body reacted before thought. His shoulders locked. His weight shifted backwards. One hand closed at his side, reaching for the wand which remained behind a locked door.

Vernon saw the movement.

He stopped.

Neither acknowledged why.

Lucius Malfoy had stood in this hall only weeks before. Vernon might not understand curses, but he understood threats, and Lucius had delivered his with the polished certainty of a man accustomed to being obeyed.

Vernon pointed towards the back door instead.

"Get outside."

Harry went. Only after the door shut behind him did his fingers begin to shake. He shoved both hands into his pockets and crossed the garden before anyone could see.

The heat gathered on the roofs of Privet Drive, turned pavements pale beneath the sun and pressed every smell close to the earth. Freshly cut grass mixed with petrol from a mower several houses away. Roses climbed trellises in disciplined rows. A sprinkler clicked across someone's lawn, releasing brief silver fans which vanished almost as soon as they touched the ground.

Harry worked beside the front hedge.

The shears were old and stiff. Each cut jarred through his wrists, dropping small green fragments across his trainers. Petunia had drawn a line with string to demonstrate the exact height she expected, though the hedge already differed from it by less than an inch.

Harry suspected she had measured. Behind him, Privet Drive conducted its usual morning performance.

Mr Parsons washed the same silver car he had polished on Tuesday. Mrs Kent carried washing into her garden, reconsidered the clouds and carried it inside again. Two children rode bicycles in slow circles near the pavement while their mother shouted instructions from an open window.

Nobody looked twice at Harry.

That was the triumph of streets like Privet Drive. Curtains remained clean, grass remained short and anything unpleasant happened quietly enough to preserve property values.

Harry raised the shears. The metal scraped. His pulse jumped.

For one second, he no longer held garden tools. He held Godric Gryffindor's sword. Silver flashed beneath green light. A vast jaw opened above him, dripping venom.

The shears struck the hedge crookedly. Harry released them, dropping them onto the grass with a dull thud. He stood staring at the clipped leaves.

Nothing moved inside them.

His breath had caught high in his chest. He forced it lower, counting the way Madam Pomfrey had taught him after he woke in the hospital wing unable to understand why Draconia was alive.

Four seconds in. Hold. Six seconds out.

The first attempt failed halfway through. The second reached five. By the fourth, he could feel the ground beneath his shoes again.

A bicycle bell rang in the street. Harry flinched less violently than before. He crouched and retrieved the shears.

"Nearly defeated by shrubbery?"

The voice came from beyond the hedge. Harry looked through a narrow gap in the leaves.

Mrs Figg stood upon the pavement in a faded purple dress, two shopping bags hanging from one arm. Her grey hair had been pulled into a loose knot which listed towards her left ear. A net bag of oranges pressed against one hip. The top of a cat's head protruded from the other bag.

Harry blinked.

"Is that a cat?"

Mr Tibbles lifted his face between a packet of tea and a tin of sardines. He regarded Harry with yellow-eyed disapproval.

"He didn't want to remain at home," Mrs Figg said.

"So you took him shopping?"

"He took himself. I found him in the greengrocer's."

Mr Tibbles sneezed.

Harry set the shears down. "Did he buy anything?"

"He disapproves of their fish."

"That isn't a no."

Mrs Figg shifted the bags. "You look dreadful."

"Good morning to you too."

"Have you been sleeping?"

"Yes."

It came automatically. Mrs Figg looked at him.

Harry had known her for most of his life. The Dursleys sent him to her house whenever they wanted an outing which would be improved by his absence. His childhood memories of those visits contained stale cake, photographs of cats and cabbage boiled beyond recognition.

She had always seemed harmlessly peculiar.

During the past year, Harry had begun to suspect the peculiarity required more effort than he had realised.

"You have shadows under your eyes," she said.

"I have eyes under my shadows."

"That is not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be alarming either."

Mrs Figg studied him through the hedge. Unlike Petunia, she did not dart her gaze away when he noticed.

"Wait there."

"Why?"

She continued towards her own house.

"Mrs Figg?"

No answer came.

Harry considered returning to the shears. Petunia was watching from the kitchen; he could feel it without turning. Speaking with neighbours during chores usually qualified as deliberate laziness.

Two minutes later, Mrs Figg reappeared carrying a glass. Condensation coated its sides. Ice cubes floated beside a slice of lemon.

Harry stared at it.

"Take it."

He reached through the hedge. The lemonade was cold enough to hurt his fingers. He drank too quickly, sweetness and sharp citrus striking the back of his throat.

"Slowly," Mrs Figg said.

Harry lowered the glass. He had emptied half of it.

"Sorry."

"Why are you apologising?"

"I nearly drank all your—"

"I brought it for you."

"Oh."

Mrs Figg's expression tightened.

Harry finished more carefully. The ice clicked against his teeth.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

Mr Tibbles pushed one paw over the edge of the bag.

Harry looked at him. "Is he meant to be doing that?"

"He is rarely meant to be doing anything."

The cat climbed onto Mrs Figg's shoulder with the confidence of an animal unconcerned by gravity. His tail passed across her face. She moved it aside without comment.

"When do you go back to school?" she asked.

"First of September."

"That's weeks away."

Harry looked towards the house.

"Twenty-nine days."

"You're counting."

"No."

Mrs Figg waited.

"Not deliberately," he amended.

"You calculated rather quickly."

"There isn't much else to do."

A mower began farther along the road, its engine coughing twice before settling into a steady roar. Harry's grip tightened around the glass. He concentrated upon the lemon slice drifting near the surface until his body accepted the noise.

Mrs Figg watched.

"Your friends write?"

"Yes."

"Both of them?"

Harry looked sharply at her.

Mrs Figg adjusted the shopping bag. "The clever girl and the blond one."

"You know about Draconia?"

"Half the street knows about the blond one. Her father arrived carrying a silver stick and frightened Vernon so badly he took the bins out a day early."

Harry smiled despite himself.

"It's a cane."

"It's pretentious."

"He'd probably agree."

"No, he wouldn't."

"No," Harry admitted. "He really wouldn't."

Mr Tibbles turned suddenly. His yellow eyes fixed upon the opposite pavement. Harry followed his gaze. A large black dog stood beneath the shade of a horse-chestnut tree.

It had not been there seconds before.

The animal was too thin. Its dark coat hung unevenly around a narrow body, and one ear appeared torn near the tip. It did not sniff the pavement or pull towards any particular scent. It simply watched Harry through the bright morning.

"Is that someone's dog?" he asked.

Mrs Figg turned.

The space beneath the tree was empty.

"What dog?"

Harry stepped closer to the hedge. "There was a black dog across the road."

Mr Tibbles continued staring at the tree. His back had risen beneath his fur.

"I didn't see it," Mrs Figg said.

"It was right there."

"Loose dogs don't last long around here. Someone usually complains to the council before they've crossed two gardens."

Harry examined the pavement. Sunlight moved through the leaves, laying broken patterns across the concrete. No gate swung nearby. No hedge stirred.

The dog could not have disappeared that quickly.

"Was it large?" Mrs Figg asked.

Harry looked at her. The question had come too quickly.

"Very."

Her fingers tightened around the shopping bag. "Probably a stray."

"You just said there aren't any strays."

"I said they don't last long."

"That's comforting."

Mr Tibbles made a low sound. Not a hiss. Something quieter, directed towards the place where the dog had stood.

Mrs Figg shifted him from her shoulder. "I should get these things inside."

Harry handed back the empty glass.

"Thanks."

She accepted it, but did not leave immediately.

"If anything unusual happens, you come and find me."

Harry glanced towards the cat, the lemonade and the house beyond her shoulder.

"How unusual?"

"Use your judgement."

"That seems risky."

"Harry."

Her tone changed. He stopped smiling.

Mrs Figg looked older in the full sunlight. Worry deepened the lines beside her mouth, and for one strange instant Harry had the impression that they were discussing something neither was allowed to name.

"I mean it," she said.

"All right."

"Day or night."

"Okay."

Mrs Figg nodded.

Mr Tibbles remained turned towards the opposite pavement as she walked away.

Harry waited until her gate closed. Then he pushed apart two branches and looked beneath the horse-chestnut again.

Nothing.

He returned to the shears.

From the kitchen window, Petunia tapped one finger against the glass and pointed meaningfully towards the unfinished hedge.

Harry lifted the blades in salute.

Petunia disappeared.

He resumed cutting.

The first letter had arrived four days after Harry returned to Privet Drive.

Hermione's owl came shortly after breakfast, diving through the narrow kitchen window with such speed that Vernon abandoned his chair and Dudley shielded his plate. The bird deposited a thick envelope beside Harry's hand, stole half a sausage and escaped before anyone recovered.

Harry had carried the letter outside and opened it beneath the shed roof while rain drummed upon the garden.

Hermione filled five pages.

The first two concerned the end-of-year feast and whether Professor McGonagall should have awarded additional points for destroying a dangerous magical artefact. The third contained a careful account of her recovery from Petrification, which consisted mostly of complaints about the hospital wing and one brief sentence admitting that waking to discover weeks had passed still frightened her.

The fourth page asked about Harry.

Not casually.

Most were questions she had asked before. Harry could hear her insisting that he answer them properly this time.

Is your arm still hurting? Madam Pomfrey said your injuries had healed, but you ought to tell her if you're still in pain.

Are you still having nightmares? You can tell me. And please don't just write that you're fine.

Are they giving you enough to eat? Mum says I should ask that more politely, but I don't know how.

And how are you? I don't just mean whether your arm's better.

At the bottom of the fifth page, her handwriting became smaller.

I know returning there must feel worse after everything. Mum and Dad have said you may stay with us whenever Professor Dumbledore says the protection allows it. Dad is still angry about the bars. He pretends he isn't because Mum says he frightens the patients when he talks about your uncle.

Please write soon.

Love from,

Hermione

Harry had stared at the final two words for longer than he intended.

She wrote it so easily. Harry had seen it on every letter she sent, and still sometimes checked that it was there before reading anything else.

Harry read the letter three times before answering.

His first reply was almost honest.

He said his arm hurt occasionally. He admitted that he had woken twice believing he was back beneath the school. He reported breakfast in enough detail to satisfy her and did not mention that Petunia had served it only because Lucius might ask.

He stopped before answering the general question.

How are you?

Harry dipped his quill into the small bottle of ink he had hidden behind a loose board in the shed. One drop fell onto the parchment, spreading into a black circle.

He did not know how to describe the space between surviving and feeling alive.

In the end, he wrote:

Mostly tired, but I'm all right.

It was not evidence.

Hermione would notice.

Her next letter arrived two days later with a paragraph explaining that tiredness persisting after sufficient rest could indicate several medical problems. Harry responded by claiming that Dudley's snoring shook the walls.

That was true enough to make the lie feel less dishonest.

Draconia's next letter came the following evening.

Her owl was large, pale and personally offended by Privet Drive. It refused to land upon Harry's windowsill until he cleared away a dead moth, then held out one leg while looking past him into the room with visible disdain.

The envelope was made from heavy cream parchment.

Only one word appeared upon it.

Potter.

Inside, Draconia had written three lines before crossing them out. The fourth attempt began:

Potter,

Are you sleeping?

Do not answer "enough." You employ that word whenever the truthful measurement would concern people.

Harry had laughed. Then he reached the next paragraph.

I haven't been, if it makes you feel better.

The words stood alone. Nothing followed them for half a page.

Draconia eventually resumed in her usual sharper manner, describing Pansy's attempt to learn what happened inside the Chamber and Lucius's increasingly creative methods of removing reporters from the Manor gates. She did not return to the admission.

Harry understood it anyway. He had spent his first weeks at Hogwarts believing Draconia Malfoy did not frighten easily. She disliked uncertainty, incompetence and losing arguments. Fear seemed too undignified to interest her.

Then he had found her lying beneath the stone face of Salazar Slytherin.

Afterwards, Harry learned the difference between being unafraid and refusing to let terror choose one's posture.

He answered Draconia after midnight.

Draco,

I'm not sleeping much either.

He nearly tore the page after writing it.

The confession felt too direct. It asked for something Harry could not name and did not know whether he deserved. His quill hovered above the parchment while Hedwig waited upon the desk, feathers puffed with impatience.

"Don't look at me like that."

Hedwig clicked her beak.

"You don't know what it says."

Her stare suggested that literacy was unnecessary.

Harry continued.

Sometimes it's the Chamber. Sometimes it's other things. I don't know which is worse.

He considered mentioning his parents. Instead, he wrote about Vernon's attempts to keep magic from occurring by forbidding it verbally.

For the first fortnight, Harry's letters became the structure of his summer.

Hedwig flew every Tuesday and Saturday. On Wednesdays, Hermione's owl arrived shortly before noon. Draconia's came unpredictably, often after midnight and once during Vernon's attempt to impress a visiting colleague in the garden. That delivery cost Harry an evening meal and earned the owl a lifelong hatred of lawn sprinklers.

Harry wrote about Privet Drive as if it were an unusually boring holiday destination.

He described Dudley's campaign to avoid exercise, Petunia's war against a dandelion growing near the fence and Vernon's outrage when a neighbour purchased a newer car. He told Hermione that the local library had reduced its summer hours, knowing this would produce at least two pages of righteous fury. He sent Draconia a detailed account of Vernon becoming trapped in a folding garden chair.

The responses made him laugh. Some evenings, that was enough. Then the nightmares grew worse.

Harry began waking before dawn with his scar burning and the taste of smoke in his mouth. He heard voices beneath running water. Whenever Petunia opened the cupboard under the stairs, stale darkness breathed through the hall and his chest tightened until he left the room.

None of the memories arrived separately.

Tom Riddle stood in the Dursleys' kitchen with Voldemort's red eyes. The basilisk moved through the walls at Godric's Hollow. Lily screamed Draconia's name. Hermione lay Petrified upon Harry's old cupboard mattress while spiders fled past her open eyes.

Sleep ceased to provide rest.

By the middle of July, Harry found it difficult to begin anything. His schoolbooks remained locked away, but he still had parchment and two novels Hermione sent through Muggle post. He placed one beside his bed and read the same page for four consecutive nights without remembering a word.

He stopped taking his broom maintenance notes from beneath the mattress.

He stopped counting how many days had passed since Hogwarts and counted only how many remained until his return.

His replies changed.

Not obviously. Harry had spent too much of his childhood shaping answers around what adults wanted to hear. He knew how to keep a voice light and a sentence ordinary.

Sleeping better.

I'm fine.

Food's terrible, but there's plenty of it.

Nothing interesting happening here.

Each statement contained enough truth to survive examination.

He occasionally slept for several uninterrupted hours once exhaustion became absolute.

There was plenty of food inside Number Four. Most of it simply belonged to someone else.

Hermione noticed first.

You told me your body no longer hurt.

Draconia says you told her it aches sometimes.

Harry read the sentence at the breakfast table and nearly spilled his tea.

He continued.

Before you become annoyed, we did not discuss you deliberately. You told us two different things, which made the subject difficult to avoid.

Beneath that, squeezed into the margin:

She says your lies require organisational improvement. I say you should stop lying.

Harry folded the letter and pressed it against his forehead. Across the table, Dudley looked up from a comic.

"What are you doing?"

"Receiving criticism."

"From who?"

"People with standards."

Dudley returned to the comic, apparently satisfied the remark did not concern him. Draconia's letter arrived that night.

Potter,

It may surprise you to learn that Hermione and I communicate when you are not present. Most people understand that their friends continue existing outside their direct observation, but allowances must be made.

Your body either hurts or it does not.

You are either sleeping or you are not.

Choose the correct answers.

Harry took out fresh parchment.

Draco,

My body hurts sometimes. I sleep sometimes. Glad we solved that.

He hesitated, then added:

Do you and Hermione always compare everything I say?

Hedwig watched from her cage. Harry read the sentence again.

"That doesn't sound worried, does it?"

Hedwig tucked her head beneath one wing.

"Helpful."

Draconia reply answered the question directly.

Only when you are suspicious, and you are frequently suspicious.

Below that, in smaller writing:

I told her about the Chamber because she asked why I no longer sleep with the bed curtains closed. I did not provide details. Do not make this important.

Harry sat at his desk with the letter between his hands.

The Malfoy Manor bedrooms were enormous. Harry remembered Draconia's room from the night they searched for the diary's origin after returning from Hogwarts: pale walls, tall windows and green curtains surrounding a four-poster bed large enough to occupy most of Harry's bedroom by itself.

Draconia no longer closed those curtains. The Chamber had not ended for her when Harry destroyed the diary.

It lived in fabric drawn across the light. It existed in blank pages and missing seconds. It waited inside sleep.

Harry lowered his quill.

It is important.

He crossed the words out.

Then he wrote:

All right.

That was the last honest thing he sent for six days. The school owl arrived on Harry's thirteenth birthday.

He woke to tapping at the window and reached instinctively for Hedwig's cage before remembering she was still out hunting. The sound continued, brisk and impatient.

A barn owl balanced upon the sill outside. One foot gripped a thick envelope; the other struck the glass repeatedly.

Harry opened the window.

The owl entered, circled the room once and dropped the envelope onto his pillow. It accepted a piece of stale biscuit in payment, swallowed without enthusiasm and departed.

Harry closed the window quickly.

Uncle Vernon had not yet left for work. The sight of an unfamiliar owl leaving Harry's room on a weekday would produce an investigation, a lecture and possibly a new set of nails.

The envelope bore the Hogwarts crest.

Harry sat upon the bed and opened it.

The first page contained the term's book list. Most of the titles appeared ordinary until he reached The Monster Book of Monsters, which had been underlined twice and accompanied by a warning to exercise caution.

The second page announced that third-year students would be permitted to visit the village of Hogsmeade on selected weekends.

Harry read the paragraph twice.

He had heard older students talk about Hogsmeade throughout the previous year. It was the only entirely magical village in Britain. Hermione wanted to visit Honeydukes and claimed this interest was historical rather than confectionery. Draconia had described the Three Broomsticks as acceptable, which Harry understood to mean she liked it.

A permission form had been attached beneath the announcement.

The undersigned parent or guardian grants permission—

Harry's excitement faded.

He stared at the blank line.

The form required Vernon's signature.

That should not have surprised him. Hogwarts knew Petunia was Lily's sister and that the Dursleys were legally responsible for him. Professor McGonagall could hardly be expected to recognise that asking Vernon to grant Harry pleasure was equivalent to asking a dragon to donate its hoard.

Still, disappointment arrived with embarrassing force.

Harry folded the form and placed it beneath his pillow.

He would ask later.

Perhaps Vernon would sign if Harry presented it when no one was angry. Perhaps Petunia might convince him merely to avoid another argument about magic. Perhaps the knowledge that Harry would be away from the castle for several weekends would appeal to Vernon's belief that Hogwarts itself suffered whenever Harry remained inside it.

The possibilities were poor.

They were not nonexistent.

Another tap sounded at the window.

Harry turned.

"No."

"Then there isn't an agreement."

Vernon stood. Harry remained where he was. They faced each other across the low table. The form lay between them, insignificant in size and suddenly large enough to divide the room.

"You do not dictate terms to me."

"You want something from me."

"I want basic gratitude."

"You want me to lie for a week and let your sister say whatever she likes."

Vernon's jaw worked. Harry continued before caution returned.

"I want a signature. That sounds like a negotiation."

Dudley had stopped smiling. Petunia rose from the sofa.

"Marge cannot know."

Harry looked towards her.

"Know what?"

"About that place."

"Hogwarts."

She flinched at the name.

"Do not say it while she is here."

"She thinks I'm at a criminal school."

"Then let her."

Harry stared at his aunt. The lamplight sharpened her thin features. Her eyes were not the same green as Lily's. Harry had searched for the resemblance when he was younger, hoping to discover some trace of his mother in the only surviving relative he knew. If it existed, Petunia had spent years teaching her face to hide it.

"She'll insult them, you know it." he said.

Petunia understood immediately. Her gaze dropped.

"They aren't here to hear it."

"I am."

The room fell quiet. Vernon looked impatient rather than moved. Dudley shifted upon the sofa. Petunia's throat worked once.

"Keep control of yourself," she said.

Harry almost laughed. The answer contained everything wrong with the house. Cruelty entered through the door, sat at their table and drank their brandy. Harry remained responsible for ensuring it caused no disturbance.

He looked at the form again.

"Sign it when she leaves," he said. "No new conditions."

Vernon nodded.

"If you behave."

"I want you to say it."

Vernon's face darkened.

"Say what?"

"That if I do what you asked for the entire week, you will sign the Hogsmeade form before I go back to school."

"Don't be absurd."

"Then no deal."

Harry reached for the parchment. Vernon placed his hand upon it. The paper vanished beneath his palm.

"All right," he said through his teeth. "If you behave properly for the duration of Marge's visit, I will sign your form."

"No new conditions."

"No new conditions."

Harry released the page. Vernon folded it once and put it inside the drawer of the side table.

"I'll keep this."

"Of course."

"Upstairs."

Harry turned towards the door.

"And Harry?"

He stopped.

"One incident," Vernon said. "One piece of your unnatural nonsense, one rude answer, one attempt to upset my sister—and the form goes in the bin."

Harry looked over his shoulder.

"That was almost a new condition."

Vernon's face turned purple. Harry left before it became one.

Upstairs, the smallest bedroom held the trapped heat of the day. He opened the window and sat upon the sill, one foot resting against the wall.

The street outside had grown quiet.

Curtains glowed behind the windows opposite. A moth battered itself against the streetlamp. Mr Parsons's car waited in the drive, polished brightly enough to reflect a distorted copy of the moon.

Harry's birthday presents remained arranged across his desk. Hermione's broom kit stood open, its brass tools shining. Draconia's silver potion case lay beside it. Hagrid's book growled occasionally beneath the bed, restrained now by two belts and an old school tie.

Harry picked up Draconia's letter.

He did not read it.

He knew the final line by memory.

I miss talking to you.

Tomorrow, Marge would arrive.

For seven days, Harry would need to conceal Hogwarts, magic and everyone he cared about. He would return to the version of himself the Dursleys understood: silent, unobtrusive and grateful for whatever remained after Dudley had finished.

After that, Vernon would sign the form and Harry would return to school.

He would board the Hogwarts Express with Hermione and Draconia. They would argue over compartments, books and whatever rules Hermione had constructed for the new term. Hogwarts would rise across the lake. The Great Hall would fill with candles, and Harry would no longer be alone inside a house determined to forget him.

He held the thought carefully. It should have made the coming week feel smaller.

Hogwarts no longer existed in Harry's mind as an uncomplicated escape. The castle contained the corridor where Hermione had fallen. It contained the bathroom where Draconia found the diary, the hospital bed where Colin Creevey lay rigid and the tunnel Harry had entered believing he might never leave.

Hogwarts was home. But Hogwarts had nearly killed everyone he loved. Both facts lived beside each other now, impossible to separate.

Harry closed his eyes.

His body stiffened, but he did not move from the window. He listened until the sound ended.

When he opened his eyes, the black dog was sitting at the mouth of Privet Drive. This time, it was closer.

Moonlight silvered the rough edges of its coat. Its ribs rose and fell beneath the fur, proving it was not a statue or a trick of darkness. The dog watched Number Four with a stillness no ordinary stray possessed.

Harry leaned beyond the window frame.

"You again."

The animal's ears lifted. Harry glanced towards the road. Nothing moved between them.

"Are you lost?"

The dog tilted its head.

"That makes two of us."

Harry did not know why he said it. The words left before he could make them into a joke.

For several seconds, neither moved. The dog's eyes caught the streetlight. They were dark, intelligent and fixed upon Harry with an intensity that should have frightened him.

It did frighten him. Yet the fear was complicated by an impression he could not explain. The dog did not appear to be waiting for Harry to come outside. It appeared to be keeping watch.

A door opened farther down the street. Laughter spilled from a house, followed by a man calling goodnight to someone Harry could not see.

The dog stepped backwards.

"Wait."

It turned. Within three silent strides, it crossed behind a parked car. Harry expected it to emerge from the other side.

It did not. He remained at the window long after the street returned to stillness.

Below him, Vernon locked the front door and checked it twice. Petunia began arranging breakfast dishes for Marge's arrival. Dudley shouted at his new game. The familiar sounds rose through Number Four and settled around Harry.
