The Philosophers' Stone

If being a wizard had taught Harry anything so far, it was not to tell anyone else his name.

He pushed open the door to Madam Malkin's, hoping he could buy robes without introductions. The bell above him gave a tired little jangle, and the clamor of Diagon Alley softened as the door swung shut. Nobody hurried over to shake his hand. For the moment, that was enough to make him like the shop.

Through the glass, owls called from shaded windows and cauldrons knocked together outside a shop farther down the street. People discussed broomsticks, spellbooks, and dragon-liver as casually as Aunt Petunia discussed groceries over the garden fence. Inside, the air smelled of warm cloth and starch. A brass iron moved slowly along the counter, trailing steam over a sleeve, while bolts of fabric stood against the walls in crowded ranks: dark wool beside velvet of green, blue and plum. Harry kept close to the door, reluctant to touch anything that might turn out to be expensive or alive.

It had been a morning of being hurried from one impossible thing to another: a brick wall that opened when Hagrid tapped it, a bank run by goblins, a vault containing more gold than Harry could properly understand. Strangers had known his name before he spoke. Some had caught his hand in both of theirs; others had bent to look at his forehead. He was still trying to take in the news that he was a wizard when everyone around him appeared to have finished deciding what sort.

The robe shop seemed to ask nothing of him except that he stand still. He hoped he might be good at that.

A squat witch in mauve robes emerged from behind a curtain, a tape measure draped around her neck. She took two pins from between her lips and tucked them into the cushion at her wrist.

"Hogwarts?" she asked, with the comfortable certainty of someone who had fitted several dozen nervous children already that week.

Harry nodded.

"First year?"

"Yes."

"Up you get, then."

She indicated a low wooden stool. Harry climbed onto it, uncertain what to do with his hands, and a measuring tape sprang from the counter. It wrapped itself around his chest before he could flinch, tightened, loosened and flew down one arm. He looked towards the witch for an explanation, but she was already drawing a black robe over his head. Apparently, being measured by something with no hands was an ordinary part of buying clothes.

"Stand straight, dear."

Harry straightened. The tape tugged his wrist as though he had done it incorrectly, then stretched down to his ankle. Beyond him, the curtain dividing the fitting area from the back of the shop shifted, revealing a second stool and three tall mirrors.

A girl stood on the stool, already dressed in half-finished black robes. She was about Harry's age, though her raised chin and careful stillness made her look older. A narrow green ribbon held her pale hair away from her face. Pins moved about the hem near her polished shoes, and she watched their progress with the expression Aunt Petunia wore when inspecting someone else's dusting. Unlike Harry, she seemed to know precisely where her hands belonged.

Her gaze found him in the mirror. Harry looked away first, pretending to examine the measuring tape as it wound around his elbow.

"Too short," she said.

Harry glanced down at the robe hanging from his shoulders. He had been too short for Dudley's clothes for years; he had hoped that buying his own might solve the problem.

"The left sleeve," the girl clarified. "It's shorter than the other."

Madam Malkin squinted at it. "Only because you've lifted that shoulder, dear."

The girl lowered her shoulder, and the sleeves became even.

"Oh."

She said nothing else, but the tips of her ears turned pink. Harry smiled before he could stop himself. Her grey eyes found him in the mirror at once.

"Something amusing?"

"No."

"You smiled."

Harry's smile disappeared. "Sorry."

That made her turn properly. One of the pins hovered indecisively near her knee while she studied him, looking less composed than curious for the first time.

"Why are you apologizing?"

Harry did not know how to answer. At home, apologizing quickly sometimes ended a conversation before it became something worse. Aunt Petunia expected it whether he understood the offense or not, and Dudley liked making him say it twice. The girl waited without telling him to stop looking stupid.

Harry shrugged. "I thought you were annoyed."

"I was asking a question."

"Oh."

She considered him for another moment, then glanced at the tape he was trying to keep away from his fingers. "You don't know much about Hogwarts, do you?"

Harry wished his ignorance were less visible. Everyone he had met that morning seemed to recognize something he was meant to understand, and he had begun to dread the questions that followed.

"Not really."

"Were your parents Muggles?"

"My parents are dead."

The words came easily. They had been true for as long as he could remember. He might as well have told her he lived in Surrey. Her expression altered all the same; her mouth closed on whatever she had been about to say, and for a moment she looked her own age.

"Oh," she said, then straightened a little. "I see."

"My aunt and uncle raised me," Harry added, though he wasn't sure why. "They're Muggles."

"And they told you nothing?"

"They didn't exactly like talking about magic."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Harry looked at her.

She was not laughing at him, and she had not put on the soft, awkward expression adults sometimes used when they learned about his parents. Her eyebrows had drawn together. She seemed annoyed with somebody, but, for once, Harry did not think it was him.

"No," he admitted. "They didn't tell me anything."

A pin settled into the fabric near her ankle. The girl ignored it, still looking at Harry.

"That is astonishingly incompetent."

A laugh escaped Harry before he could disguise it as a cough. The small sound startled him. Madam Malkin looked around, and the tape gave his wrist an impatient tug. The girl's eyebrows drew together as though he had missed a perfectly serious point.

"What?"

"No one's ever called them incompetent before."

"Then you have known a disappointing number of observant people."

Harry laughed again.

The girl pressed her lips together. One corner betrayed her, and Harry found himself looking forward to what she might say next. That was an unfamiliar feeling around another child.

"I'm Draconia Malfoy," she said after a moment.

She delivered the name carefully, then watched him with an air of expectation. Harry wondered whether he was meant to recognise it. He had never heard it before, and she had already found that he knew very little; another admission hardly seemed likely to surprise her.

"I'm Harry."

Draconia waited, still interested in the answer rather than impatient. Harry forgot the decision he had made at the door.

"Harry Potter."

Madam Malkin dropped her pins. They struck the floor in a bright little scatter, and the tape around Harry's chest slackened and slid to his waist.

"Harry Potter?" she repeated.

Harry's good humour vanished. He knew what came next: a glance towards his fringe, a question about the scar, another explanation of how important he was. People had been delighted to meet him all morning without seeming particularly interested in anything he might say. He had liked being only Harry for those few minutes on the stool.

Draconia stared too. He wished he had remembered to keep the rest of his name to himself.

Then her gaze shifted from his scar to the oversized sleeve of Dudley's old shirt beneath the fitting robe, and something thoughtful entered her face.

"You really didn't know," she said.

It was not a question.

Harry shook his head.

Madam Malkin recovered herself and summoned the pins into her palm, apologising so rapidly that Harry could make out only every third word. He told her it was all right, although he was not sure what she was apologising for. Draconia paid her no attention.

"My parents have books about you."

"That sounds strange."

"It is rather strange now that I've met you."

Harry glanced down at the stool, unsure whether to be insulted.

Draconia seemed to notice. "I meant that the books make it sound as though you knew what you were doing."

"I was a baby."

"Yes. That does seem to have escaped the authors."

That drew another unwilling laugh from him.

Madam Malkin resumed her work, though she continued casting glances at Harry whenever she thought he would not notice. Draconia, at least, watched him openly.

"What did they tell you?" Harry asked.

"About you?"

He nodded.

Draconia's expression became guarded. "That you defeated the Dark Lord."

"Hagrid said that too."

"And?"

"And I don't remember it."

She glanced at the scar beneath his fringe.

Harry resisted the urge to cover it.

"Perhaps that's for the best," she said.

Harry looked at her. Everybody else had wanted to know what he remembered, as though he might produce an answer if they asked enthusiastically enough. She had made it sound as though forgetting was allowed. He let his hand fall before it could reach his fringe.

The pins finished their work. Madam Malkin helped Draconia down and sent her behind the curtain to change, leaving Harry alone with the flying tape. He expected her to leave now. Dudley's friends usually found something else to do once they had finished asking questions.

A minute later, Draconia emerged in a travelling cloak fastened neatly at her throat. She stopped beside his stool instead of going to the door, and Harry tried not to look too pleased.

"Do you know about the Houses?"

"Four of them," Harry said, pleased to possess an answer. "Hagrid mentioned that."

"Did he explain them?"

"Not really."

Draconia sighed. Harry suspected Hagrid had joined the Dursleys on a list she was keeping, though he hoped the two categories were separate.

"You should learn before the Sorting."

"Why?"

"Because it matters."

"How?"

She opened her mouth, then stopped. The answer had evidently seemed obvious until he asked for it. Harry waited, rather pleased to have found something she could not explain immediately.

"Your House is where you live," she said at last. "It is who you study with, who people expect you to trust and, quite often, what they decide about you before speaking to you."

"That sounds stupid."

"It frequently is."

Harry studied her. "Which one are you going to be in?"

"Slytherin."

This answer came without hesitation, but it sounded different from her name. Rehearsed rather than certain.

"Is that the best one?"

"My family thinks so."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Her eyes narrowed at the return of her own words. For a heartbeat, Harry thought he had spoiled things. Then one corner of her mouth lifted. She looked pleased, as though he had finally worked out a rule she had not bothered to explain.

"Slytherin values ambition, resourcefulness and knowing when not to announce everything, you're thinking."

"I might be bad at the last one."

"I had noticed."

Harry smiled.

"What do the others value?"

"Ravenclaw prizes intelligence. Hufflepuff likes loyalty and hard work. Gryffindor likes bravery." She considered that last description. "Often before anyone has checked whether being brave is a good idea."

"Hagrid was in Gryffindor."

"That does not surprise me."

She adjusted the clasp of her cloak, though it was already straight, and glanced towards the window. Whoever was collecting her must be nearby; Harry wished they would take a little longer.

"My point is that you should think about it. Everyone will have an opinion about where Harry Potter belongs. That does not mean any of them should be allowed to decide."

Harry's smile faded.

Nothing grand in the way she said it. She spoke quietly, almost impatiently, as though warning him about a stair that tended to move beneath people's feet. Yet the words settled somewhere beneath the day's confusion.

Nobody had asked what Harry wanted from the magical world. They had told him he belonged in it, told him who his parents had been and what he had done as a baby. Even Hagrid, kind as he was, spoke about Hogwarts as though Harry's happiness there were already decided.

"How do they decide?"

"The Sorting Hat."

Harry waited for her to indicate that she was joking. She did not. "A hat?"

"It is enchanted, Potter. They don't simply drop us into an ordinary one."

"Right." After the bank and the measuring tape, a hat with opinions was hardly the strangest thing he had heard that day. "Can it put me somewhere I don't want to go?"

Draconia glanced towards Madam Malkin, who was occupied with a rack of winter cloaks.

"My father says the Hat sees what matters most."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," she conceded. "It isn't."

Someone called her name from outside the shop—a woman's voice, gentle but carrying unmistakable expectation.

Draconia stepped towards the door, then paused with her fingers against the handle. Harry could see the reflection of a pale-haired woman waiting outside.

"I'll see you at King's Cross."

Harry blinked. "How do you know?"

"We're both going to Hogwarts, Potter."

The corner of her mouth lifted.

"That makes it rather likely."

She left before Harry could answer.

Through the shop window, he saw her join a tall, pale woman dressed in cream and a man whose long blond hair fell over the collar of his black robes. The man looked towards the window. Harry could not tell whether he had heard their conversation, but his attention rested on Harry for one uncomfortable second before returning to his daughter.

Draconia glanced back once, and Harry lifted his hand before he had time to wonder whether she expected him to. She gave a small nod. Then the crowd carried the three of them away, and he was left watching a window full of strangers.

When Harry emerged several minutes later, Hagrid was waiting beside a display of self-stirring cauldrons with two rapidly melting ice creams in his hands.

"There yeh are," he said. "Thought they might've sewn yeh into the curtains."

Harry accepted the less melted cone. Chocolate and raspberry ran together down one side, and he caught the drip with his thumb before it reached the brown-paper parcel containing his new robes. He still wore Dudley's shirt. It felt different, knowing he had clothes of his own under his arm.

Hagrid set off through the crowd. Harry followed, glancing back after several steps. There was no pale-haired girl by the window now, only the shadows of hanging robes and a new customer waiting near the counter. He wondered whether Draconia would remember him at King's Cross. It was a long way away, September, and she probably knew plenty of people already.

"Hagrid?"

"Mm?"

"Are there really books about me?"

Hagrid looked down. "Books?"

"The girl in there said her parents own some."

"Ah." Something uncomfortable passed across Hagrid's face. "Well, yeah. S'pose there would be."

Harry nearly walked into a stack of cauldrons. Hagrid steadied the top one with a hand large enough to cover its lid.

"Why?"

Hagrid slowed. People flowed around them on both sides, carrying parcels and cages while shopkeepers called from beneath brightly coloured awnings.

"Well," Hagrid began, lowering his voice, "what happened that night—it weren't a small thing, Harry. Everyone in our world knows about it."

"But they don't know what happened."

The certainty of it made Hagrid look at him again.

Harry stared at the raspberry ice cream spreading over his fingers. "Draconia said the books make me sound different. And you said that this... Voldemort tried to kill me, but you didn't say why."

At the name, a passing wizard stumbled and looked around sharply. Hagrid flinched as well.

"Keep yer voice down."

"Why?"

"Because people don' like hearin' it."

"That still doesn't tell me why he wanted to kill me."

Hagrid's stride lost some of its easy momentum. He steered Harry out of the crowd towards a patch of shade between two shops, where nobody had to squeeze around them to pass.

"I don' know why he went after yeh, Harry," he said at last. "Wish I did. I weren't there when it happened."

"He killed my parents first, didn't he?" Harry asked.

Hagrid stopped.

The upper floors leaned towards one another above the narrow street. Harry could smell hot metal from a nearby workshop and the sweetness of his melting ice cream. A minute ago, it had tasted wonderful. Now he held it without remembering to eat.

"Yeah," Hagrid said at last. "He did."

"Were they fighting him?"

"Your mum and dad stood against him," Hagrid replied, choosing the words slowly. "They were brave people. Both of 'em."

Harry tried to picture them.

The attempt produced very little: a green flash he did not know whether he remembered or had invented, and a high, cold laugh that sometimes followed him out of a dream. He could picture Aunt Petunia's face down to the small crease beside her mouth, yet he had no face at all to put beside the word Mum. His parents had stopped being victims of a car crash that morning. He still did not know how to imagine them alive.

"What were they like?"

Hagrid's expression softened.

"Your dad could make trouble out of an empty room," he said. "Your mum was clever—proper clever—and kinder than most people had any right ter expect. Stood up for what she believed in, even when it cost her."

Harry looked towards the far end of the Alley. A man was arguing with a cage full of puffing purple creatures; he watched them without seeing much. Trouble out of an empty room. He could almost picture that. It gave his father something to do besides stand bravely in a story that ended before Harry could remember him.

"Was my dad in Gryffindor?"

"Both of 'em were."

"Is that the best House?"

Hagrid gave a booming laugh, plainly grateful for a question he could answer with pleasure. "Now yer askin' the right questions."

Harry smiled despite himself. "Thought so."

"Course," Hagrid added, with an attempt at fairness that did not survive his grin, "every House'll tell yeh it's the best. You'll find out soon enough."

Harry thought of Draconia saying almost exactly the same thing in a rather different way.

"Do I get to choose?"

"Sortin' Hat decides."

His hand tightened around the cone.

"Oh."

Hagrid noticed. "Nothin' ter worry about. Hat's been sortin' students for hundreds of years. Knows what it's doin'."

Harry nodded because Hagrid was smiling down at him, but hundreds of years were no help if the Hat decided he belonged somewhere he hated. He had spent long enough living where other people put him. Draconia's warning stayed with him as they rejoined the crowd.

Hagrid began listing the things still left to buy. Harry listened with half his attention, trying to fit his father's talent for trouble beside the fame, the four Houses and all those strangers who knew more about his family than he did. He finished the ice cream before it could melt completely. It seemed a waste to lose something he liked while thinking about things he could not change.

Much later, with their shopping finally done, Hagrid stopped at the mouth of the Alley and searched through the pockets of his coat. He produced several loose coins, a length of string, something furry that Harry decided not to ask about and, finally, a thick paper ticket.

"First o' September," he said, handing it over. "Hogwarts Express leaves King's Cross at eleven."

Harry examined the ticket.

London to Hogwarts, Platform Nine and Three-Quarters.

He turned it over. The other side was blank.

"Platform nine and three-quarters?"

"That's right."

"How do I find it?"

"Between nine an' ten."

Harry waited for the rest.

Hagrid had already become distracted by the bookshop window.

"Hagrid."

"Mm?"

"There isn't a platform between nine and ten."

Hagrid looked down at him, surprised. Then he smiled as though Harry had asked how owls managed to fly.

"There is if yeh know where ter look. Magical entrance." Hagrid folded the ticket back into Harry's hand. "At the divider. Yeh'll see other students with trunks an' owls—just go where they go."

Harry looked down at the printed number. He would have preferred a map, but Hagrid was already counting their parcels and checking that none had been left behind. Harry slipped the ticket carefully into his pocket. Finding people with owls sounded manageable.

On the first of September, King's Cross proved louder than Diagon Alley and considerably less helpful.

People hurried beneath the great clock with bags, newspapers and the conviction that everyone else was in their way. Trolleys rattled over the floor. An announcement echoed overhead, lost halfway through beneath the noise of an arriving train. Harry could see plenty of luggage, but no other owls. Uncle Vernon stood beside his trunk and stared at the ticket as though he had finally found proof that the whole summer had been an elaborate nuisance arranged especially for him.

"Platform nine and three-quarters," he read again.

Dudley sniggered, rocking on his heels as though he hoped the platform would fail to appear and Harry would have to be brought home again.

"It's here somewhere," Harry said, keeping his voice level.

"Oh, certainly," Uncle Vernon replied. "Perhaps they've tucked it between platforms seven and eight for a laugh."

Aunt Petunia's mouth tightened at the word they. She kept looking around as if a wizard might emerge from behind a timetable and recognise her.

Harry took hold of the trolley.

Uncle Vernon relinquished it gladly. "Well, we've brought you to the station. More than could reasonably be expected."

Harry looked from him to the enormous clock above the concourse. There was still nearly an hour before the train was meant to leave.

"Aren't you going to help me find it?"

Uncle Vernon gave a short, unpleasant laugh, and Dudley joined in. Harry looked towards Aunt Petunia. She was not laughing.

For one strange moment, her eyes rested on Harry's trunk, on the new brass cauldron strapped above it and the snowy owl blinking from her cage. Something old and unhappy moved across her face. Then Uncle Vernon called to her, and it disappeared.

"Come along, Petunia."

The three of them left. Harry watched Uncle Vernon's broad shoulders disappear into the crowd, with Dudley hurrying to keep up and Aunt Petunia half a step behind. None looked back. He had expected them to go from the moment Vernon agreed to drive him; he had even been looking forward to it. That did not prevent him from standing beside the trolley for a little too long, wondering what he would do if the platform really was not there.

Hedwig clicked her beak softly.

"Yeah," Harry murmured. "I know."

Platforms nine and ten were easy enough to find. Between them stood railings and a solid divider, with travellers streaming past on either side. Harry watched for trunks like his, for cages or robes or any sign that somebody knew what to do. There were suitcases everywhere and no snow-white owls.

He checked the ticket again. Nine and three-quarters. The number had not become more useful in the last ten minutes.

He wheeled his trolley along the barrier, searching for a hidden gate or sign. Nothing appeared. A station guard told him there was no such platform and moved away before Harry could ask another question.

Ten minutes passed, then fifteen. Every time Harry looked up, the great clock seemed to have advanced farther than it ought to. He began considering whether Hagrid might have meant a different divider, or even a different station. Then a girl's voice rose above the noise.

"Mum, I told you, there isn't a platform listed because ordinary people aren't supposed to find it."

Harry turned.

A girl with a great deal of brown hair stood beside two adults who looked almost as lost as Harry felt. Her father held a railway map, folded and unfolded so often that one corner had begun to tear. Her mother kept a hand on a trolley loaded with a new trunk and several labelled bags. The girl herself had a letter open between both hands and appeared to be treating it as the only sensible person in the group.

"I understand what you told us," her father said patiently. "I'm asking how you know this is the correct barrier."

"The instructions specify the divider between platforms nine and ten."

"Yes, but they don't specify what to do with it."

The girl looked down at the letter in her hand as though determined to extract an additional sentence through force of concentration.

Harry hesitated. They were having an argument without anybody sounding angry, which made it harder to judge whether he would be welcome. Then he noticed the edge of a brass cauldron beneath one of the girl's bags. He pushed his trolley a little closer.

"Are you going to Hogwarts?"

All three turned.

The girl's attention immediately dropped to Hedwig, the trunk and the wand-shaped parcel sticking out from Harry's coat pocket.

"Yes," she said. "Are you?"

Harry nodded. "I can't find the platform either."

"I have found it," she corrected. "I simply haven't determined how to enter."

Her mother gave Harry a sympathetic smile. "Do your parents know?"

"No."

The answer came too quickly. He had been thinking of Aunt Petunia and Uncle Vernon, and only realised what the woman had actually asked when her smile faltered.

Harry tightened his grip on the trolley. "I mean—they're not here."

The girl looked at him more carefully. Unlike most people in Diagon Alley, she did not look first at his scar. She looked at his face.

Before she could ask anything further, a family of red-haired people hurried past, steering an improbable collection of trunks and cages towards the divider. Harry turned so quickly that Hedwig clicked her beak in protest.

"Percy first," said a plump woman. "And no dawdling this time."

A tall boy pushed his trolley directly towards the barrier.

Harry opened his mouth to warn him. The trolley struck the divider without a crash, the boy followed it, and both vanished. A woman carrying a briefcase walked through the place where he had been, oblivious.

The brown-haired girl made a small, triumphant sound.

"I knew it."

"You didn't," her father said.

"I had narrowed it down."

The red-haired woman noticed them watching and smiled.

"First years?"

Harry and the girl nodded together.

"Nothing to worry about. Walk straight at the barrier between platforms nine and ten. Best do it at a bit of a run if you're nervous."

The girl's mother went pale. "Straight at it?"

"Straight through," the witch assured her.

The brown-haired girl immediately seized her trolley.

"I'll go first."

Her father caught the handle. "Perhaps we might observe one more person."

"Mum!" one of the red-haired boys called.

The witch turned to answer him while another of her sons pushed his trolley through the barrier. Harry stared at the place where he had been. This was what Hagrid had meant by following the other students. It seemed an important difference, knowing through rather than simply towards. He did not want to run headfirst into a divider while dozens of ordinary people watched. He wanted even less to be standing here when eleven o'clock came.

"I'll do it," he said.

The girl looked at him with sudden interest. "Have you done this before?"

"No."

"Then how do you know it's safe?"

"I don't."

Her eyebrows rose.

Harry faced the divider and began to walk. The little wheels rattled beneath the weight of his trunk; Hedwig shifted inside her cage. Every sensible instinct told him to stop. At the last moment, he closed his eyes and pushed harder, bracing for the impact.

Cold rushed across his skin. There was no crash. The trolley rolled freely, and Harry opened his eyes on a world full of steam.

A scarlet steam engine waited beside a platform crowded with witches and wizards. Owls called over the hiss of steam; cats wound between trunks; students leaned from open windows to call last farewells. Above the train, a sign declared:

HOGWARTS EXPRESS — ELEVEN O'CLOCK

Harry let out the breath he had been holding and looked back. From this side, the entrance appeared to be a solid archway. Before he could work out how to recognise it again, a second trolley emerged at considerable speed.

"Move!"

He jumped aside as the brown-haired girl shot through the barrier, stopped herself and immediately turned to inspect it from the magical side.

Her parents followed more cautiously. Her mother embraced her so tightly that the girl's face disappeared into her coat, while her father checked the train, the platform and several nearby families as if attempting to memorise every danger at once.

Harry looked away as the girl's mother smoothed her hair, kissed her forehead and began checking whether she had packed something warm enough. He suddenly felt as though he were listening at somebody else's front door. Quietly, he turned his trolley towards the train.

"Wait," the girl called.

He stopped. The girl hurried after him, leaving her parents to manage the trunk. Up close, she was breathing rather quickly from her dash through the divider, though she seemed determined not to let that matter.

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"You went first."

Harry shrugged. "Someone had to."

"That doesn't make it sensible."

"No."

She seemed unsure what to do with his agreement. Harry had no defence to offer for going first; he had simply wanted to get through before he lost his nerve.

"I'm Hermione Granger," she said.

"Harry."

"Harry what?"

"Potter."

Hermione stopped walking.

Harry braced himself and kept one hand on the trolley. Her gaze moved to his forehead, but only for a second; then she frowned as though trying to remember a passage she had read.

"Harry Potter," she repeated. "I read about you."

"Apparently everyone has."

"I've read Modern Magical History, The Rise and Fall of the Dark Arts and Great Wizarding Events of the Twentieth Century." She paused. "The accounts are not very consistent."

Despite himself, Harry became curious. "What do they say?"

"That nobody knows precisely what happened."

Harry felt some of the tightness leave his shoulders. She had read three books and still admitted that nobody really knew. He liked her better for that than he could have explained.

Hermione's father caught up with them and held out three books. She took them, balancing the stack against her chest. "Some historians think the curse rebounded. Others suggest an accidental magical defence. One proposed that—"

"Hermione," her mother called.

She looked over her shoulder.

"I have to say goodbye."

"Right."

"I expect I'll see you on the train."

Harry nodded.

Hermione took three steps before turning back.

"You should board soon. The rear carriages are already becoming crowded."

Then she hurried towards her parents.

Harry smiled faintly.

He had now met two witches his age. One had told him to think for himself and then informed him how to do it. The other had thanked him for something she considered foolish and immediately begun organising his journey. Perhaps witches were simply like that. He hoped he would see them both on the train.

Harry found an empty compartment near the middle of the train.

An older student helped him wrestle the trunk into the luggage rack, accepting his thanks with a grin before disappearing along the corridor. Harry placed Hedwig's cage by the window and sat on the edge of a red seat worn smooth beneath generations of elbows. The compartment smelled of upholstery and coal smoke. Nobody was telling him he had chosen the wrong place, so he eased farther back.

Families embraced outside. Parents passed forgotten parcels through windows. A small boy farther down the carriage appeared to be receiving several final warnings concerning a toad.

Harry watched until he found himself searching the platform for someone he knew. Hagrid might have come, perhaps, if Harry had thought to ask. He looked away before the hope could turn into something embarrassing.

The compartment door slid open. Draconia stood in it, one hand still on the handle.

Her expression changed before she could arrange it into something more dignified. Surprise came first; then she looked pleased, plainly and briefly enough that Harry wondered whether he had been allowed to see it.

"You came."

Harry glanced around the compartment. "I said I was going to Hogwarts."

"I meant—" She stopped. "Never mind."

Behind her stood two broad boys with dark robes and nearly identical uncertain expressions.

"You may go," Draconia told them.

One frowned. "Your father said—"

"I know what my father said."

The boys exchanged a look, but retreated down the corridor.

Harry watched them leave. "Friends of yours?"

"No."

"What are they, then?"

Draconia glanced towards the corridor. "Inherited acquaintances."

"Do you inherit a lot of those?"

"Far too many. Father asked them to help with my trunk. They seem to think that includes deciding where I sit."

Harry laughed.

She pushed a small case beneath the seat and sat opposite him. Today she wore a dark green coat over her travelling clothes, and her pale hair was braided away from her face. A few strands had escaped near one ear. Harry remembered the ribbon and the pink tips of her ears in Madam Malkin's, and found it easier to answer her than it had been the first time.

"Did you learn about the Houses?" she asked.

"A little."

"And?"

"And they all sound better when people are describing their own."

Draconia looked pleased. "That is the first sensible thing anyone has ever said about them."

"You said Slytherin was the best."

"I said my family thinks it is."

"You implied the rest."

"That was before I knew you were going to remember everything I said."

Harry grinned.

A whistle sounded outside. Doors slammed along the train, followed by shouted warnings and a last burst of laughter from the platform. Draconia glanced towards the corridor, then back at Harry. "Did you come alone?"

"My aunt and uncle brought me to the station."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Harry's smile faded slightly. "Then yes."

She ran a thumb along the cuff of one sleeve. Harry waited for her to ask why they had not come farther, but she did not. He was grateful enough to turn towards the window before she could notice.

The train lurched.

Harry leaned towards the glass. The platform began sliding past in glimpses of waving hands, bright robes and white steam. Farther along the train, Hermione leaned from a window, waving fiercely to her parents. They walked beside her carriage until they could not keep up, then remained with their arms raised as the curve of the station carried them from sight.

London unfolded beyond the tracks: walls covered in soot, yards full of stacked timber, back gardens small enough to cross in three steps.

For a while, neither spoke. The wheels settled into a rhythm that Harry felt through the seat, and Hedwig tucked her head beneath one wing. Brick walls gave way to roofs and gardens, then to stretches of field divided by hedges. He watched each one pass, unable to stop measuring the distance from Privet Drive. He was going farther than he had ever been, with no uncle beside him and no requirement to be grateful for the journey.

"Are you frightened?" Draconia asked.

Harry looked at her.

She was looking out of the window, picking carefully at the edge of her cuff. For the first time, Harry wondered whether knowing about Hogwarts and going there were different things.

"A bit."

"Good."

"Good?"

"It would be embarrassing if I were the only one."

Harry smiled.

The door slid open so abruptly that both turned.

Hermione stood in the corridor with the round-faced boy Harry had noticed on the platform. Her hair had expanded in the damp air, and one hand held a little wooden box. The boy kept looking from the box to the floor, miserably certain that the answer to something ought to be there.

"Have either of you seen a toad?"

"No," Draconia said.

Hermione looked beneath both seats anyway.

"He's lost one," she explained, nodding towards the boy. "Neville Longbottom."

Neville gave a miserable little wave.

"It's Trevor."

"How did he get out?" Harry asked.

"I opened the box to make sure he was comfortable," Neville said. "He must've jumped when I wasn't looking."

Draconia regarded him. "That rather suggests he was comfortable elsewhere."

Hermione frowned at her. "That isn't helpful."

"It might help him reconsider the box."

Neville looked down at the empty box. Harry knew that expression: the moment someone explained a mistake after it was far too late to avoid it. He stood before Draconia could offer further advice.

"We'll help look."

Draconia turned towards him. "We will?"

"You said you were frightened."

"Of Hogwarts. Not amphibians."

"It'll give you something else to think about."

He stepped into the corridor before she could refuse. Hermione gave Draconia a brief, satisfied look and followed. After a moment, Draconia joined them, shutting the compartment with more force than necessary.

They searched beneath seats and among bags in an unattended compartment, then worked their way around a stack of cauldrons that clattered whenever the train curved. Hermione asked everyone they passed. Draconia supplied increasingly unhelpful opinions about people who could not distinguish a toad from a lumpy purse, but she still bent to check beneath their seats. Harry looked in the quieter places: behind a fallen coat, between a trunk and the wall, under anything low enough to feel like a hiding place.

They found Trevor beneath the trolley witch's lowest shelf. Harry had crouched to retrieve a paper wrapper when two golden eyes blinked at him from behind the Cauldron Cakes. Trevor appeared entirely satisfied with the journey so far. Neville gathered him into both hands and held him against his chest, thanking them before he had quite managed to breathe.

"Thank you!"

"Hermione found the tracks," Harry said.

"They were crumbs," Draconia observed. "Toads do not leave tracks on train carpets."

"The crumbs showed where the trolley had gone."

"The trolley travels through the entire train."

"Yes, which narrowed our search to the corridor."

Draconia stared at her.

Hermione stared back.

Neville edged away.

Hermione handed back the box. Trevor wriggled as Neville tried to put him away. Harry caught him before he reached the floor and lowered him into the box himself.

"Maybe keep the lid shut this time."

Neville nodded fervently and hurried towards his compartment.

Hermione glanced down the corridor. "I should find somewhere to sit."

"Don't you have a compartment?" Harry asked.

"There are spaces, but the people in them are being extremely childish."

Draconia looked her up and down. "They are eleven."

"So am I. It isn't an excuse."

Harry bit the inside of his cheek.

Draconia's eyes narrowed at him. "You're smiling again."

"No."

"You are."

Hermione looked between them. "Is that an ongoing disagreement?"

"Apparently," Harry said.

The trolley witch cleared her throat behind them. All three were blocking the corridor.

Harry stepped aside. "You can sit with us if you want."

Hermione accepted before Harry had quite finished the invitation, then added, "If you're sure," as though she had taken time to consider it. Draconia noticed. Harry could tell by the way she looked from Hermione to him and back again.

"Our compartment is not especially large," she said.

"There are six seats."

"There are also standards."

Hermione lifted her chin. "I'm sure I can improve them."

Harry laughed aloud.

Draconia looked scandalised for perhaps two seconds. Then something reluctant tugged at her mouth.

"Fine," she said. "But if you reorganise my luggage, I will throw yours from the train."

"I wouldn't touch someone else's belongings without permission."

"Good."

Harry slid their compartment door open when they reached it. A strap from Draconia's case had worked free beneath the seat and lay across the entrance.

"Your case is trying to stop us getting in, though," Hermione observed.

Draconia looked down. Harry tucked the strap away before either girl could claim victory, and Hermione brought her things from farther along the train. By the time she had arranged her books, it was difficult to remember why the compartment had ever seemed intended for fewer than three.

By midday, the seat between Harry and the window had disappeared beneath sweets.

Harry had never possessed enough money to buy something merely because he wanted it. At home, wanting had usually been followed by Dudley getting whatever it was. When the trolley reached them, he looked at the price labels and realised that none of them meant no. He bought Pumpkin Pasties, Cauldron Cakes, Liquorice Wands, Bertie Bott's Every Flavour Beans and enough Chocolate Frogs to make Hermione lower her book.

"You cannot possibly eat all of that."

"I know."

"Then why did you buy it?"

Harry looked at the pile.

"Because I could."

Hermione looked at the pile again. Draconia stopped unfastening her lunch tin. Harry waited for one of them to ask why buying sweets mattered, and was relieved when neither did.

Harry pushed the sweets towards the middle. "You can help."

Hermione had brought sandwiches cut into precise triangles. Draconia possessed a silver lunch tin containing small pastries, sliced fruit and something that looked far too elaborate to eat aboard a train. Within ten minutes, all three meals had become communal property. Hermione insisted that sweets did not constitute lunch, then accepted a second Cauldron Cake. Draconia moved her fruit into the middle as though sharing it had been her intention all along. Harry tried a pastry with a name he could not pronounce and decided he liked not having to ask which food he was allowed.

A Chocolate Frog leapt from Harry's hand and struck Draconia on the shoulder.

Hermione laughed so hard she nearly dropped her sandwich. Draconia peeled the frog from her coat, holding it between two fingers while it tried to kick free. Harry reached to help, thought better of it at the look on her face, and offered the empty box instead.

"If either of you repeats this story," she said, "I will deny it."

"It left chocolate on your collar," Harry said.

She looked down in alarm.

Hermione laughed harder.

A red-haired boy appeared in the doorway while Draconia was attempting to clean the mark with a handkerchief.

"Have you lot got room?"

There was still room, once Hermione had moved a stack of books from the seat beside her.

Harry nodded. "Sure."

The boy hauled his trunk through the doorway and caught it against the seat. Harry helped lift one end, and together they pushed it out of the way. The boy dropped beside Hermione, a little red from the effort, and eyed the assortment of sweets with interest.

"I'm Ron," he said. "Ron Weasley."

Hermione introduced herself. Harry did the same.

Ron's eyes widened. "Harry Potter?"

Harry felt himself tense. Ron looked towards his fringe, then seemed to think better of whatever he had been about to ask. He rubbed one ear and glanced at the sweets instead.

"Right," he said. "Good to meet you."

Then he saw Draconia, and the ease left his face. He sat straighter. Across from him, she lowered the handkerchief she had been using on her collar.

"Malfoy."

"Weasley."

Neither appeared to require a first name. Harry looked between them, suddenly aware that he had invited someone into a quarrel which had begun before any of them boarded the train.

Hermione looked between them. "Do you know each other?"

"Our families do," Draconia said.

"Unfortunately," Ron added.

Draconia folded the stained handkerchief into a small, exact square. "You could have chosen another compartment."

"So could you."

"It's my compartment."

Harry glanced at Draconia. "It was empty when I found it."

She gave Harry a look of deep betrayal. Ron grinned, encouraged by the small victory, and Harry began to wish he had explained that six seats did not mean six invitations to argue.

The conversation might have recovered if Ron had left it there. Instead, encouraged by Draconia's irritation, he leaned back and said, "Suppose you'll be in Slytherin, then."

"Yes."

"Wouldn't catch me there."

Draconia's face cooled. "I imagine they'll endure the disappointment."

Ron's ears reddened. "My whole family's been in Gryffindor."

"Then you won't have to think very hard, will you?"

Hermione closed her eyes briefly.

Harry looked from one to the other. "Do you both have to do this?"

"She started it," Ron said.

"He said Malfoy as though he'd found something unpleasant in his shoe," Draconia replied.

Harry stared at her until she sighed.

"Fine."

Ron reached for a pasty. "Slytherins are all the same anyway."

Draconia's hand stopped upon her cup.

Harry remembered Draconia's warning about people deciding who you were before they spoke to you. He remembered her on the stool in Madam Malkin's, making him laugh when everybody else had wanted to look at his scar. He had liked her before he knew what Slytherin meant. That seemed a better reason than any of the ones being offered now.

"You don't know all of them," he said.

Ron shrugged. "My brothers do."

"That isn't the same."

Hermione had answered before Harry could. When everyone turned towards her, she sat straighter and moved her sandwich off the book in her lap.

"Your brothers haven't met every Slytherin either," she said. "And I don't see why somebody's House tells you everything about them."

Ron frowned. "Which side are you on?"

"I wasn't aware facts had sides."

Draconia looked at Hermione. The handkerchief loosened between her fingers, and she seemed, for once, to have no immediate correction to make.

Ron stood, muttering that he had promised to meet someone farther down the train. He took a Pumpkin Pasty when Harry offered one, however, and Harry noticed that Draconia waited until the door had closed before reaching for another herself.

"He was rude," Hermione said.

"He was a Weasley," Draconia replied.

"That is precisely the same sort of reasoning."

Draconia opened her mouth, paused, then selected a grape with immense dignity.

"I don't recall requesting an evaluation."

"No," Hermione said. "You plainly needed one."

Harry looked out the window so they would not see him laughing.

By the time the countryside grew wilder, Hermione and Draconia had disagreed about House-elves, wand woods, the pronunciation of three spells and whether Hogwarts: A History contained an obvious anti-Slytherin bias. The argument about pronunciation ended when neither could make the spell work and Harry suggested the word might not be the only problem. Both told him he was not helping. A minute later, Hermione was explaining the part he had missed, and Draconia was leaning across the table to draw a better diagram.

It was the longest conversation with children his own age he had ever had. Whenever he thought a disagreement might end it, one of them found something else to say.

Sometimes both girls spoke at once. Sometimes they turned to Harry for judgement, and he had to admit he understood neither argument. Draconia knew the magical world as though it were a house in which she had grown up: which doors stayed shut, whose portraits mattered, which rules people obeyed without asking why. Hermione had learned it from books and kept wanting to look behind the doors. Harry knew almost nothing, but neither girl seemed to think that disqualified him from the conversation. Between them, it began to bother him less.

The train began to slow beneath a darkening sky.

A prefect passed through the corridor, reminding them to put on their school robes and leave their luggage aboard when they arrived. Hermione gathered her things and went to join a group of girls changing in a compartment farther along the carriage. Draconia stayed where she was and unfastened her coat; she could pull her robe over the clothes she already wore. She watched the door close, then retrieved a bean from the crease of the seat and set it carefully aside.

"She is exhausting."

Harry pulled his robe over his head. "You like her."

"I absolutely do not."

"You argued with her for four hours."

"She was wrong for a surprising amount of them."

"She said the same about you."

She worked her head through the collar, leaving her braid skewed and several pale strands stuck across her cheek.

"Then she was wrong again."

Harry grinned.

Draconia tried to flatten her hair, missed a strand and became visibly annoyed. Harry pointed towards the window, where her reflection showed faintly over the darkening fields. She gave him a suspicious glance, then used the glass to find the strand she had missed.

"You invited her," Draconia said.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

She asked while studying her reflection, as though the question mattered less than putting her braid straight. Harry had begun to recognise when she did that.

"She helped Neville."

"So did we."

"And she thanked me at the station."

"That is a very low standard for friendship."

Harry's hands slowed on the clasp of his robe.

"It's higher than you'd think."

Draconia stopped fiddling with her hair. He wished he had made another joke instead. It had been easy to forget, for a few hours, how strange some perfectly ordinary things were to him.

The train shuddered beneath their feet. Beyond the window, mountains crowded the horizon, their black shapes cutting into the final blue of evening. The empty sweet wrappers on the table trembled with the wheels. Draconia lowered her hands to her knees and looked out at the mountains.

"I wasn't saying she shouldn't have stayed," Draconia said.

Harry looked at her.

"I was only asking."

"Right."

"And if we are all placed in different Houses, the arrangement may become inconvenient."

"You'll be in Slytherin."

"Yes."

"Hermione said she doesn't care where she goes."

"She cares enormously. She simply hasn't gathered enough information to develop a preference."

Harry laughed. "And I don't know."

Draconia's grey eyes settled on him.

"You don't have to know yet."

It was a quieter version of what she had told him in Diagon Alley. Harry nodded and fastened his robe at last. He had imagined knowing exactly where to go once he reached the magical world; it helped that she did not expect him to.

The door opened and Hermione returned in her robes, carrying three books she had apparently decided were necessary for the remaining ten minutes of the journey.

Draconia eyed them. "Planning to revise while we cross the lake?"

"I'm planning for the possibility of delay."

"By reading?"

"Some of us find it calming."

Draconia glanced at Harry. "Exhausting."

Hermione sat beside him. "She likes me."

"I told her that."

"You are both mistaken."

The train drew into the station before they could answer. Cold air swept into the carriage as the doors opened, carrying the smell of rain and pine. Harry followed Hermione onto the platform, where students were lifting their voices over one another and nobody seemed certain which direction to take.

"Firs' years! Firs' years over here!"

Hagrid's lantern bobbed above the crowd. Harry had not realised how much he wanted to see a familiar adult until Hagrid spotted him and grinned. They followed him down a steep path, boots slipping on loose stones, with Draconia keeping one hand on the rail and Hermione trying to watch both the path and whatever lay beyond it.

Hogwarts first appeared as light upon black water.

The first-years crowded into small boats at the edge of a lake, their earlier excitement subdued by darkness, cold and the enormous outline rising ahead of them. Towers climbed into the night. Hundreds of windows burned gold against the stone, some high enough to resemble fallen stars.

Harry forgot to breathe. He had seen drawings of castles, but none had prepared him for windows hanging so far above the water or stone that seemed to fill half the sky.

He sat in the middle of a boat with Hermione beside him and Draconia opposite. Neville occupied the final seat, clutching Trevor's box with both arms. For several minutes, even Hermione had nothing to explain. Draconia stared upwards, her mouth slightly open, and did not notice when a strand of hair blew across her face.

The boats moved without oars. Water whispered against their sides as they crossed beneath the shadow of the castle. Harry tipped back his head, trying to see the highest tower, and felt something expand inside him—wonder threaded through with an ache so sharp he could hardly separate the two.

He had been allowed to come here. That was the thought he kept returning to while the towers grew larger: somewhere behind those windows was a bed meant for him, and a desk, and lessons he could attend without pretending he did not want to learn. The castle looked far too grand to have made room for somebody who had spent most of his life under the stairs. His trunk was coming all the same.

Hermione's shoulder pressed lightly against his whenever the boat rocked. Across from him, Draconia caught his eye. No clever observation passed between them. She only smiled, small and unguarded.

Then the boat rocked against another, the catch on Trevor's box sprang loose, and the toad made an immediate bid for the water. Harry caught him against the front of his robe while Neville made a sound which would have carried halfway across the lake. Hermione secured the lid with the ribbon from one of her book parcels.

By the time they reached the underground harbour, she was explaining why lids ought to be tested, Draconia was insisting Trevor possessed "a developed instinct for freedom," and Harry was laughing hard enough that he nearly missed the step onto shore. Draconia caught his sleeve, told him to pay attention and nearly missed the same step herself.

They climbed through passages which smelled of wet stone, then followed Hagrid up onto the grass and through the great entrance doors. The floor beyond shone in the torchlight; somewhere above them, footsteps echoed along a staircase they could not yet see. An austere witch in emerald robes collected the first-years and led them into a smaller chamber outside the Great Hall. Harry recognised her name when Hagrid greeted her: Professor McGonagall.

"Welcome to Hogwarts," she said.

Her gaze swept across the group and silenced them more effectively than a shout.

"In a few moments, you will pass through these doors and join the rest of the school. Before you take your seats, you will be Sorted into your Houses. While you are here, your House will be something like your family."

Draconia looked towards him. Harry kept his attention on Professor McGonagall as she described points, the House Cup and the behaviour expected of them, but he heard only fragments. Something like your family. He knew she meant to reassure them. Around him, children stood a little straighter, comforted by the promise of somewhere to belong. Harry rubbed his thumb against the seam of his sleeve. He would have preferred to learn what the House was like before agreeing to call it that.

The other first-years had begun whispering. Ron was assuring another boy that the Sorting couldn't possibly be as bad as his brothers claimed. Hermione checked the fastening of her robe, then checked it again. Draconia stood very still, her face returning to the careful composure she had worn in Madam Malkin's.

The fingers at Draconia's side had curled into her robe. Harry remembered her picking at her cuff on the train. He stepped closer, leaving just enough space that she would not have to acknowledge the reason.

"What if it's wrong?" he whispered.

Her gaze remained fixed upon the doors. "The Hat?"

He nodded.

Draconia considered the question, still watching the doors.

"Then prove it wrong."

Harry turned the answer over while Professor McGonagall went to check that the Hall was ready. It was the first suggestion he had heard that allowed the Hat to make a mistake without deciding the rest of his life. Before he could tell Draconia that, McGonagall returned and the doors opened.

Four long tables filled the Great Hall, crowded with faces turning towards them. Candles floated overhead without chains or holders, their flames steady beneath a ceiling full of stars. Harry looked up so quickly that he nearly stopped the line behind him. Clouds drifted across the dark as though the roof had been removed; he felt for rain and found only the warmth rising from hundreds of people.

Ghosts shone among the students, their pale robes passing through benches and shoulders without disturbing either. Golden plates reflected the candles. At the front, the teachers sat behind a long table, and a silver-haired wizard watched the first-years through half-moon spectacles with an expression so cheerful that Harry wondered whether he had noticed what they were about to endure.

Then Harry saw the shabby pointed hat resting on a stool. Its brim was frayed and one side sagged. After Draconia's description, he had expected something larger, or at least something that looked capable of a firm opinion. There was no duel, no troll, no examination waiting beneath it. Hermione released a long breath beside him.

Draconia murmured, "Disappointed?"

"Quiet," Hermione whispered.

A tear near the hat's brim opened, and it began to sing. Its voice was surprisingly strong. A few older students leaned back as though settling in for a familiar performance, while the first-years stared. Harry tried to listen to what it said about the Houses, but faces at the tables were beginning to turn towards him. The whispering moved faster than the song.

Is that—?

The scar—

Harry Potter—

Harry stared at the back of Neville's robes and wished the boy were taller. He had spent most of the train journey forgetting that anyone might stare at him here. Now the afternoon seemed very far away.

Professor McGonagall unrolled a scroll. The song ended, the Hall applauded, and the names began.

Students approached the stool one at a time. The Hat shouted; tables applauded; children hurried towards their new Houses with expressions of relief, pride or astonishment.

"Granger, Hermione."

Hermione inhaled and tightened her grip on the books she had refused to leave behind. She looked towards Harry, then Draconia, as though both of them ought to have an instruction ready.

"Good luck," Harry said.

Draconia added, "Do try not to correct it."

Hermione gave Draconia an indignant look, and the familiar argument seemed to settle her. Harry took her books while she crossed the Hall. On the stool, she sat very straight, her hands pressed together in her lap. When the Hat fell over her eyes, she gave a little start that she would probably deny afterwards.

The pause lasted long enough for Harry to wonder whether she really had begun correcting it. Then the brim lifted.

"GRYFFINDOR!"

The table on the left erupted.

Hermione removed the Hat, her face bright with relief, and looked back before joining her House. Harry lifted the books to show he still had them; Draconia inclined her head with solemn approval. Then Hermione went to the Gryffindor table, and a space opened beside Harry where she had been standing.

More names followed. He kept her books tucked against his chest, watching her find a seat.

"Malfoy, Draconia."

The whispering changed. It was quieter now, with a few students leaning towards one another as Draconia stepped out of the line. Harry recognised the way people looked when a name had told them something before its owner could speak.

Draconia crossed the Hall with her shoulders straight. Just before she sat, she smoothed the front of her robe with both hands. Harry thought of the uneven sleeves in Madam Malkin's and wondered whether she was remembering to keep her shoulders level. She looked as though she knew exactly what would happen. He could no longer quite believe it.

The brim had scarcely touched her hair when it shouted:

"SLYTHERIN!"

Applause thundered from the green-and-silver table.

Draconia removed the Hat and remained on the stool for a moment, looking towards the cheering table. Everything had happened precisely as she expected, yet she appeared less certain now that it was done. Then she found Harry among the waiting students. He smiled. Her grip on the Hat loosened before she handed it back.

She joined the Slytherin table, where older students made room at once. A prefect shook her hand. Several people leaned closer to speak to her, and Harry could see the careful expression return as she answered. Within moments, the pale head he recognised was one among many beneath the green banners. He was still holding Hermione's books, with nobody beside him to point out that his arms were beginning to ache.

The Sorting continued. Harry tried to follow the alphabet, then wished he had not. Each name left him fewer moments in which he could pretend his own was not coming.

He remembered Diagon Alley: Everyone will have an opinion about where Harry Potter belongs.

He remembered Ron declaring that his whole family had been Gryffindors, and Hagrid saying his parents had been there too. Hermione sat beneath the red-and-gold banners, watching him without blinking. Draconia waited across the Hall. He had known them both for very little time. It was still longer than he had known anyone else he wanted to sit beside.

"Potter, Harry."

The whisper became a roar.

Professor McGonagall held out a hand for Hermione's books before he sat. Harry passed them over, fumbling the top one. Every eye seemed to follow the movement. He wished he had given them to someone first, wished he knew whether to smile, wished there were a way to cross a room without his name getting there ahead of him. His hands were cold by the time Professor McGonagall lowered the Hat.

The brim dropped over his glasses, and the faces vanished into darkness. Harry could hear the Hall, but it sounded farther away now.

Well, said a small voice near his ear. This is interesting.

Harry gripped the edge of the stool. The voice seemed to be inside the Hat and inside his head at once. Nobody had warned him about that part.

Plenty of courage, the Hat said. Though you have not always had much choice about using it. A good mind, too. Watchful. You notice a great deal before you let anybody see that you have noticed. And you are stubborn, aren't you?

Harry thought of the cupboard beneath the stairs, of keeping quiet when he had something to say, of the strange things that still happened no matter how often Uncle Vernon ordered him to stop them. He wondered how much of that the Hat could see.

You could do very well in Slytherin.

Harry waited for the alarm he thought he ought to feel. It did not come. He knew one person in Slytherin already, and she had been kind to him when she could easily have laughed.

He thought of Draconia across the Hall: sharp-tongued, difficult, strangely kind Draconia, who had noticed the things he did not say. He imagined joining her table and felt a pull towards it.

Then he thought of Hermione thanking him on the platform. Hermione searching the train for someone else's lost pet. Hermione defending Draconia while insisting she did not care about sides.

Two girls at opposite tables. At home, the choice had usually been between staying out of somebody's way and getting in trouble. He had not expected wanting two good things to be difficult too.

Curious, the Hat murmured. You are waiting for someone to tell you what you want.

Harry swallowed.

They've done that often enough, haven't they? Told you what you are. What you must be. What you ought to feel.

Draconia had warned him not to let other people decide. Yet she had not asked him to choose Slytherin. Hermione had not asked him to follow her into Gryffindor either. He could remember both of them talking all afternoon without either saying that sitting at the wrong table would end it. Perhaps he was the one turning the choice into something he might lose.

"I don't know," Harry whispered.

No, said the Hat, not unkindly. But I do.

Harry listened to the faint noise beyond the brim. Someone coughed. A bench scraped. The whole school was waiting while he tried to work out what he wanted, and the Hat did not sound impatient with him.

You have spent your life adapting yourself to danger. Slytherin could teach you to wield that instinct. Yet it is not what you need first.

Harry's grip loosened on the stool. He had never considered that being useful at something might not mean he had to spend his life doing it.

"What do I need?"

To learn that courage can be more than endurance.

The Hat straightened upon his head.

Better be—GRYFFINDOR!

The Hall exploded with noise. Harry removed the Hat, blinking in the sudden light. Students at the Gryffindor table rose to their feet; Hermione was clapping with her whole face bright. Ron shouted from among the first-years still waiting to be Sorted.

Harry stood and accepted Hermione's books from Professor McGonagall, grateful to have something to do with his hands.

Before walking towards them, he looked across the Hall.

Draconia was applauding too.

She clapped twice more before folding her hands on the table. Harry could not read her expression, but when he caught her eye she raised one eyebrow, exactly as she had when he denied smiling in the robe shop.

Your choice? it seemed to ask.

Harry did not know how to explain that it had not felt entirely like one.

He smiled anyway.

Then he joined Hermione at the Gryffindor table. She shifted along the bench before he reached her, making a space, and accepted her books as though he had carried them for this purpose all along. An older boy introduced himself, shook Harry's hand and began explaining the House as the Sorting continued. Harry remembered about half of it. The place beside Hermione stayed his.

The feast appeared in a blaze of golden dishes.

Harry had thought the sweets aboard the train extravagant. They became insignificant beside platters of roast chicken, bowls of potatoes, steaming vegetables, pies, gravy boats and loaves of bread that replenished themselves whenever someone took the final slice.

At first, Harry took less than he wanted. He was used to judging how much would be noticed, and to finishing before somebody decided he had had enough. The dishes kept coming round. Hermione passed the potatoes without asking why his plate was almost empty, and he helped himself properly the second time. Only after he had eaten more than he could remember eating at one meal did the urge to hide a roll for later begin to quieten.

Ron sat several places away, entertaining those around him with impressions of his brothers. Hermione listened to a prefect explain the House timetable, interrupting to clarify details. Across the Hall, Draconia spoke to an older Slytherin girl who had handed her a folded timetable. A boy beside her kept trying to interrupt; she answered him once without looking away from the paper.

Every so often, Harry looked across at her and found her looking back. Once, he caught her eye just as Hermione began asking the prefect a fourth question, and Draconia's raised eyebrow nearly made him laugh into his drink. He wished she were close enough to hear the answer.

The separation felt strange after the train. There had been room for all three of them among books and sweets and Neville's missing toad. Here, with tables long enough to seat dozens, they had been told where to belong and could no longer finish the same conversation.

When the feast ended, the Houses departed in different directions.

Harry managed to lose Gryffindor before they had climbed the second staircase.

One moment he was following the prefect's red-and-gold tie and listening to his voice; the next, a suit of armour had moved aside to argue with a portrait. Harry stopped to stare. By the time he remembered to keep walking, the staircase had shifted and the last robes ahead of him had vanished around a corner.

He chose the left of three corridors because it looked the least dark. Several turns later, he stopped beneath a window and admitted that he had been making the same choice for some time without getting anywhere recognisable. Below the glass, the lake reflected a thin scatter of stars. No helpful red-and-gold students appeared in either direction.

"You're going the wrong way."

Hermione approached from behind, slightly breathless, with a folded piece of parchment in one hand. Her annoyance was so plain that Harry began working out an apology before she reached him.

Harry leaned against the wall. "How did you find me?"

"I noticed you were missing."

Harry had been noticed. He looked at her for a moment, letting the answer settle before asking anything else. At the station, he had watched three people walk away without turning round. Hermione had come back after knowing him for less than a day.

"Do you know the way back?"

"Yes."

"Definitely?"

"Mostly."

Harry smiled. "That sounds like no."

"I understand the route. The staircases are being uncooperative."

Together, they retraced his steps. Hermione had sketched a map from the prefect's explanation while they ate; several moving staircases had already made parts of it useless. She crossed out one line, added an arrow and told a portrait that it was not helping when its occupant laughed. Harry found it easier to be lost with her.

They reached a landing overlooking the Entrance Hall, and Hermione stopped with one hand on the rail. Draconia waited below. Two Slytherin students stood near the dungeon corridor, evidently waiting for her; one had folded his arms and was inspecting the ceiling with increasing impatience. She ignored them and looked directly up at Harry.

"You got lost," she said when he and Hermione descended.

"I was exploring."

"You were going in the opposite direction."

"That could still be exploration."

Hermione reached the final step. "How did you know where he was?"

Draconia glanced at her. "He stopped halfway up the stairs. I saw the last Gryffindor turn the corner without him."

"And you waited?"

Draconia's chin lifted. "I was curious whether he would eventually reappear."

Harry looked towards the Slytherin students. One had begun tapping his foot.

"You're going to get in trouble."

"So are you."

"Hermione knows the way," Harry said. "Mostly."

Hermione frowned at him.

Draconia's mouth twitched.

For a moment, the three stood at the foot of the staircase while the castle divided around them. Students passed in little groups, heading up towards the towers or down towards the dungeons. Harry could hear laughter fading along both routes. He had been looking forward to escaping the Dursleys for weeks; he had not considered that reaching Hogwarts would require him to say goodbye to anyone else.

"Tomorrow," Hermione said, "we should compare our timetables. First years share several subjects across Houses."

Draconia examined her. "Are you arranging to see us?"

"I'm preventing Harry from arriving late."

"I can hear you," Harry said.

Hermione gave him a brief look to show that she had heard, then returned to Draconia. Harry folded his arms. It seemed this was another matter on which he would be permitted an opinion after they had reached a decision.

"We have Potions with Gryffindor on Friday," Draconia said.

"I know."

"Of course you do."

"And the library is available to every House."

Draconia looked down at the timetable still in her hand.

"Yes."

Harry looked between them. They were arguing about when to meet, not whether they would. He could have hugged them both for making the distinction so obvious, but suspected they would find that difficult to understand.

One of the Slytherin boys called, "Malfoy."

Draconia's shoulders tightened. She folded her timetable along its old creases and took a step towards the dungeon corridor, then looked back.

"Try not to get lost before breakfast, Potter."

"Hermione will find me."

Hermione nodded firmly. "I will."

Draconia regarded her for a moment.

"I expect you will."

Then she left.

Harry watched until her pale hair disappeared around the bend. Beside him, Hermione unfolded the map, but instead of choosing a staircase she looked at him.

"Did you want Slytherin?" she asked.

Harry turned sharply. "What?"

"I mean, you seemed to be thinking about it."

"How do you know?"

"You looked at their table before you sat down."

Harry glanced towards the empty dungeon corridor.

"I looked at you too."

Hermione's grip on the map eased. She seemed surprised to have been part of the answer, and Harry wondered why. She had made room beside her without waiting to be asked.

"Oh."

"The Hat said I could do well there," Harry continued. "But then it chose Gryffindor."

"Did you ask it to?"

"No."

Hermione thought about that as seriously as she thought about everything.

"So you didn't tell it not to put you there."

Harry shook his head.

"Does Draconia know?"

"I don't think so."

"We should tell her."

"Why?"

"Because she'll wonder."

Harry looked at Hermione.

She sounded certain. Harry had been worried about explaining a choice he had not quite made; Hermione had thought about how it might feel to the person waiting at the other table. She did not seem to consider that a reason to keep the answer to herself. It was another thing he liked about her.

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

Hermione pointed towards the next staircase.

"Tomorrow."

The staircase shifted halfway to the next landing and carried them towards a corridor Hermione had not marked. She made an outraged noise, steadied herself against the rail and began correcting the map before the stairs had finished moving. Harry laughed. They were lost again, perhaps, but she was already making a plan.

Tomorrow, they would find Draconia. They would compare timetables, argue about which way the library was and probably arrive somewhere neither girl intended. Harry followed Hermione towards Gryffindor Tower with the unfamiliar pleasure of having people to look for when he woke.

For once, he was impatient for morning.
