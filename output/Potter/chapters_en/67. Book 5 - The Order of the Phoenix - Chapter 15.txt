15

Hermione's letter arrived with a smear of red jam on the flap. Harry noticed because she rubbed it with her thumb before opening the envelope. Usually that would have prompted an explanation about breakfast in the Grangers' kitchen, or her father's habit of spreading things too close to important papers. This morning she only looked at the mark for a moment and reached for her spoon.

On Saturday, the twenty-fourth of February, breakfast had become an exercise in watching owls circle the Hall until the Ministry inspectors finished examining their deliveries. Yesterday's report lay folded beside several plates, its corners already stained with tea. Harry had felt better seeing people read it. Now he watched an inspector hold a letter up to the light and wondered how much longer the morning post would take. Feathers drifted into the porridge; somebody farther down the table was trying to persuade a furious tawny owl that the delay was not his fault. Harry had been watching a barn owl attempt to land upon Umbridge's head. She moved to avoid it and the owl corrected its course, parcel swinging, with a determination he rather admired.

Beside him, Hermione slit the envelope with the edge of her spoon. The owl which had brought it accepted a piece of toast from Ron and departed before an inspector could decide the envelope needed another look. Hermione's mother's handwriting covered the front, including an extra line asking whoever handled it to be careful, and continued across the first page. A printed sheet lay behind it, folded twice and marked with a crest Harry did not recognise. Hermione read the letter, went back to the beginning and put her spoon down across the envelope. She had not finished her toast. A curl of butter softened beside the crust while she sat perfectly still, holding the two sheets together.

Harry stopped watching the owl.

"What is it?"

"Nobody's hurt. It isn't anything like that." Hermione glanced at the letter again. "It's the Ministry."

Harry waited. That answered a question he had not asked. Hermione laid the printed sheet between them. Its heading belonged to the Office for the Regulation of Magical Contact with Non-Magical Households. Beneath it, three paragraphs explained that her parents' association with a publication concerning protected educational information had prompted a review of their access to the magical community. The review would include a home visit, an assessment of correspondence and consideration of their continued participation in school consultations.

"They copied the report for the parents' association," she said. "Mum told me she would. They used the version we approved, Harry. Nobody named a child who hadn't agreed."

Across the table, Ron stopped buttering his toast.

"What's a dentist got to do with school security?"

"They say they're inspecting the magical material kept there." Hermione smoothed the paper flat. "Apparently that makes it different."

Harry read the next sentence. Officials conducting the review would be authorised to question staff present at the household's place of employment if necessary to establish the extent of contact.

"They've put the practice address on it."

"It's on the registration form my parents completed when I received my Hogwarts letter."

She said it precisely, as though placing facts in the correct order might prevent them from adding up to a threat. Harry could see her mother's handwriting beneath the notice. The last line began We are all right, sweetheart. He had written almost the same thing to Sirius after nights when he could not manage a second sentence. He wanted to believe her mother had meant it, and could not stop noticing how little space the reassurance occupied compared with the printed sheet.

"Have you spoken to them?"

"The next authorised Floo appointment is Wednesday."

"What about the visitors' mirror?"

"The school end stays with McGonagall. The appointments cover that too." Hermione touched the pocket where she kept her own handpiece. "Mine reaches Hawthorn, not Mum and Dad. I can ask Narcissa to pass something on, but I want to speak to them myself."

Harry had become accustomed to the small piece of glass in his pocket answering when he needed someone. Hermione's parents had a standing visitors' frame, but its school connection was kept in an office she had to ask to enter. Their telephone could not reach this table either. The Ministry had begun deciding how often they could hear their daughter's voice, and Harry understood why being offered somebody else to deliver a message was not enough. He reached for the notice. Hermione held it down with two fingers.

"I know what you're going to say."

"Impressive. I hadn't decided."

"You'll say we should ask Lucius. Or Sirius. Or Dumbledore. And then I'll have to explain it all again while everybody decides what to do."

"They could help, Hermione. You wouldn't have to explain it to each of them on your own."

Her fingers tightened on the corner. Harry heard his own impatience a moment too late. He had wanted to put help within reach; he had made it sound as though she was being foolish for not taking it. He looked down at the jam upon the envelope and lowered his voice.

"When it was Umbridge and me, you stayed while I told people. We can do that for you. If you want us there."

Hermione lifted her hand from the paper. At the Slytherin table, Draconia had already noticed that something was wrong. She set down her cup, spoke briefly to Pansy and rose. A few students watched her cross the Hall. Since Hogsmeade, people seemed to consider every journey she made towards Harry an event worth reporting. This morning he was grateful that she ignored them.

She read the notice standing beside Hermione's shoulder.

"The report has nothing to do with the practice," she said.

"It does according to this." Hermione tapped the second paragraph.

"Then someone needs to challenge that before they arrive and start opening cupboards."

Hermione pulled the envelope towards her. "Please don't make it sound worse."

Draconia sat. The bench was crowded. Harry shifted until his shoulder pressed against Ron's; Ron moved his plate without being asked, leaving enough room for Draconia to sit beside Hermione. Dean looked down the table, saw the notice and returned to his conversation. Harry was grateful for that too. There were times when an audience could make someone look protected and feel surrounded.

"I'm sorry," Draconia said. "I am trying to understand what they can do."

"So am I."

"Can we speak to them today? McGonagall might let us use her fire."

Hermione stared at the printed crest. "They are Muggles. If someone arrives with a wand and a piece of Ministry parchment, how are they supposed to know whether it's valid?"

Harry had no answer. The possibility was suddenly plain: a waiting room full of ordinary patients; her father halfway through a conversation about a tooth; a stranger altering the room with a flick of wood. Her parents had met magic as something wonderful which belonged to their daughter. Mr Granger asked questions about moving staircases and Hermione's mother sent notes thanking people for looking after the children. Harry remembered Mrs Granger hugging him at King's Cross, one hand still holding a shopping bag, as though welcome was a thing she could offer without preparing for it. He did not like imagining that same open doorway with Ministry officials standing in it.

"My mother can be there," Draconia said. "She's dealt with people who arrive expecting not to be questioned. But we should ask your parents first."

Hermione looked up.

"She won't make the decision for them," Draconia added. "She can explain what the visitors are doing and insist they explain it too."

Ron pushed his untouched toast into the middle of the table.

"Dad knows the Muggle liaison people. Some of them are decent. He could find out who's been assigned."

Hermione glanced from one face to the next. Her eyes were dry, which made Harry more worried than tears would have done.

"I don't want them punished because I wrote something true."

"Neither do I," he said.

Ron broke his toast in half and put the larger piece beside her plate. Hermione stared at it, then picked it up. Harry could not have explained why that relieved him. She was still pale, still reading the notice, but she had accepted something without having to argue herself into it.

"We'll ask McGonagall after breakfast," he said. "Unless you want to go now. I can bring your bag. She won't be in a class, will she?"

"It's Saturday. She won't be teaching."

"Good. One less reason for her to wonder whether I listen in Transfiguration."

Hermione's mouth moved a little. She folded the letter with greater care than the notice and slid both into the envelope. Across the Hall, the barn owl finally abandoned Umbridge and landed on a jug of milk. Nobody at their end of the table watched it.

McGonagall's office smelled of the fire she had just lit and the polish somebody had worked into the desk. Harry stood beside a cabinet of Transfiguration models while Hermione explained the notice. A wooden rabbit upon the nearest shelf appeared to be watching her; its ears twitched whenever the papers rustled. McGonagall heard her out and asked for both sheets. When she offered the visitors' mirror or her office Floo, Hermione chose the fire; her mother would be at home, and she wanted to see more of the room than the frame usually allowed. McGonagall arranged the call before lunch.

She did so after reading the notice once and the Grangers' registration conditions twice. Harry watched her mark a passage with the tip of her quill. The feathers trembled in her hand; the voice which followed was perfectly level.

"Your parents registered to receive school information, Miss Granger. That does not give anyone unrestricted access to their practice. Tell them I have asked for the authorisation, including the part which supposedly permits this visit. I will send them my objection in writing."

Hermione knelt upon the hearth rug. She had brushed ash from it before sitting down, then apologised for doing so. McGonagall ignored the apology and stepped into the corridor, leaving the office door open. Draconia followed her. Harry remained only after Hermione caught his sleeve. The flames turned green. A few seconds later, Hermione's mother answered from the fireplace in the small back room of their house. Harry recognised the blue wallpaper from a photograph Hermione had shown him when they were making their summer plans. A framed painting of a harbour hung beside the fire. Someone had straightened it since the photograph was taken, and an ordinary clock ticked above it, rather more slowly than McGonagall's.

"Oh, darling. We didn't mean to frighten you."

Hermione's father joined her, sleeves rolled above his elbows. He had been preparing something in the kitchen; flour marked one wrist. A little of the flour came off when he brushed his hand against his trousers. Harry could almost smell whatever was baking beyond the green fire. They had interrupted a Saturday which ought to have contained nothing more alarming than a burnt crust.

"You should have told me sooner," Hermione said.

"It arrived yesterday afternoon," her mother replied. "We sent the letter straight away."

Hermione's argument died before she could make it. Harry lowered himself to the rug, against the side of McGonagall's desk. The fire warmed one cheek; the floor was cold through his trousers. Hermione was pulling at a loose thread beside her knee, winding it around a finger until the tip whitened.

"You've pulled half your hair out of its tie," her mother said gently.

Hermione pushed the escaped strands behind her ears. "Mum."

"I know. Tell us what you need to ask."

It sounded like a conversation they had had before a dozen examinations. Hermione let go of the thread.

"What exactly have they asked for?"

Her father held up the original notice. "Magical correspondence, registration papers and any material supplied to us by school staff or pupils which concerns current Ministry security arrangements. We asked the lady who delivered it whether that included your letters."

Hermione went rigid.

"What did she say?"

"That she would consult her supervisor."

Her mother spoke before Hermione could begin listing statutes.

"We have put your letters somewhere safe. None of the visitors has read them. Nobody is going to read them merely because they ask in an official voice."

"Mum, they can—"

"Yes, sweetheart. We understood that part. We haven't thrown the notice away; we've asked them to explain it."

Harry looked towards the door. Draconia stood just beyond it with her hands loosely clasped. At Mrs Granger's last sentence, her mouth tightened. She glanced at Hermione, then lowered her eyes to the notice McGonagall had left upon the desk.

"Mrs Malfoy could attend," Hermione said. "If you wanted. She knows the procedures. Professor McGonagall is requesting the authority. Mr Weasley can ask about the department."

Her parents exchanged a glance.

"We would be very grateful," her father said. "Provided Mrs Malfoy doesn't object to arriving through the door. The fireplace in the practice has been boarded up since nineteen eighty-seven."

Hermione laughed once. It sounded painful and relieved.

"I'll tell her."

"Tell Harry and Draconia thank you too," her mother said.

"I'm here," Harry said.

Both Grangers turned towards him. He moved closer to the hearth, uncertain how much of him they could see through the flames.

"I thought you might be. How are you?" Mrs Granger asked.

He had almost prepared an answer about Umbridge before realising she meant him. He told her he was sleeping better than last month. It was mostly true. Her husband asked whether the Walkman still worked, and Harry told him the motor charm had begun making everyone sing rather quickly until Hermione corrected it. He had liked one of the songs better that way, which she refused to accept as a reason for leaving it.

"She pretended it wasn't my fault," Harry added. "Then she asked whether I'd left it on the radiator."

"And had you?" Mr Granger asked.

"Only the end."

"Only the end," Mr Granger repeated, trying not to smile. "That sounds familiar. Your friend has been giving us very exact answers since she was six."

Hermione made a noise which was almost a laugh. For a few minutes the conversation contained schoolwork, the headphones Ron had sat on and a patient who had asked Mr Granger whether the extra number of sweets purchased on Valentine's Day justified postponing an appointment. Her father thought this would only move the problem to Easter. Her mother disagreed that the fire was an appropriate place to continue an argument about appointment scheduling.

Harry listened while Hermione interrupted them both to point out that they had missed the patient's joke. Her hands stopped working the rug. Then her father put down the notice.

"We discussed the report last night. We are still willing to stand by our part in publishing it. That may change if we learn something we don't know yet. We will tell you if it does. But don't withdraw it on our behalf, Hermione."

"I only wanted—"

"To look after us. I know. But we did read it before we copied it. Your mother read it twice."

"Three times," Mrs Granger said. "There were things in it we needed to know."

Hermione looked at the folded notice in her lap.

"You don't have to keep doing it because of me."

"We read what happened to those children," her father replied. "We thought other parents should know. You didn't make that decision for us, and you don't have to undo it for us either. If we need to stop, we'll tell you."

Harry looked at the flour upon Mr Granger's wrist again. He wanted Hermione to hear the choice her parents were making, and knew how badly he would want to argue if Sirius made the same one. The letter had brought danger into their house. Refusing their help would not make it leave. When the call ended, Hermione stayed on the hearth rug. Ordinary orange flames returned beneath the grate. The office clock clicked twice before she lifted her face.

"I hate that they sounded brave."

Harry moved closer to her.

"I know," Harry said. "I'd have wanted Sirius to say he was staying home and nobody would get near him."

She pressed the folded notice against her knee.

"I wanted them to sound annoyed. Dad gets very annoyed about forms. He reads the footnotes aloud until Mum threatens to leave the room."

"He'll probably do that after Narcissa arrives."

"She might help."

"She'll probably ask why the same word means three different things on one page. Your dad will like that."

Hermione leaned into his shoulder. Harry rested his cheek briefly against her hair. Through the open door, he heard Draconia ask McGonagall where a visiting adult should report before contacting the Grangers. Her voice had the same determined courtesy Narcissa used when arranging something which mattered. Hermione's elbow pressed against his ribs as she moved closer. Harry adjusted his arm around her shoulders without looking towards the door. She had stayed with him through enough silences for him to know that filling this one would not help.

McGonagall returned after a few minutes, set a box of tissues upon the desk and began sorting a stack of essays at the other end of it. She did not announce why. Hermione took one, wiped her eyes with considerable irritation and asked whether they could send Narcissa a message before lunch.

"Certainly," McGonagall said. "Miss Malfoy has already asked."

Draconia came back inside. She knelt far enough from the fire to keep her hem out of the ash and waited while Hermione found the name of the visitor who had delivered the notice. When they finally left, McGonagall had three names in her notebook, and Hermione was hungry.

By Saturday afternoon, Harry had discovered that asking Draconia on a date had not spared him the difficulty of arranging one. He had suggested the lake yesterday. Then he had spent breakfast watching rain soften the remaining snow and imagining the two of them standing beside frozen water, pretending not to be cold. Somewhere indoors would be better. He needed to tell her that before Sunday, preferably without making it sound as though he had forgotten what he had asked.

She emerged from the library carrying a sheaf of parchment tied with a red ribbon. A loose strand of hair had caught beneath it; she freed the strand while using one shoulder to keep the door from closing on a younger student. Harry moved forward to hold it for her. She thanked him, glanced up, and stopped adjusting the papers. Hermione had gone to speak to Cedric about other families receiving notices. Ron was at the pitch. Harry had rehearsed one perfectly ordinary sentence on his way upstairs and could no longer remember its beginning.

"About tomorrow," he said.

Draconia paused with one hand upon the library door. "You haven't changed your mind?"

"No. No, I was thinking somewhere warmer than the lake. You did say yes to somewhere inside if it was too cold."

"I remember what I said, Potter."

She was smiling now. He felt his shoulders loosen and wished she had started doing that sooner.

"I'm trying to arrange somewhere we can talk without Madam Pince threatening to throw us out. No essays. Just us."

"You could simply whisper."

"I'd spend the whole afternoon wondering whether you could hear me."

"I could lean closer."

Students crossed behind her, forcing Harry to move against the wall. Draconia followed, carrying the parchment between them until she lowered it to her side. Harry looked at the ribbon rather than her mouth, which did not help much.

"You're making this difficult," he said.

"You already asked me on a date. I already said yes. Which part are we doing now?"

"Making sure you don't arrive by the lake and find me in a greenhouse."

Her eyes brightened. Harry realised what he had given away.

"A greenhouse?"

"Possibly. I still have to ask Neville which plants to avoid."

"A sensible precaution."

He grinned before he could arrange anything more dignified, and her composure gave way too. For a moment he saw the girl from Madam Malkin's: pleased with herself, interested in him and willing to make both things visible. He no longer had to guess whether she wanted the afternoon. Arranging it ought to have been easy after that. His hands had not accepted the argument.

"Three o'clock?" he asked. "I'll tell you which door once Sprout agrees. If she says no, I'll find somewhere else."

"You might want to ask before borrowing her chairs."

"That's why I'm on my way to Neville."

"You were walking towards the stairs to Gryffindor."

"I got distracted."

She touched the front of his jumper, smoothing a crease which did not need her attention. The movement was small enough for passing students to miss; Harry felt it everywhere. Draconia glanced up at him, suddenly less certain of her teasing.

"I am looking forward to it," she said.

"So am I. I just want it to go well."

"We don't need an entire afternoon planned. I'd like to sit with you. Tea would be nice."

He nodded, grateful for the smallness of the request. He could manage tea. He had watched people make it in the Hawthorn kitchen for long enough, and nobody there behaved as though choosing the wrong cup might end a friendship.

"Tea, then."

"And something to eat. You will remember?"

"I'll consult an expert."

"Try one who doesn't put tricks in the biscuits."

She went back into the library. Harry stood outside it long enough for a portrait to enquire whether he intended to guard the door until Sunday.

"Only until I remember where I was going," Harry said.

The portrait considered him. "Young lady?"

"Yes."

"Ah."

Harry refused to give a painting the satisfaction of another answer. He took the stairs down and turned towards the courtyard exit, where a draught rattled the latch. He had promised somewhere warm. Neville would know where to start.

On Sunday afternoon, Harry had been in Greenhouse Three long enough for his hands to stop aching with cold. He rubbed the last damp patch from a cup and set it beside the other, then moved both back from the edge of the bench. Every time he looked at the arrangement he found something else to straighten. Professor Sprout had permitted them its small workroom after Neville explained what Harry wanted. She required him to leave the potting benches usable, avoid the cupboard labelled Carnivorous Cuttings and finish before the evening watering. Harry considered these reasonable conditions for romance.

The greenhouse stood at the edge of the kitchen garden, where snow had begun retreating beneath rows of dark yew. Its windows were clouded with moisture. Pots crowded the shelves inside; some held sleeping roots, others flowers Harry had never seen before. The workroom smelled of earth and something citrus which grew in a shallow bowl near the door. Neville lent him two chairs. Hagrid supplied a kettle. Hermione, upon discovering that Harry intended to serve tea without remembering cups, supplied those. He had asked her to choose a place to meet them for dinner afterwards. She said she would find them, then checked whether he had remembered sugar. He had not.

Ron had offered to supply conversation too, then stopped when Harry pointed out that this would require him to be there. His final contribution had been a packet of biscuits bought from the twins and a promise that the wrapper was ordinary. Harry had tested one in the common room before bringing the rest. It tasted of ginger. After five minutes he still possessed his own tongue, which he considered encouraging. Ron had eaten another with exaggerated patience to prove he trusted his brothers, then quietly checked the inside of the wrapper.

Draconia arrived just before three. She had left her school robes behind and wore a cream jumper with the sleeves folded neatly at her wrists. Harry saw her through the misted glass and forgot to remove the teapot from its warming stand until it began rattling.

"Should I come back?" she asked from the doorway.

"No. Come in. I think I left it warming for too long." He lifted the pot from the stand before it could rattle again.

She closed the door. Cold air travelled across the floor around her boots, carrying the smell of melting snow. As she unwound her scarf, her eyes moved over the chairs, the cups and the plate of biscuits. Harry had declined Hagrid's offer of rock cakes with an explanation about already having enough food; he hoped Hagrid would never ask what he had brought instead. Draconia draped her scarf over the back of a chair and touched one cup, checking its warmth before looking at him.

"You consulted Neville."

"I wanted somewhere warm. He knows which buildings bite."

"Good. I would rather discover that before we sit down."

She sat. Harry poured the tea and discovered his hands had become unhelpfully aware of themselves. A little spilled into the saucer. Draconia accepted the cup and looked past his shoulder at a shelf of flowering plants.

"Do you know what those are?"

"Not yet. Neville lent me the labels."

Harry reached for the little pile of cards and promptly knocked one beneath the bench. He crouched to retrieve it, bumped the underside of the wood with his shoulder and waited a moment before straightening. Draconia steadied the teapot and shifted it away from his elbow. She did not pretend she hadn't seen him blush, but neither did she laugh until he did.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome. The blue ones?"

He read the card. "Winter bells. Apparently they're only blue if you forget to talk to them."

She leaned closer to the shelf. "Good afternoon."

Nothing happened.

"I don't think it works that quickly," Harry said.

"I was being polite."

He grinned and sat beside her. Beyond the inner door, a watering pipe gave a hollow knock. The sound was harmless enough to forget about, and after a minute he did.

For several minutes they talked about things which carried no immediate threat. Pansy had received six letters following Blaise's aunt's account of Hogsmeade and had answered each in a different handwriting. Professor Binns had continued lecturing after Umbridge entered his classroom, passed through her and finished a sentence from last Tuesday. Draconia's mother had spoken to the Grangers that morning about arrangements for the inspection, which had been set for Thursday, the seventh of March. By the time they finished, she knew what Mr Granger was baking for lunch and had received a detailed answer to a question about dental instruments she had meant to ask since their conversation at Christmas.

"What did she want to know?"

"How he cleans them. Father asked whether it would work on silver fittings. He claims it's a practical question."

"It might be."

"Then why did he ask how fast the drill turns?"

Harry laughed. "What did Mr Granger say?"

"He offered to show him. Father changed the subject."

"Your mother's told you all this?"

"She finds it delightful. She has been married to him a very long time."

She was laughing too. Harry watched her lift her cup and hide the last of the smile against its rim. He had spent years noticing when she was frightened, angry or trying to protect him. Her hair had loosened beside one ear, and the smile came back before she managed to hide it. He wondered why he had once been so certain that she never became flustered. Perhaps it had been easier to believe than noticing all the times he had made her feel that way.

A plant on the shelf gave a small, indignant cough.

Draconia turned towards it. "Did you invite an audience?"

"Neville said it reacts to dry air."

"The windows are dripping."

"Perhaps it disapproves."

She set down the cup and reached across the narrow gap between their chairs. Harry took her hand. The difference from the library was absurdly large. There was no pile of books to shift, no nightmare to recover from. He could have left his hand upon his own knee. Instead he turned it beneath hers, settling their fingers together, and felt the ridiculous urge to tell somebody that he had finally got one thing right.

Her thumb moved once across his knuckles.

"You are very quiet."

"I'm trying not to say something which sends you back to the castle."

"You have said considerably worse things in the castle. I continued visiting."

"Loyalty to a difficult case."

"Interest in a difficult boy."

He felt heat reach his face. Draconia watched it with an expression he would once have called smug. Now he could see how much courage it had cost her to say the sentence.

"Is this what you thought it would be like?" he asked.

"This afternoon?"

"Being here with me. Not having to guess whether it's a date."

She studied their hands. Through the workroom's open inner door, water dripped from an overhead pipe into a trough. A leaf turned slowly towards the warmth of the kettle.

"I thought you would notice sooner," she said. "Then, whenever you seemed about to, I said something unhelpful about your hair."

"Your compliments need work too, then."

Her smile faded into something softer.

"I imagined feeling certain once I knew you wanted me. I thought uncertainty belonged to the part before that."

Harry thought of Blaise walking beside her in Hogsmeade and how quickly he had decided there was something to fear. Then he remembered standing outside the library, unable to ask for an afternoon he already knew he wanted. He had been waiting for certainty too. It had seemed sensible until he realised how much time they could lose waiting for it.

"And?"

"Now I know you want to be here, and I still worry I'll say the wrong thing. I thought that part would stop."

He wanted to promise she could not lose him. The words came easily, almost automatically; he knew how to offer himself in whatever form would quiet another person's fear. He stopped them before they became a promise about a war neither of them controlled.

"I want to do this," he said instead. "I want another afternoon. And to ask you again, properly, even if it isn't a Hogsmeade day."

Draconia lifted her eyes.

"I'd like that. Especially if you don't wait for another school notice to give you an excuse."

She turned his hand over, tracing a faint line near the base of his thumb. It had been there for months. Harry resisted the instinct to tell her it did not hurt; she had not asked whether it hurt. She was only touching him.

"Could you tell me when I get it wrong?" he asked.

"I usually do."

"Before you spend three days calling me Potter as if it's something you found on your shoe."

Draconia looked up with an expression of wounded innocence he would once have believed.

"Two days."

"Draco."

"I'll try. You could stop deciding I'm better off not knowing when something upsets you."

He nodded. It was easier to agree with her here than to imagine doing it while ashamed or frightened. He wanted to remember the afternoon when that happened: the damp glass, her fingers upon his palm, the fact that she had asked instead of assuming he would understand.

"I don't know how to make everything simple."

"I have known you for nearly five years, Harry. I wasn't expecting simple." Her fingers tightened around his. "I'd rather you told me something awkward than spent a week trying to guess what I want to hear."

He laughed, relieved enough that it hurt. Draconia glanced at the cleared end of the potting bench and rose. Harry moved the tea out of their way and sat beside her there, close enough that her knee pressed against his. His hand settled at her waist after she leaned into him. Nothing demanded speed.

"You can kiss me, you know," she murmured.

"I was getting to that."

"You were taking a very long time."

He kissed her. She tasted faintly of tea. The touch of her hand against his neck was familiar now, though the closeness still surprised him. He let himself notice things he had rushed past before: the warmth beneath the fold of her sleeve, the small sound she made when he drew back too far, the way she followed him with her eyes closed for another moment. When he kissed her again, she smiled against his mouth.

Somewhere above them the coughing plant shed a leaf. Draconia laughed and put her forehead against his shoulder.

"It has very firm views."

"We could take it outside."

"It might inform Sprout."

Harry held her while the laughter passed. The greenhouse windows shone pale gold as the afternoon lowered. He could hear voices outside, students returning from a walk by the lake. His scar did not hurt. The green stone beneath his shirt rested against him, no more intrusive for the moment than the edge of his collar. He had checked it often enough while arranging the cups; now he forgot it for a while, noticing instead the weight of her shoulder against his chest and the place where her boot rested beside his.

The workroom clock was approaching half past four when the watering pipes began to knock. Draconia sat up reluctantly, pulling her jumper straight where Harry's hand had creased it. He pretended to look for the biscuit wrapper. She caught him smiling and nudged his knee with hers. They washed the teapot in the little sink and returned Neville's chairs to their places. Harry read the label upon the coughing plant before leaving. It warned against sudden changes of temperature. He decided its opinions about kissing were its own problem.

On the way back, Draconia put her hand in his coat pocket beside his own. He closed his fingers around hers and walked more slowly than the cold required.

Up in the castle, Hermione had meant to work while they were gone. She had arranged everything necessary on the desk in the empty Arithmancy classroom: the report's circulation figures, three replies from parents, a copy of the liaison notice and her unfinished Charms essay. The arrangement gave her afternoon a sensible shape. She finished two calculations, wrote half a sentence about Summoning accuracy and looked at the clock.

Seven minutes had passed since she last looked. Before that, she had spent nearly an hour drafting an answer to a question nobody had yet asked her parents. The page contained three beginnings and no sentence she wanted to keep. She turned it face down. She disliked herself for knowing how long Harry had been with Draconia. She disliked herself more for returning to the figures and discovering that the number she had just written belonged to yesterday's column. The clock clicked again. She crossed out the mistake, copied the correct figure and lost it while turning to the next page.

Her stomach sank. For a moment, she could not tell whether the room had already held this afternoon. The chalk mark beneath the window seemed familiar in the wrong way; she remembered a page turning, a hand closing upon the table, the sound of that same minute reaching its end twice. Her fingers moved instinctively towards the place where the Time-Turner's chain used to lie.

There was no chain. She had returned it more than a year and a half ago. She knew the date. She could picture herself handing it back and remember the relief of its weight leaving her neck. Knowing did not quite settle the feeling that she had forgotten to turn something, or already turned it and failed to count.

Hermione laid both palms flat upon the desk. The wood was cool. Her ink bottle stood on the right. The clock read four twenty-six. Sunday. She had eaten lunch. Neville had asked whether she wanted to visit the next Herbology exhibition in the spring. Harry had asked Draconia out on Friday and confirmed the plans yesterday. Hermione had given him cups this morning because she wanted their afternoon to go well.

She wanted it to go well. She did not want to have to want it quite so hard. Outside the classroom, shoes scuffed along the landing and a boy called that he had forgotten his bag. The sound came once, followed by a door shutting farther away. Hermione listened until she was certain of that. Once. Then she slid her unfinished letter beneath the Charms essay and stood to open the window.

The latch was stiff, resisting her until she braced one hand against the sill and pulled with the other. Cold air entered with the smell of the wet grounds; she stood in it for a moment, feeling the chill reach her wrists beneath her sleeves. It cleared the stuffiness she had not noticed while sitting. Below, two students were crossing the courtyard with a blanket held over their heads. One slipped. The other laughed, then helped him up. Hermione watched them reach the archway and shut the window before the fern outside her door could complain about the temperature.

"How did you know I was here?"

"Neville. He said you'd gone to work. He left the fern outside because the chalk dust dries its leaves."

Draconia collected the fallen pages before putting down the cups. Her face was unmistakably happy. Hermione had seen it often enough in little flashes; seeing it without concealment gave the afternoon a cruel clarity.

"Was it nice?" she asked.

Draconia straightened, still holding one of the fallen pages. "It was. We had tea. There was a plant which objected to us enjoying ourselves."

Hermione nodded, then looked away. She had asked. Draconia had answered. Neither had done anything wrong. She had known what he would use it for. Seeing it returned somehow made the afternoon harder to imagine away.

"You don't have to sound apologetic."

"I am trying not to. It's harder than I expected."

That almost made Hermione smile. She picked up the Charms essay and discovered the same unfinished sentence for the third time. Draconia rested a hand on the desk beside her. The wood gave a warning creak when she leaned upon it, and she straightened at once. Hermione watched her eye the desk with suspicion. This was the same girl who had once accused a library chair of having been designed to encourage poor posture.

"How long have you been here?"

"Since three."

"Working?"

"Mostly."

"Granger."

Hermione gave up. She placed the essay between them, where the single line and its repeated corrections were impossible to disguise.

"The clock started feeling wrong again. I couldn't tell whether I'd heard the same minute before."

The happiness in Draconia's expression did not vanish, but she set it aside with care. Hermione appreciated that more than a frightened question.

"Is it happening now?"

"No. I know where I am. I just couldn't tell whether I had done this before."

Draconia reached for the clock, then stopped.

"Would moving it help?"

Hermione thought about the sound.

"Turning it round."

Draconia turned the clock towards the wall. It still ticked, but Hermione no longer had to watch the hand prove something every few seconds.

"No. The last one was in January. It stopped, and I thought—" Hermione looked at the corrections on her essay. "I thought I was getting better."

She could hear how accusing she sounded, as though the improvement had been an assurance somebody gave her and then withdrew. Draconia pulled out the chair beside her. It dragged upon the flagstones, loudly enough to make both of them glance towards the open door.

"One bad afternoon doesn't mean you haven't been. But she ought to know about it."

Hermione pressed the heel of her hand against one eye.

"Don't use the sensible voice. I hear enough of that in my own head."

Draconia sat in the chair she had pulled close.

"Would you prefer my entirely unreasonable opinion of the Ministry?"

"Possibly."

"It requires several words you previously asked me not to use in the library."

Hermione laughed. The sound escaped before she could stop it, and the relief of hearing something unexpected from herself made her almost cry.

"I am happy for you," she said.

Draconia waited.

"I am. I just don't know what to do with the rest."

"You don't have to explain it now."

"I might need to say it sometimes. Even when it makes me sound horrible."

"You haven't sounded horrible. You've sounded upset. I can hear that without wishing you hadn't told me."

Hermione lifted her head. "I don't want you afraid to tell me when something good happens."

"I don't want you to think you must make it easier for me."

A drop of water fell from one of the cups onto the desk. Draconia caught it with the cloth wrapped around them, then passed the cloth to Hermione when she realised she was looking for something to wipe her face with. It smelled faintly of tea. Hermione pressed a dry corner beneath one eye, feeling absurd and grateful. Outside, somebody called for a lost Gobstone and received advice from a portrait which clearly misunderstood the game.

Hermione could not resolve more than four years of affection at a desk in half an hour. Draconia stayed beside her while she folded the cloth. Her jumper sleeve had slipped over one hand; there was soil beneath the edge of her thumbnail. Hermione had been trying so hard to understand what had changed between them that she had almost forgotten all the ordinary things which had not. Draconia still knew when she was lying about work. "Neville asked about the Herbology exhibition," she said eventually.

"I said I'd like to go. I also said I wasn't certain what I could promise beyond that."

"What did he say?"

"That I could decide about the rest when I knew. He wanted to show me the new glasshouse first."

Draconia smiled. "That sounds like Neville."

"He looks so pleased when I say yes. I like spending time with him, Draco. I do. Then I start wondering whether he thinks I'm certain about everything else."

"You told him you weren't certain. That sounds fairly clear. If you're worried, ask him again—but let him answer. He might simply want you to see the glasshouse with him."

Hermione exhaled. The answer was irritatingly difficult to disagree with. They put the essays away. Draconia rinsed the cups at the small classroom sink while Hermione wrote Shah a short letter about the afternoon. She described what had happened without attempting a diagnosis or attaching a revised twenty-page procedure. It took four sentences. Draconia lent her the seal Harry had given her at Christmas, turning it so Hermione could press the impression without smearing the wax. The otter lay beside the serpent as neatly as Harry had meant it to. Hermione addressed the envelope before she could decide that four sentences were insufficient and begin again.

"Did you eat the biscuits?" Hermione asked as they packed the last book.

Draconia looked at her. "Most of them."

"Harry checked them for tricks. Ron said he was offended."

"Was he?"

"He'd checked them first."

Draconia laughed, and this time Hermione found it easier to laugh with her. They went to dinner together, taking the fern from outside the door. Its leaves brushed Hermione's sleeve all the way down the stairs. She kept adjusting her grip to protect the new growth, thinking about an afternoon in the spring which she had chosen for herself.

Two people asked to leave the D.A. in the last week of February. The first request reached Harry between lessons, while he was still trying to decide whether the Transfiguration essay in his bag could survive another day without a conclusion.

Cedric found him outside Transfiguration and waited until the crowd had thinned. Megan Jones's mother had received an inspection notice about her apothecary. It mentioned the safety report but cited her business's potion-storage licence. Megan had signed only one account, about a corridor search which had delayed her scheduled dose of medicine. Nothing in it criticised an individual officer by name. Harry remembered the passage: a prefect had offered to fetch her bag and been told to wait until the search was finished. He had read it three times before the report went out, angrier each time. He had not imagined a notice arriving at an apothecary because somebody let a customer read it.

"Her mum left a copy beside the till," Cedric said. "Someone asked where it came from. She told them her daughter's school. The notice arrived yesterday."

Cedric waited while two first-years hurried past, arguing about who had left a book in the classroom. Then he lowered his voice.

"She wants the original back. And her name removed from anything we keep."

Harry's first reaction was outrage on her behalf. His second, less generous reaction involved wanting to explain that her published account was already anonymous, that they had taken precautions, that her mother should not have to give the Ministry exactly what it wanted. He recognised the second reaction before speaking.

"Then we give it back."

Cedric watched him. "And about leaving?"

"She can go. I hate why she has to, but I'm not going to make it harder."

"Good. Come and tell her that. She thinks you might be disappointed."

Megan waited in an empty corridor beside the courtyard. Rainwater had gathered in the worn hollow beneath the nearest arch; every few seconds a drop from the roof broke its reflection of the sky. A draught tugged at the notice pinned behind her. Harry had passed this spot a hundred times on the way to lunch. Today the walk towards her felt longer. She had not brought her wand out of her bag. Harry noticed the strap wound twice around her wrist and the way she kept her face turned towards the window while Cedric approached.

"I'm sorry," she said before Harry spoke.

"You don't have to be."

"It isn't that I think you're wrong."

"I know."

She finally looked at him. Her eyes were swollen, and he wondered how often she had made herself explain this decision since the letter arrived.

"Mum can't lose the shop. It's just us."

"You don't have to explain any more," Harry said. "Hermione will give you the original. We'll remove the connection to your name from the private records. You won't have to come to a meeting to collect it."

Megan swallowed. "Can you remove it from the report?"

Harry could not. Copies had reached shops, classrooms and another country. The sentence lived in places no one person could collect.

"We can leave it out of every new version," he said. "We can't recall the ones already printed."

Her chin trembled. She nodded as though agreeing might make the limitation kinder.

"I should have thought."

"That doesn't make this your fault," Harry said. "Or your mum's. She should be allowed to leave a school report on her own counter."

Megan looked towards Cedric, then back at him.

"You can't stop them coming."

"No. I wish I could." He swallowed. "We should have explained more about what might happen."

Cedric said nothing. Harry was grateful; he needed to finish the admission without somebody sharing it too quickly.

"We talked about names and copies," Harry said. "We didn't talk enough about what could happen after the copies left us. I'm sorry."

Megan unwrapped the strap from her wrist. The skin beneath it was red.

"Can I still ask Neville about shields?"

"Of course. You can ask any of us."

"Even if I don't come back?"

Harry thought of the bent candlestick and the reasons it had mattered before anyone gave it a plaque.

"Especially then."

She wiped one eye with the heel of her hand, embarrassed at being seen doing it. Harry looked through the archway while she found a handkerchief. Cedric asked whether she wanted him to come when she collected her statement, and she nodded. After she left, Harry stood beside the rainwater with his unfinished essay digging into his side through the bag.

"She'll still have us," Cedric said.

"I know."

"She might need telling more than once."

Harry nodded. He could do that much. It would not give her mother a quiet week, but he did not want Megan thinking she had to earn help by returning to a meeting she was frightened to attend.

At Thursday's meeting, on the twenty-ninth of February, Harry noticed the missing spaces before anyone said anything. Harry had asked the room for twenty-nine chairs. He counted them before the others arrived, finding the neat circle smaller than the one they had used last week. Beside Hannah's chair, there was enough space to put a bag where Megan used to sit. He moved the chairs closer before anyone could wonder why he was looking at them. A Ravenclaw fourth-year had sent word through Anthony that his guardian wanted him to stop attending until the Ministry review ended. Nobody had asked for his reason beyond that.

Hermione had returned both original statements in separate envelopes before dinner. During the afternoon she had checked where the names appeared in their own records, and Draconia helped her remove the entries identifying the accounts. The next printing would leave those passages out. With both pupils' agreement, Hermione also severed their coins' links to the master and checked that neither could receive another meeting time. They kept the ordinary coins. Harry had watched her mark the changes upon their working copy without adding a word about why the children had asked. They did not discuss the circumstances publicly. Harry announced that people had taken a break and would receive the same help if they asked for it tomorrow.

Zacharias raised a hand.

"Could they tell someone where we meet?"

Harry looked towards the front door. He had shut it himself after the last pair arrived; for the moment, it stayed shut. He could not use that to answer Zacharias.

"Anyone here knows the place," Cedric said. "Leaving doesn't make them less trustworthy than the rest of us. And being put under pressure wouldn't make this easy for anybody."

"That's comforting."

"It's why we need to talk about it."

Pansy sat with her ankles crossed beneath her chair. "Most of the school has worked out that we meet somewhere. The Ministry doesn't need a confession to suspect it."

"It needs a place," Anthony said.

"Then we don't use the same corridor every time we collect statements," Hermione replied. "And we don't leave anybody's papers in here. If a message worries you, ask one of us quietly when you see us in the Hall. Don't announce it across a table, and don't go looking for a sender on your own. A reply reaches the master coin; it doesn't tell you where the person is."

Harry noticed Theodore standing near the secondary exit. He had listened without contributing, one thumb beneath the cuff of his sleeve. When Hermione mentioned the papers, his attention moved briefly towards her bag.

"That includes us," Harry said. "If I tell you to go, you leave. If someone else tells you to go, you don't wait for me to approve it."

"What if you're the one in trouble?" Neville asked.

The question brought every face back towards him. Harry could answer it by repeating the rule about shared risk. He could also acknowledge that all of them had watched him make himself the exception.

"Then someone else makes the decision about helping me. I don't get to order everybody away and call that leadership."

Ron leaned towards Ginny. "We should get that in writing."

"We have witnesses," she replied.

Laughter travelled through the chairs. Harry let it settle before explaining the evening's exercise. He was glad Neville had asked. He would have preferred Neville to ask when they were alone, which was probably one reason somebody needed to say it in front of the others. The room built a corridor across its practice floor. Low walls offered cover; high windows admitted flickering light. At one end stood three doors. One opened freely, another required a countercharm and the third was painted onto stone. Harry had learnt not to assume that looking like an exit made something useful.

Each team had two rescuers and a person pretending to be injured. They had to reach an exit and get a message to a communication point, deciding who could do which part as they went. Each person playing a casualty received a card describing what they could and could not do. The injuries were pretend. Before starting, they checked the strength of the practice jinxes and agreed the areas they could aim at: sleeves and boots, with heads and exposed skin left alone. Anyone could call a halt. Hermione demonstrated the blue marking charm on an empty robe so the younger pupils could see what it did before volunteering. At the communication point, a bell would sound only when the message supplied a location, a number of people and the immediate danger.

"Don't add something halfway through because you think your turn's too easy," Harry said.

Fred lowered his hand.

"Thank you," Hermione said, without looking at him.

Ron lay on the floor for the first attempt and announced that he required a biscuit before agreeing to be rescued. He had finished it before Hannah brought the staves over; Hermione insisted he sit up and swallow rather than attempt the exercise with food in his mouth. His card described him as dazed but able to walk with help; Hannah and Pansy had not read that far. They made a stretcher from two staves and a length of cloth, lifted him a few inches, and immediately found it tilting towards Pansy. Ron grabbed the cloth at either side. His outrage might have been more convincing if he had not started laughing halfway through it.

"Put him down," Harry called. "Ask him what he can do."

Pansy crouched beside Ron. "Can you stand?"

"I could before you tried that."

"Then you'll be delighted that we're changing the plan," Pansy said. "Hannah, help me get him up."

They put him down and completed the exercise while he complained behind them. Neville and Susan reached the countercharm door together, supporting Lee between them. His card had given him a twisted ankle, and he kept the affected foot off the floor while they worked. Neville held the shield while Susan worked the lock. Lee leaned against the wall, good foot planted firmly beneath him. Susan tried the countercharm they had practised on Tuesday; the lock flashed purple and remained shut.

Harry watched them share the difficulty. Neville's wand hand began trembling a little before Susan finished the lock. She saw it and asked him to take over the countercharm. Lee kept his good foot planted beside the wall while Susan stepped into Neville's place, raising her shield before he lowered his. They lost a few seconds making the change. The next jinx struck Susan's spell instead of Neville's sleeve. Neville steadied his wand with his other hand, finished the sequence Susan had begun and heard the lock click open.

His turn went less neatly. Ginny lay near the painted door, holding still the arm her card said she could not use. Harry saw the wand fall beside her and moved too quickly. Ron caught the back of his robes before a blue-marking jinx struck the place he had been about to step.

"That's stone," Ron said.

"I know."

"You're running towards it."

Harry stopped. The spell had made the corridor look longer than it was; urgency had done the rest. He crouched behind a low wall and let Ron cover Ginny while he checked the other routes. They finished third. Ginny reached the communication point under her own power, good hand upon Ron's shoulder, and recited the location with enough irritation to make the bell sound twice. Harry had a blue mark across one sleeve and another upon his boot.

"I said slow down," Ron reminded him as they left the practice corridor.

"Yes. I heard you."

"Wasn't obvious at the time."

Harry glanced back at the painted door. From this side he could see the shallow brush marks around the false handle. He had looked straight at it and seen the exit he wanted. He brushed blue dust off his sleeve, getting half of it onto his trousers instead. Harry disliked finishing third more than he wanted anyone to know. Draconia clearly knew. She waited until they returned to the chairs, then passed him water instead of commentary.

"You stopped," she said.

He accepted the cup.

"After someone grabbed me."

"You listened when Ron stopped you. That counts too. You don't have to notice everything before he does."

Across the room, Cedric had already begun the next attempt. Harry drank the water and stayed where he could see the whole corridor.

March arrived with rain. Remus came for Friday's lesson and found Harry in muddy Quidditch robes, still carrying the broom he should have put away ten minutes earlier.

"You were due in the classroom ten minutes ago."

"Practice ran over."

"Was that agreed?"

"Angelina wanted one more run through the formation. I didn't tell her I had to leave."

Remus waited.

Harry shifted the broom. "I should have sent a message. Sorry."

Remus considered the broom in Harry's hand, the wet gloves tucked beneath his elbow and the smear of mud running from one knee to his boot. Outside the courtyard, rain drifted past the stone arches. The pitch was empty now, its distant hoops disappearing into the grey.

"How did you manage to fall off a broom during practice?"

"I landed."

"With your knee?"

"Not on purpose. The ground was further up than I expected."

Remus's visitor badge lay beneath his coat this time. Harry could see the edge of the pin at his collar. Remus tucked the wet corner of Harry's gloves more securely beneath his elbow before one could fall.

"Next time, tell Angelina you have an appointment. If she wants me to explain why, I will."

"She might make you practise too."

"I can offer considerable expertise in falling off things."

"Snape sent word that he cannot supervise tonight," Remus said. "So there's no mental work. We can take a walk, or leave the lesson until next week if you're too tired."

Harry shifted the broom to his other hand. He wanted a shower, dinner and the prospect of seeing Draconia in the library. He also wanted not to waste time with Remus merely because time was available again tomorrow. He had begun noticing that the Order made adult calendars unreliable.

"Walk. After I change?"

"I would appreciate that."

They took the path behind the greenhouses twenty minutes later. The rain had thinned into a fine mist which collected upon Remus's hair and made the shoulder of Harry's coat shine. Harry waited until they had passed the kitchen garden before asking.

"Did something happen?"

Remus glanced down.

"A loose stair."

"Where?"

"A building we were checking."

"We?"

"Tonks and I. Moody stayed near the access point."

Harry felt his own shoulders lower and resented the necessity of that relief.

"I'm not asking because I think you can't look after yourself."

"I know."

"Sorry."

"You are allowed to have an afternoon which isn't organised around my movements."

"My head hasn't accepted that yet."

"I moved."

Harry shook his head.

"The other doors?"

"After Ron stopped me."

"Here?"

"Only looking and choosing a route. No memory work, and no jinxes. I can add ordinary noise once you know what we're doing. You can tell me to stop."

"A voice calling for help. Mine. You would hear it before we tried the exercise."

"All right. But not anybody else's voice."

"Agreed."

"You want me to stop and look while someone's being hurt?"

"Especially then. It will feel like wasting time."

Harry dragged the toe of his boot through the gravel.

"Sometimes it is."

"Yes."

The admission surprised him. Remus drew his stick back from the doorpost and leaned upon it, letting the rain reach both of them without disguising the difficulty.

"I won't tell you that stopping always works," Remus said. "But if you run into something you haven't seen, somebody else may have to come for you as well. We can practise noticing a little sooner. That's what I'm asking you to try."

They used the boathouse. Remus positioned three objects behind its doors: a lamp, a cork life ring and an empty chair. Harry stood twenty feet away. With each opening he had to name the light, the route and something which would obstruct him before deciding where to move. At first it was absurdly easy. Harry named the lamp and the coil of rope, chose a route round the chair and wondered whether he could have learned as much while sitting somewhere warm.

Remus let him hear the next part before using it. A simple repeating charm carried his own voice from the boathouse: I need help by the steps. It sounded rather peculiar to hear it while he stood beside Harry. There was no scream, no stranger pretending to be a child. Harry asked him to play it again, then nodded.

On the next attempt, the door opened while the voice called. A charm shifted the lamplight across the floor. Harry stepped forward before naming the floor.

Remus held up a hand. "Pause there. Tell me what you can see before you move."

Harry knew Remus was standing three feet away. His body still wanted to answer the voice before his eyes had finished finding the threshold. That irritated him almost as much as being stopped.

"I heard it."

"So did I. What could you see?"

Harry forced himself to check. Ropes crossed the threshold. Water shone along the left wall. The chair had moved nearer to the steps. He named all three and found the clear route. They continued until the daylight began to fade, taking a break after Harry's fourth attempt. Once he had named the way out, Remus asked him to say where a second person could stand without blocking it. On another turn he moved the lamp, leaving the floor less visible. Harry had to admit that he could not see enough to run across it. Remus handed him a torch rather than praising him for the admission.

Harry was wet, cold and annoyed at how much improvement depended on doing simple things repeatedly. He had also begun noticing the ropes without having to search for them. Remus's ankle was stiff enough that he sat upon the step between attempts, giving the instructions from there.

"Your turn," Harry said.

Remus raised an eyebrow.

"I know the arrangement."

"Then stop changing it."

Harry moved the life ring and one rope, keeping the practice free of jinxes. Remus agreed to one attempt. He named the light and the clear route without standing, then pointed to a loose end of rope which Harry had failed to tuck away. Harry changed it and tried again. They made three attempts, with Remus staying upon the step for all of them. Harry found considerable pleasure in making a teacher admit that he had not noticed something.

On the third, Remus pointed to the threshold, caught Harry moving the chair and laughed.

"There. You are enjoying teaching."

Harry grinned. "Only particular students."

They sat beneath the overhang to recover. Remus poured tea from a flask, and Harry held the small metal cup between his hands while warmth returned to his fingers. The castle's windows had begun to glow above them. He could make out lights along the third-floor passage. Hermione had said she wanted to finish her essay before dinner; he wondered whether she had managed it. Draconia's common room lay beneath the lake; from the shore, he could see nothing of it.

"How do you stop thinking about all the people you can't reach?" he asked.

Remus turned the flask lid in his hands.

"Badly, sometimes. I remember what you saw in that Christmas memory. I have spent years making leaving seem like consideration. It has often been cowardice."

Harry listened. The lake slapped quietly against the mooring posts.

"Sirius knows when I'm doing it now," Remus said. "He asks whether I actually want to leave, or whether I've decided everyone would prefer it. I'm trying to answer the question before finding an excuse."

"Sirius will help."

"He has considerable enthusiasm for the task."

Remus unwound the scarf at his neck and laid its dry end across his knee. Harry recognised it as one Sirius had worn over Christmas.

"He sent that this morning," Remus added, following his glance. "Apparently I can't be trusted to notice weather."

"He put two scarves in my trunk in September."

"Only two?"

Harry laughed. Remus smiled down at the wool for a moment before winding it round his neck again.

"So will I."

"I had noticed."

Harry finished the tea. Remus accepted the cup without standing immediately, giving his ankle another few minutes of rest. The quiet suited them. Harry's shoulder rested against Remus's as the lamps came on in the windows above the entrance hall. When they finally rose, Harry carried Remus's bag. Remus began to object, changed his mind and took the offered shoulder on the steepest part of the path.

That night, Harry dreamed of trying to reach the Cup. His hand crossed the same stretch of mud again and again without getting closer. Behind the headstone, Cedric breathed once, too loudly. A black shoe moved towards the sound. He woke with his fingers locked around the bedpost. For a few seconds he could not release it. The dormitory was dark, his shoulder hurt from the angle at which he had dragged himself upright and his heart kept beating as though something had to be maintained through its rhythm. He could feel the pressure of the wood against each finger, but could not make the feeling belong to a bed.

A draught lifted the edge of the curtains. Beyond them, somebody turned over and the springs gave a familiar creak. Harry stared at the brass ring holding his curtains open until he could see its screw. There had been no curtain rings in the graveyard. He tried to reach for the water and knocked the glass sideways. It struck the bedside table before falling onto the rug, the sound abruptly loud in the sleeping room.

Ron stirred behind his curtains, then pushed them apart.

"Vision?"

Harry tried to decide. There had been no new room, no borrowed body, no detail he could check. He remembered the mud; he remembered the shoe. He had lived those things before.

"Nightmare. I think."

Ron sat up and lit the lamp behind his curtains.

"Want the mirror?"

"Not yet."

The answer did not mean he wanted to hide. He wanted one minute in which the room became the room. Ron stayed on his own bed and talked about the match formation, which was still either inspired or suicidal. He did not require Harry to contribute. "He drew them backwards," Ron said. "I've got proof."

Harry loosened his grip upon the post one finger at a time. The last two hurt when he moved them. Ron noticed him looking at his hand and asked whether he wanted the lamp nearer. Harry shook his head; the small circle of light upon the other bed was enough.

"There was nothing new," he said at last.

Ron nodded. "All right. Tell Sirius that too when you call."

Then he bent to retrieve the fallen glass, checked it for cracks in the lamplight and dried the water before it could reach Dean's slippers. Harry's scar was quiet. The green charm felt cool. He put on the headphones Hermione had given him and found the tape marked Quiet Enough to Sleep. For once, it was not quiet enough to sleep; it was quiet enough to sit awake without hearing a shoe move over dirt.

He called Sirius after the first song. Sirius answered with his eyes half closed. Harry asked the fresh question they had agreed that evening; Sirius answered, then gave him his own. The familiar exchange took longer than usual because Harry lost a word halfway through. Sirius waited. Once they had checked, he asked which kind of bad night it had been, and Harry told him. Ron took over the glass long enough to show the room, then returned it without commentary. Sirius looked rumpled and entirely awake now. A lamp appeared beside his shoulder as he reached for it.

Harry showed him the reddened fingers. "Bedpost."

He did. Sirius watched, then asked which tape he had chosen. Harry read him the label and said it had not done what the label claimed.

"I doubt you can demand a refund from Hermione."

"She'll explain why I'm using it wrong."

"You probably are," Ron said.

Harry turned towards him.

"Sorry. Sounded like her."

Sirius's mouth twitched. Harry looked at Ron's rumpled hair, the blanket wrapped around his shoulders and the glass he had rescued from the floor. Ron had been pulled out of sleep too. He was trying to make the dormitory feel like somewhere Harry could be annoyed with him. It was beginning to work. They stayed on the call until Harry could feel the weight of the blanket across his knees. When the mirror darkened, Ron went back to sleep and Harry did not for another hour.

He attended breakfast anyway. The Hall was too bright at first, and he chose the side of the bench which faced away from the windows. Hermione asked whether the headphones had behaved; he told her the tape helped and that he had spoken to Sirius. When Draconia came over, he said he had slept badly before she could ask.

She looked at his hand, then put the milk within reach. He drank his tea slowly while Ron continued arguing that Fred did not own an arrow. Harry still felt tired when the plates emptied. He did not have to recover quickly enough to make breakfast comfortable for everybody else.

After lunch on Saturday, Theodore asked to speak to them. They used an alcove near the library. Harry opened the Map beneath his sleeve and checked the adjoining passage before folding it away again. Filch was two floors below; the nearest Patrol members were near the courtyard. No one stood behind the tapestry covering the alcove's other entrance.

There was an armchair in the alcove, but nobody used it; its stuffing had begun escaping through one arm and Draconia had once found a spider inside the other. Hermione took a clean sheet from her bag. Theodore watched the movement and waited another moment before speaking.

"They're asking for equipment records," he said. "Everything imported to Hogwarts since September. Charms materials, classroom fittings, protective objects. Not just student belongings."

Hermione reached for her notebook.

"Who is asking?"

"The Commissioner's office. My access notice names the stores beneath the east stair. I told her I hadn't been there."

"Have you?" Draconia asked.

"No. It was easy to tell the truth this time."

He handed Hermione a copy of the notice. It authorised him to report movements involving unregistered equipment and persons with access to it. He read the word unregistered again, trying to remember whether anybody had defined it when they handed Theodore the notice.

"There's another part," Theodore said.

He drew a second envelope from inside his robes, smaller and sealed. He kept it against his leg instead of offering it with the other notice. Harry stopped reaching for the papers.

"A family solicitor asked me to confirm which relatives are permitted to make educational decisions on my behalf. The list includes people I have never met."

Harry watched him hold the envelope against his leg. The last time Theodore had brought a pink notice to Draconia, it had asked him to turn his father's name into a useful reason to watch other children. Now the same name seemed to be bringing people towards him.

"Do you have to answer?" Harry asked.

"I don't know."

The admission was almost inaudible. Theodore rubbed a thumb along the sealed edge of the envelope, careful not to break it. Harry had thought the letter was unopened until he saw the little tear beside the fold. It had been read and closed again, perhaps more than once.

"Are they asking you to sign something?" Hermione asked.

"To confirm I don't object to the list."

"Before they've told you what the people on it can do?"

Theodore looked at her. "Yes."

"Your father?"

"Has enough interests in your family's affairs that he should not be the only person you ask."

Theodore's eyes flicked towards hers.

"Would he know you asked?"

"He might know we're looking for someone independent," Draconia said. "I can ask Mother to keep the letter private. Neither of them needs to read it to find you an adviser."

Theodore looked at the letter for a long time. Then he loosened his grip enough for Hermione to read the name upon the back. Harry moved his own bag away from the chair, leaving room if Theodore wanted to sit. He did not, but his shoulders no longer touched the wall. He let Hermione copy the solicitor's name and agreed that Draconia could pass it to Narcissa. Hermione showed him the few words she had written before closing the notebook. Then he put the envelope back inside his robes and left through the uncovered entrance.

Harry checked the Map until Theodore reached the dungeon stairs. A name moving safely across parchment had become comforting. He reminded himself that the comfort lasted only where the parchment could see.

The equipment in the east stores was unpacked on Monday, the fourth of March. By Tuesday, Flitwick had requested a trial before any part of it was connected to the castle's permanent wards. By Wednesday, Harry sat in McGonagall's classroom listening to the conditions under which he could observe Thursday's trial. Rain ticked upon the tall windows behind him. Somebody had left a half-transfigured brass jug upon the front bench; its spout resembled a swan's neck, and it kept turning towards the door. A Ministry form lay upon the desk between them. She had crossed out two paragraphs and written her objections in the margin with such force that the ink showed through the back.

"You are not required to attend," she said. "Your guardian has agreed that you may. That is permission, Potter. It is not an appointment you can be disciplined for missing."

Harry read the remaining paragraphs. The proposed ward intercepted curses travelling between fixed points. According to the accompanying letter, a successful trial would permit its installation near entrances used by students and visiting staff. The letter named the attack upon the Hogwarts Express three times. It named communication once, among functions expected to remain unaffected. Harry put his thumb beneath the line and read it again. He knew what it felt like to wait for an answer from a mirror. He wanted the person who had written this sentence to know too.

"And they want the mirrors there because they're registered exceptions?"

"Professor Flitwick wants them there because something being expected to work is insufficient evidence that it will. Your friends have been invited on the same basis. They will decide separately."

"Can we leave while it's running?"

"At any time. You will stand away from the target, with a clear exit behind you. A member of staff will be on each side of the boundary. None of the test spells will be aimed at you."

"And if somebody says we should wait to finish?"

McGonagall looked at him over her spectacles.

"You may leave, Potter. The permission is written here, and Mr Lupin will be beside you. If anyone objects, he can deal with it after you are out."

Harry looked at the heading again. After Umbridge's wooden box, assurances printed beneath a crest made his skin feel too tight. McGonagall did not ask him to believe the paper. She waited while he read the handwritten changes, then showed him Flitwick's diagram: two ward anchors, a short length of empty corridor and a narrow strip marked for observers.

He could stand beside Remus. Sirius would be outside the field with the answering mirror. Neither arrangement depended upon Umbridge's approval.

"If I don't go, can Professor Flitwick check mine anyway?"

"With your permission."

The answer helped him decide. He wanted to see what happened to the glass he trusted, provided he could hand it over without having to remain. He traced the edge of the observation strip upon the diagram, leaving a faint mark where the parchment had softened beneath his thumb.

"I might go out even if nothing happens."

"Then Mr Black will be waiting on the other side."

Harry nodded. She had not made it sound like a waste of anybody's time.

"I'll come."

McGonagall passed him the amended copy.

"Keep this. I have already sent one to Mr Black."

"Will the Commissioner be there?"

"She has chosen to attend."

Harry folded the diagram into his pocket.

"Then I hope it stops irritating noises as well."

McGonagall removed her spectacles.

"We must not ask more of the prototype than its designers claim."

On Thursday afternoon, the stores corridor smelled of fresh limewash over old damp. The scent caught at the back of Harry's throat as he followed Remus down the shallow steps, one hand brushing the cool wall. Harry had passed the entrance to the stores passage hundreds of times without noticing the shallow stair beyond it. Below, a passage ran between the stores and an unused classroom, narrow enough for the ceiling to seem lower than it was. Crates stood against the far wall. Chalk marked where their corners had rested before the Ministry workmen moved them. A high window admitted a square of afternoon light, catching dust which drifted down whenever someone crossed the floor above.

The two anchors stood twelve feet apart. Beyond them, Flitwick had fixed a heavy stone target against the far wall and marked a clear line across the floor. The observers' strip ran beside the opposite wall, leading back to the stair without crossing the spells' path. Each was a block of grey stone fitted into a brass frame, with pale lines cut deep into its face. Copper threads connected the frames to a control plate upon a trestle table. Harry could hear the faintest hum before anything was switched on. Hermione had arrived ahead of him. She stood beside Flitwick, asking whether the field remained within the anchors if one lost power. Draconia was reading the consent form again. Across a chalk boundary, Sirius spoke to Dumbledore with his mirror already open in his hand.

Seeing him here was different from seeing him through glass. His hair was still damp from the weather; a drop fell from the shoulder of his coat when he turned. He raised the mirror to show Harry the corridor from his side.

"A remarkably poor picture of the person holding the other half," he said.

Harry's glass showed Sirius, the opposite wall and one of McGonagall's elbows.

"Move it. I'm trying to count your nose."

Sirius brought the mirror close enough to fill it with one eye. Harry laughed and completed the check properly. Hermione used her own pair to contact Narcissa. She was at Hawthorn, with the familiar low lamp upon the table behind her. The inspection at the Grangers' practice was over for the morning; she would speak to Hermione about it properly that evening. For now she showed them the room and answered a question about the call she and Hermione had arranged the previous day. Hermione wrote the time in her notebook. Draconia opened her separate connection to Hawthorn, where Lucius answered from the study. He and Narcissa had agreed to keep the two answering pieces in different rooms for the trial. Draconia checked the question they had chosen the night before, then left the glass open. Three pairs were ready: Harry's with Sirius, Hermione's with Narcissa, and Draconia's with her father.

Harry lowered his glass, leaving Sirius's face within sight. He could also see the real Sirius reach up to tuck his hair behind his ear. Watching the two movements agree settled him more than reading the form had done. Remus waited by the observation strip. He had brought his stick but no Ministry badge; McGonagall had issued the ordinary visitor's card and attached it to his coat herself. His ankle seemed better. When Harry joined him, he made room without asking how brave Harry felt about standing there. He pointed out a loose corner of the rug beside the stores door, well away from the field but close enough to catch a boot if they left quickly. Harry flattened it with his heel.

"That's better," Remus said.

It was a small enough task to let Harry finish it before he started considering everything else. Umbridge entered as the technicians finished checking the copper threads. She carried a folder with a gold Ministry seal upon its cover. Beside the grey walls, her pink robes looked almost aggressively clean.

"I trust everyone understands that this apparatus is intended to protect them."

"We understand its stated purpose," Dumbledore said.

The older technician cleared his throat. His name was Peake; Harry had heard Flitwick use it twice. He wore work gloves with the fingers cut away and appeared more interested in the control plate than in who could take credit for it.

"Ninety seconds for the live interval, unless Professor Flitwick ends it sooner," Peake said. "Professor Flitwick will cast towards the marked target. A coloured displacement charm first, then a controlled jinx. Both terminate against the stone if the field fails. Nobody crosses the target line. You may leave along the wall whenever you wish."

Harry checked the floor. Light, route, obstruction. The square of sun ended before the boundary. Nothing blocked the left-hand wall. Remus stood close enough for Harry to touch his sleeve, although Harry did not need to. Peake turned the control plate. The hum deepened. Pale lines travelled across the nearer anchor and appeared upon its partner a moment later. Between them, the air became faintly blurred, like the space above a warmed flagstone. The surface of Harry's mirror stayed bright.

Flitwick cast the first charm. A blue spark crossed the passage, reached the shimmer and divided. Two thin trails curled into the anchors. The target remained untouched. Umbridge made a satisfied sound. Hermione spoke to her mirror.

"Mrs Malfoy?"

Harry looked down at his own. Sirius's face had become still. The image held him halfway through a blink, one hand lifted towards the corner of the glass.

"Sirius?"

Across the corridor, the real Sirius answered at once.

"Here."

The glass repeated nothing. Harry turned it slightly, expecting the picture to shift with his wrist. Sirius's raised hand remained suspended beside his face. Harry glanced across the passage again. The real hand was already lowered. He knew which was Sirius. He knew exactly where Sirius stood. The unchanging picture still made his throat tighten.

Hermione's quill scratched.

"Both of these calls have stopped."

Draconia held up her own mirror. Lucius's image had frozen with his mouth half open. She spoke his name once, waited, then looked towards Flitwick rather than repeating the attempt.

"Mine too. Father was answering just before you cast."

Flitwick lowered his wand. No second spell crossed the passage.

"Disengage," he told Peake.

The technician turned the plate back. Its handle stopped halfway. He frowned, moved his other hand to the copper connection and said something quietly to his assistant. The field continued humming. Peake's assistant reached beneath the table for a wooden case, set it down and pulled out a pair of insulated clamps. Her hands were quick, but she paused while Peake showed her the fitting he wanted removed.

"The handle hasn't released it," he told Flitwick. "We're isolating the charge manually. Keep the target clear."

Harry did not look at the target. He was looking at the hand caught in his mirror, and his stomach tightened. Sirius was twenty feet away. He could see every movement of his face. Nevertheless, the frozen image in his hand had begun to resemble a closed door, and his thoughts were getting caught upon it.

"The exit is clear," Remus said beside him.

Harry knew. He had checked it. He made himself look again.

"Left wall."

"Yes."

They walked. Remus shortened his stride to match Harry's. Nobody pulled him; nobody waited for him to prove he could remain. At the edge of his vision, Draconia turned her head. He looked back at her long enough to show that he had seen her, then found the chalk line. Draconia followed Hermione, leaving enough space between them that they would not block the strip. Harry reached the chalk boundary and stepped across it.

Sirius's face moved in the glass. Harry heard the beginning of his name through it and looked up to see the real Sirius speaking. The sounds agreed again. Behind him, Hermione asked Narcissa to repeat her last words; Draconia's father's voice returned a moment later.

Harry kept looking between Sirius and the mirror. "Would the guardian alarm get through? If somebody tried to take it while I was in there?"

"We haven't tested that signal," Flitwick said. "So we mustn't promise that it would. We can check the alarm charm separately, without using any of you to set it off."

"Your picture stopped on my side too," Sirius told him. "I knew the call had failed. If I'd been at home, I wouldn't have known why, or what you needed."

Harry glanced back at the blurred strip. The alarm had brought help in Umbridge's office. He had started treating that as something which would always happen; seeing a familiar face go still had made the difference unpleasantly clear.

Peake's assistant drew a copper connection free with the insulated clamps. The hum stopped. Light left the anchors in two slow pulses. Dust continued falling through the square of sunshine as though nothing in the corridor had changed.

"Eighty-three seconds," Hermione said.

She wrote it beside field active, then marked the earlier moment when Flitwick had ordered it stopped. Eighty-three seconds was shorter than the planned trial. It was still much longer than anyone had intended to wait after asking for the power to be cut. Sirius stepped beside Harry without crossing the target line. He did not put the mirror away until Harry had finished looking at it.

Peake did not attempt to call the test a success. He sat upon the edge of the trestle table and removed his gloves. A burn had reddened one knuckle where it touched the copper fitting. Flitwick asked to see it, waited for Peake's nod and treated it before asking him to explain the control plate. The technician flexed his fingers and looked towards the fitting his assistant had placed in the wooden case. Harry was glad nobody had stepped over it in their hurry to leave.

"The intercept loop holds until the stored charge disperses," Peake said. "It should have released when I reversed the handle. We've tested it against incoming spells."

"What happened when you tested conversations across it?" Hermione asked.

He looked at her notebook.

"We checked a single link at low power, without a spell caught in the intercept loop. We didn't run a test like this one."

"Three student mirrors," Hermione said. "At the power you were planning to use in the corridor."

"Yes." He looked towards Flitwick. "That should have been tested before we brought it here."

The technician rubbed his unburnt hand across his forehead. Harry had expected him to argue that the mirrors were unusual, or that the students had used them incorrectly. Peake asked Hermione to keep the exact times and copied the failed stop into his own record. His assistant stood close enough to correct him when he wrote the wrong fitting number.

Flitwick held his wand above one anchor without reactivating it. A thin blue line traced the brass frame as he examined the remaining charge.

"It draws moving magic into the stones," he said. "That stopped the spell reaching the target. It also stopped your conversations getting out. The alarm uses the mirror's enchantment too, but we haven't measured how the field treats that signal. Until we have, you must not rely on it getting through."

"Even though it was supposed to be an exception?" Harry asked.

"Yes. Registration gives you permission to carry it; the field cannot read that permission. The design was supposed to let communication pass, and it hasn't."

Harry looked at Hermione's mirror. She had already spoken to Narcissa and confirmed that the interruption had lasted less than two minutes. Narcissa's face was gone now. Ordinary glass reflected the high window.

"We had three people answering," he said.

"You did," Flitwick replied. "Sirius was here; Mr and Mrs Malfoy were at Hawthorn. But every call still had to get through this field. Different people at the other ends couldn't change that."

Draconia touched the grey charm beneath her collar.

"The stones?"

"We did not trigger either stone, so we have not established what would happen to a warning." Flitwick glanced towards Harry. "I would like to borrow them for a short check this afternoon, then show you the results tomorrow morning. I would test a warning and the directional charm separately, using a demonstration attachment instead of either of you. Not here, and not with these anchors running."

Draconia undid the fastening beneath her collar. Harry hesitated over his, then took it off and placed it beside hers upon the cloth Flitwick laid out. The professor described the check before folding the cloth, including how he would keep it separate from the disconnected corridor equipment. They would have both stones back before dinner.

Harry nodded. The green stone lay in a fold of cloth beside the grey one. Without its weight against his chest, he kept feeling for the cord. He had agreed to lend it; he still wished he could make the professor finish the check in the next five minutes. Umbridge closed her folder.

"A brief interruption to private conversations does not invalidate a protective ward."

Sirius looked at her.

"You have just watched three children lose their way to call for help. And your equipment wouldn't switch off when the professor told it to."

"The field operated for less than its intended interval."

"It failed to switch off when instructed," McGonagall said.

Peake lowered his eyes. Dumbledore moved beside the control table, leaving the target and its marked line clear.

"No anchor will be connected to the school's permanent wards until the interference has been corrected and independently tested. The trial record will include Professor Flitwick's findings, the failed connections and the failure to disengage when instructed."

"Installation has been authorised by the Ministry."

"An authorisation is not a demonstration of safety."

Umbridge's fingers tightened upon the folder. Harry watched her measure the people in the corridor: guardian, deputy head, technician, three students carrying records. It was the first time all afternoon that she seemed to consider the test inconvenient.

"I will notify the Minister that the Headmaster has refused a security improvement."

"Send him the results," Dumbledore said. "He may find the description more useful."

Harry expected something sharper. It did not come. Peake asked Dumbledore whether he wanted the original trial record as well as the school copy, and Dumbledore said that both should be signed before the equipment left.

Umbridge stood beside the table while they did it. Harry watched her glance at Hermione's notebook. Hermione closed it only after Peake had checked the times with her. Dumbledore asked Peake to seal the control plate, instructed Flitwick to witness it and waited while the Ministry technician did so. Each anchor was disconnected from the other before anybody left. Peake gave Flitwick an unused sample of the same warding material and its specifications for the separate check he had proposed. The sample had not been part of the active trial.

The anchors went into separate crates. Peake marked the first fastening with red wax; Flitwick added his own mark beside it. Harry could still smell warm copper when the lid shut, but the blur in the air was gone. He could see the worn floor clearly all the way to the door. Sirius stood beside Harry while he put away the mirror.

"I could see you," Harry said quietly. "It still felt—"

He could not find the right comparison without bringing the graveyard into a room which had contained no attacker. Sirius rubbed one thumb along the seam of Harry's sleeve.

"I saw. Want to sit somewhere else?"

Harry did. They went upstairs together.

The unused classroom had been warm all afternoon. McGonagall had left a fire lit and a jug of water beside the window. Harry wondered how many small preparations happened around him without needing to announce themselves. Ron arrived with sandwiches after receiving Hermione's message. He looked around at the adults, handed Harry the plate and claimed he had intended to eat them himself until everyone made that impossible. Sirius took one. Ron stared at him, clearly reassessing whether his objection had been wise.

"He's very persuasive," Harry said.

"I heard sandwiches," Sirius replied. "I stopped listening after that."

Remus reached for the water jug. His hand caught the end of Sirius's damp cuff, and he pushed it away from the plate before pouring. Sirius watched him do it, then quietly took a second glass for him. Harry took two sandwiches.

For a while they talked through the test. Moving beyond the local field had restored all three mirror connections. Hermione had noted each answer separately rather than assuming that one working pair meant the others had recovered. They had tested one short stretch of corridor. Harry wanted to know whether a larger arrangement could affect the whole castle; Remus said that nobody present had tested that, and they should ask Flitwick rather than decide it from the diagram. Flitwick would discuss the paired charms with them the following morning, after the brief check he was doing now. While their stones were with Flitwick, they had no warning from them at all. Even after they were returned, a quiet stone would tell them only that it had not reacted; the separate check might show another reason it could stay quiet.

Hermione drew the corridor in her notebook. Ron added the chalk boundary, then a second position beyond it where a person could wait with a working mirror. The page began to resemble their practice floor. Hermione noticed it too and opened the notebook to the exercise they had used last Thursday. There was Ron's name beside a note about reading the casualty card before making a stretcher. His card had said he could walk; beneath that, someone had added that he would stop complaining only when rescued.

"I was perfectly clear about the biscuit," he said.

Harry's laugh came out unexpectedly loud in the quiet classroom. The tension in his shoulders eased enough for him to notice it.

"If you can't get out?" Ron asked.

"Someone already outside has to notice," Harry said.

Remus sat near the fire, his stick resting against one knee. "Or someone who can reach them has to carry the message. Neither method should depend upon the object which has stopped working."

"Not another mirror in the same pocket," Hermione said.

Harry looked at the line separating the two positions. He remembered watching Ron cover Ginny while he found a door that opened. The difficult part of the exercise had been standing still long enough to let somebody else perform the part he could not.

"We can practise that," he said.

"We should," Cedric replied from the doorway.

Ron passed him the remaining sandwiches without asking why he had come.

"I heard the test affected your exceptions," Cedric said. "I'd like the account before someone turns it into a rumour about the mirrors being confiscated."

Hermione offered him her notes with the personal relay details covered. McGonagall had permitted them to share the observed failures; Cedric did not need the names of the people answering the other ends. He read beside the window while Draconia wrote to her parents. The classroom door remained open. Students passed it on their way to dinner. A second-year stopped to ask whether Professor Lupin would be teaching them again; Remus smiled and said not this term. Harry watched the boy leave disappointed, then saw Remus straighten the visitor's card where it had caught beneath his coat.

"You could come to one of our practices," Harry said.

Remus looked up.

"If McGonagall can arrange a session we're actually allowed to hold. Not as an excuse for us to stop doing the teaching. I'd like you to see what we've been working on."

"We'd need permission for practical Defence, not just for my visit," Remus said. "And would the others want me there?"

"We can ask them."

Remus glanced towards Cedric, who nodded through a mouthful of sandwich. His expression softened.

"Then ask. I would like that."

Ron leaned towards Harry. "Does this mean homework?"

"Only if you keep interrupting," Remus said.

Ron sat back. The response had come quickly enough to remind Harry of Defence lessons before there were visitor's cards to consider. For a moment he could picture Remus standing at the front of the room, chalk upon one sleeve, looking pleased because somebody had finally worked out what a creature was trying to do.

Flitwick sent their stones back with a note just before they went to dinner. The first check had found enough to require a proper explanation in the morning. Harry put his on slowly; Draconia fastened hers, met his eyes and left the note upon Hermione's notebook. They could ask the questions together.

Narcissa called after dinner with news from the practice. Harry remained beside Hermione because she asked him to. They had permission to use the same classroom after dinner. Draconia held the notebook in which they had recorded the review questions, and Hermione pulled her chair closer to Harry's so they could both be seen in the glass. The fire had settled since the afternoon. A red seam glowed beneath the newest log, brightening whenever the draught reached it. Sirius had returned to Hawthorn with Remus and called to tell Harry they were home. Harry had seen Remus in the kitchen behind him, hanging the borrowed scarf over a chair. His own mirror was closed now; Hermione's separate handpiece held Narcissa's face.

The inspectors had attended that morning. One attempted to enter a treatment room while a patient was inside. Mrs Granger stopped him at the door, and Narcissa asked him to identify the part of his authority permitting the interruption of Muggle medical treatment. He spent the next quarter of an hour consulting his papers in the waiting room.

Hermione put a hand over her mouth.

"Was Mum all right?"

"She was furious. Your father had to stop her asking whether the Ministry intended to compensate the patient for the additional appointment."

"He probably wanted to ask it himself."

"He did. He preferred that I ask."

Harry smiled. Hermione's mother had sounded brave by the hearth; he was relieved to imagine her sounding annoyed somewhere else. The result was less comforting. McGonagall's objection had kept the inspectors out of the treatment rooms, but the review remained open. School correspondence had been inventoried without being copied. The Grangers would receive further questions in writing. Nothing authorised the seizure of Hermione's personal letters; Narcissa had obtained that statement beneath both officials' signatures.

"Your parents would like you to call on Saturday," she said. "They have requested the appointment themselves."

Hermione nodded, then realised she had lowered the mirror towards her lap. She brought it back up so Narcissa could see her.

"Thank you."

"I'm glad I could be there. Your mother made it very clear where she wanted me to stand."

"I'm glad you were there."

"Though you might ask your father to stop offering Lucius a demonstration of the drill. I believe they have begun encouraging one another."

The laugh surprised Hermione. It lasted only a second before she pressed her free hand against Harry's wrist, needing somewhere to put the relief. Draconia asked about Theodore's solicitor. Narcissa confirmed that, with Theodore's permission, she had put him in touch with an independent adviser. He had since sent the letter himself, and the answer would go to him. She had told Lucius she was helping him find advice, without giving him the private letter. According to Narcissa, he had several opinions about the claim and would be sending none of them in place of the adviser's answer.

"The letter asks him to reply by tomorrow," she said. "The adviser has asked for more time and written to Theodore to say he should not confirm the list before it has been checked. Professor Snape has put the school's position in writing too. The answer is on its way to him; you can tell him that much if you see him first."

Draconia wrote it down and underlined the sentence once. Harry remembered Theodore holding the envelope against his leg and hoped the answer would reach him before he spent another night deciding whether silence could be used against him. He expected her to return to the notebook; instead, she put both away. The jam mark was still upon the envelope. She pressed her thumb against it, then pushed the envelope beneath the strap of her bag, where it would not slip between the books.

"I was going to rewrite the whole contact plan tonight," she said. "I thought I would feel better once I'd done it."

"Would you?" Draconia asked.

"For about ten minutes."

Harry stretched his legs towards the hearth. He found a sandwich crumb upon his jumper, brushed it away and missed the plate. Draconia picked it off the arm of the chair.

"You could just let it stay there," he said.

"And listen to you complain when it gets into your sleeve?"

"I might have been brave about it."

She smiled at him. "You've eaten half a plate of sandwiches. I'd rather not have you sharing the leftovers with your clothes."

"We can look at the plan tomorrow," he said. "When we've heard Flitwick's results. I'll help—you don't have to write it tonight."

Hermione opened her mouth, then stopped. Her hand was already reaching towards the notebook. She looked at it for a moment before drawing it back. Then she glanced at her bag and leaned back against the chair.

"All right. But you're both reading it when we've finished."

Harry let his head rest against the cushion. Sirius and Remus had reached Hawthorn; Hermione could speak to her parents on Saturday. The notice was still in her bag, and Flitwick's folded note remained upon the table. He wanted answers to both. For the moment, the fire was warm against his shins, and he could hear Hermione set down her cup without immediately reaching for a quill. Draconia's chair creaked as she settled back. Harry let himself sit with them, too sleepy to think beyond the classroom and the few minutes they still had before they needed to leave.

Draconia reached for his hand beneath the edge of his sleeve.

"Next Sunday?" she asked.

Harry turned towards her.

"The greenhouse?"

"Somewhere without the judgemental plant."

"It'll miss us."

"Then it should have been nicer while we were there."

Hermione laughed into her cup. Harry caught Draconia's eye, still surprised by how plainly she could want another afternoon with him, and nodded. Outside, footsteps passed the open classroom door. Inside, the fire needed another log. Harry got up, fetched one from the basket and set it carefully upon the embers. A few sparks rose behind the screen. When he turned back, Draconia had moved her chair closer to his; Hermione was reaching for the cup she had forgotten to finish.

"What are we doing on Sunday, then?" Draconia asked as he sat.

"I'm arranging it."

"You've said that before."

"It went well."

She glanced at Hermione, who smiled into her tea. Harry took Draconia's hand again and began considering where Neville might keep plants with fewer opinions.
