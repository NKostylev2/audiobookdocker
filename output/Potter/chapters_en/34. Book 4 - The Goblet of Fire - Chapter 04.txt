4

Dumbledore arrived at three minutes past two the following afternoon.

The cottage announced him differently from every other visitor. Hermione and Draconia had drawn two chimes from the wards; Hagrid had made them ring continuously until he remembered to duck beneath the outer arch. Dumbledore produced a single clear note that travelled through the stone, the floorboards and the water glasses waiting on the sitting-room table. Every flame in the house bent towards the front door.

Sirius noticed.

"Show-off," he muttered.

Harry sat between Hermione and Draconia on the green sofa, his new journal unopened on his knees. The brass key hung beneath his shirt, warm from the afternoon and the nervous hand that had checked it several times without reason. Remus occupied an armchair near the fireplace. A full night's sleep had restored some colour to his face, though the scars beneath his eyes had not entirely faded. Sirius stood behind Harry instead of taking the remaining seat, and no one suggested that he sit.

The six locks opened in sequence. Dumbledore entered wearing deep blue robes light enough for summer, with silver stars stitched along the cuffs. His beard was caught beneath a leather strap across one shoulder, and the travel bag attached to it appeared too small to contain anything useful. He removed his hat.

"Good afternoon."

"You're late," Sirius said.

Dumbledore checked the narrow watch on his wrist, where seven hands pointed in different directions.

"By three minutes."

"Harry has been waiting since one."

Harry turned. "You said not to mention that."

"I said I wouldn't."

"You just did."

Dumbledore's eyes moved between them.

"Then perhaps we might consider my lateness an opportunity for valuable communication."

Sirius did not smile. Harry did, and Dumbledore's attention settled upon him. It always felt different from being watched by an ordinary person. Most people noticed surfaces first: the scar, the glasses, the resemblance to parents Harry barely remembered. Dumbledore seemed to begin somewhere behind all three and work outwards. Harry held his gaze.

"Happy birthday for yesterday," Dumbledore said.

"Thank you."

"I am told the celebration was eventful."

"Sirius was assaulted by a banner."

"Lucius was holding it," Sirius added.

"Then the matter may require further investigation."

Dumbledore placed his hat beside the door and approached the sitting room, his gaze passing briefly over Hermione and Draconia.

"I had expected to speak with Harry, Sirius and Professor Lupin."

"They're staying," Harry said.

Dumbledore paused. The response had come out sharper than Harry intended, but he did not correct it.

"They have experience I don't," he continued.

Draconia's posture became rigid beside him. Hermione touched the notebook hidden near her hip.

Dumbledore inclined his head. "Then I am grateful for their presence."

Some of the tension left the sofa. Instead of taking the empty armchair, Dumbledore conjured a straight-backed seat and placed it opposite Harry, preventing the conversation from gathering around him as though he were holding court. Sirius noticed that too, though he remained standing.

"Before we begin," Dumbledore said, "I wish to make something clear. I do not intend to enter your mind today."

Harry had not known how firmly he expected the opposite until relief loosened his shoulders. Draconia did not relax.

"What do you intend?" she asked.

"To examine the magic surrounding Harry's scar, hear his account and determine whether teaching him to defend against intrusion would reduce or worsen the connection."

"You said you would not enter his mind."

"I will not."

"Then how can you determine what happens inside it?"

Dumbledore regarded her.

"Through Harry's account, external magical evidence and the limits of my own certainty."

The final admission changed the room. Draconia looked briefly towards Harry, then nodded.

"Very well."

Hermione opened her notebook. Sirius came around the sofa and sat on its arm.

"If Harry says stop, everything stops."

"Of course."

"Immediately."

"Yes."

"No explanation required."

Dumbledore folded his hands. "I understand the meaning of stop, Sirius."

"People often do until Harry says it."

Harry turned towards him, but Sirius kept his face directed at Dumbledore. The sentence contained more than nightmares or magical examinations. A cupboard stood inside it, and a barred window, and every adult who had once decided Harry's discomfort mattered less than their reason. Dumbledore heard that too.

"Then I shall endeavour not to join their number," he said.

He opened the travel bag. Its interior held far more space than its exterior suggested, and Dumbledore withdrew a silver bowl, three glass rods, a narrow lens mounted in a brass frame and a folded piece of black cloth. He arranged them on the low table while Hermione leaned forwards, following each movement.

"What do those measure?"

"Magical resonance."

"Whose?"

"Harry's first."

"And then?"

One of Dumbledore's eyebrows lifted. "You do intend to make this difficult."

"I intend to understand it."

"An admirable distinction."

He invited Harry to place his wand inside the silver bowl. Harry drew it from Draconia's holster, the movement smooth enough for Dumbledore to glance towards his wrist.

"A thoughtful design."

"Thank you," Draconia said before Harry could.

Harry laid down the wand. Pale light gathered around holly and phoenix feather without touching either, following the curve from handle to tip. Dumbledore passed the first glass rod through it, and the end turned gold.

"That colour is yours," he explained. "Core magic, undisturbed."

The second rod entered near the wand's centre. A thread of white travelled through the gold.

"Your mother's protection."

Harry stared. The light looked too small. For thirteen years, adults had spoken of Lily's sacrifice as something vast enough to survive death, blood and Voldemort's return. Inside the bowl, it appeared no wider than sewing thread. He watched it hold its place, delicate and unbroken, while the gold moved around it.

"Why is it white?" Hermione asked.

"Colour is an interpretation supplied by the instrument. White indicates magic which continues to act without drawing power from the caster."

"Because she's dead," Harry said.

Dumbledore's fingers paused on the third rod.

"Because the choice was completed."

The answer did not soften anything. Harry appreciated that. Dumbledore lifted the final rod, which was made from smoky glass.

"This should respond to magic within the scar."

Sirius leaned closer. "Should?"

"No two curse scars are identical."

"No one else has this one," Harry said.

"Precisely."

Dumbledore lowered the rod into the bowl. For several seconds, the gold and white remained unchanged. Then a thin red line appeared inside the smoke. It pressed against the glass as though searching for a flaw, travelling from one end to the other without mixing with Harry's magic. Draconia stopped breathing, and Hermione's quill went still above the page.

The red line reached the tip. Harry's scar burned.

He jerked backwards, and the sitting room vanished. Stone walls rose around him. A fire bent low, and something pale moved near the floor, long enough to circle a chair. Harry heard breath that belonged to no healthy body and the scrape of metal against glass.

His own name entered the dark. It had not been spoken; someone else had remembered it.

Harry reached for the key. Brass pressed into his palm through the fabric of his shirt, and the cottage returned in pieces: the green sofa, blue robes, Hermione saying his name. Draconia's fingers were locked around his left wrist. Sirius was already on his feet.

"Stop," Harry said.

Dumbledore pulled the rod from the bowl. The red line disappeared, every instrument went dark, and Sirius's wand pointed at Dumbledore's chest.

"What did you do?"

"Sirius," Remus said.

"What did you do?"

Dumbledore did not raise his own wand.

"The resonance appears to have activated the connection."

"You said external evidence."

"I did not expect—"

"You didn't know."

"No."

Harry pressed two fingers to his scar. The pain had already begun to retreat, leaving his skin hot and the room slightly too bright.

"Put the wand down," he said.

Sirius did not.

"Sirius."

His godfather looked at him. Harry nodded once, and the wand lowered. Dumbledore covered the silver bowl with the black cloth, his movements stripped of their effortless certainty.

"I am sorry, Harry."

Harry breathed through the last pulse beneath his skin.

"Did you see it?"

"No."

"Then what was the point?"

The question came from anger, but Dumbledore accepted it as though Harry had asked academically.

"We learnt that the foreign magic within your scar remains separate from your own. We also learnt that attempting to draw it into the open strengthens the connection in both directions."

Hermione's quill resumed.

"Both?"

Dumbledore turned towards her. "Harry felt Voldemort. Voldemort may have felt the examination."

Sirius swore. Harry stared at the black cloth covering the bowl.

"He remembered me."

No one spoke.

"That's what it felt like," Harry said. "Not like he saw the room. Like my name passed through his head."

Draconia still held his wrist, her thumb resting over his pulse. Harry concentrated on that small, steady pressure. Her fingers were cool; his pulse was not. The contrast gave him one present fact that belonged wholly to the cottage, and the last trace of the stone room loosened its hold.

"Can he find the cottage?"

"No," Dumbledore answered. "The wards conceal place as well as presence. A name is not a direction."

"He knows Harry is with Sirius," Hermione said.

"He likely assumed as much after Pettigrew's capture."

"That is not reassuring."

"No," Dumbledore agreed.

Sirius began pacing between the sofa and the mantel.

"Then we close it."

"Perhaps we can."

"Not perhaps."

"Mental magic does not respond to insistence."

"Everything responds eventually."

Remus rose and intercepted him near the fireplace. Beside Harry, Draconia had gone pale, her gaze fixed on his face with a concentration that made his own fear seem briefly less important.

"I'm here," he said.

Her eyes returned to his.

"I know."

She studied him for another second, as though seeing him upright mattered more than hearing the answer. Only then did some of the fear leave her expression. She did not release his wrist.

Dumbledore returned the lens to his bag.

"Formal Occlumency training would be unwise at present."

"Why?" Hermione asked.

"Because most traditional instruction begins by asking the student to expose thoughts before learning to defend them. The teacher applies controlled pressure; the student identifies the intrusion and resists."

Draconia's hand tightened.

"No."

The word was quiet and absolute. Dumbledore turned towards her.

"I agree."

She appeared surprised. "Then what do you suggest?"

"That Harry first strengthen his ability to distinguish his own thoughts, sensations and memories from those entering through the scar. No intrusion. No forced exposure."

Hermione wrote quickly. "How?"

"Anchoring. Sensory identification. Deliberate recall. The methods Professor Lupin has used with Harry provide a starting point."

"Five things he can see," Sirius said.

"Among others."

Draconia released Harry's wrist only to fold her arms.

"Most books describe clearing the mind."

"Most books were written by wizards who mistook absence for control," Dumbledore replied.

"If he empties it, there is more room for someone else."

Dumbledore's expression altered. He understood that she was no longer speaking theoretically.

"Yes," he said.

Draconia looked down, and Hermione shifted nearer without touching her.

"Harry should not be taught that fear, anger or memory are failures," Draconia continued. "The diary used those things because I concealed them. It made secrecy feel like strength."

Sirius stopped pacing. No one interrupted her.

"If you teach him to hide everything even from himself," she said, "you will not make his mind empty. You will make it unwitnessed."

Harry felt the sentence strike at the centre of everything the previous year had forced them to learn. Dumbledore removed his glasses, and for a moment he looked his age.

"You are right."

Draconia lifted her face. Adults rarely said those words to her without preparing a qualification, but Dumbledore added none.

"Then how do we help him?" Hermione asked.

The we came as naturally as breathing. Dumbledore replaced his glasses.

"Harry chooses several memories, objects or sensations belonging unquestionably to his present. When the scar activates, he identifies them. He names where he is, who is with him and what he knows to be true."

"The key," Sirius said.

Harry touched it beneath his shirt.

"The compass," Hermione added.

"His wand," Draconia said. "The holster responds only to him."

Remus returned to his chair. "A Patronus memory may help."

Harry shook his head.

"Too much magic?"

"Too easy for fear to ruin."

He thought of the lake, his father's shape and every fragile thing gathered into one stag. The memory remained powerful, and risking it felt worse because of that. He wanted to keep something of it untouched, however useful it might be.

Dumbledore accepted the refusal.

"Then choose something smaller."

Harry let his attention travel around the room. In the silver frame on the mantel, Hermione reached for the bird, Draconia laughed, and Sirius checked Harry's shoulder. Remus smiled behind them. The familiar movements had repeated all morning without losing what they meant.

"That," Harry said.

Sirius followed his gaze. "The photograph?"

"The moment."

Draconia's hand brushed the green ribbon tied around Hermione's plait. Hermione looked at her.

"All right."

They built the first anchor together. Harry described the garden after rain, the damp wall beneath him, Hermione on his right and Draconia on his left. Sirius's hand had rested on his shoulder; Hagrid's beard had been above everyone. He remembered the paper bird landing in his hair and the sudden movement on either side of him.

Whenever he slipped into explanation, Dumbledore returned him to sensation. What colour had the sky been? Where had his feet rested? Whose voice had he heard first? Harry answered until the memory ceased being a photograph and became somewhere he could enter deliberately, with cool stone beneath his palms and laughter close to his ears.

"Now state one thing which remained true before, during and after the moment," Dumbledore said.

Harry considered. I am safe felt too absolute. Safety changed; basilisks entered schools and Dementors surrounded lakes. A bedroom could belong to him and still contain nightmares. Voldemort cannot reach me was already uncertain.

Hermione waited. Draconia did not offer an answer for him, and the room held every person he had needed to find.

"I wasn't alone."

Dumbledore nodded.

"That does not promise you will never be separated."

"I know."

"Nor does it mean others can always protect you."

"I know."

"What does it mean?"

Harry closed his hand around the key.

"That I know how to find them."

Sirius looked away. Hermione wrote the sentence at the top of a clean page, and Draconia took the quill to underline it once.

The World Cup was approved an hour later. Dumbledore could not promise that Nott was outside Britain, nor explain why Voldemort's thoughts had reached Harry more clearly. The danger remained, but the Ministry had established anti-Apparition barriers, identity wards and more than two hundred officials around the stadium. Sirius, Remus, Lucius and Narcissa would stay close, with Harry's emergency mirror linked to all four.

"If your scar hurts," Sirius said, "you tell us."

"Immediately," Hermione added.

"Before deciding whether it matters," Draconia finished.

Harry sighed. "I liked the party better."

Dumbledore packed the last silver rod.

"Parties generally involve fewer conditions."

"You have not attended one organised by Hermione."

Hermione shut her notebook. "There were only twenty-three."

Sirius stared at her. "Conditions?"

"Considerations."

"That is worse."

Dumbledore smiled. Before leaving, he placed a hand against Harry's shoulder.

"Joy is not carelessness," he said quietly. "Do not let anyone persuade you that fear is the price of being prepared."

Harry glanced at Sirius. Dumbledore's eyes followed.

"That advice may be shared."

Sirius folded his arms. "Your three minutes are becoming expensive."

Dumbledore put on his hat. The wards released him with another single note, and every flame straightened after the door closed.

The remaining weeks of summer acquired a shape.

Mornings began with the cottage pipes knocking behind the walls and Sirius insisting that the noise meant they were working. Harry learnt which stair complained, which kitchen cupboard needed lifting before it closed and how far the bathroom window had to remain open before steam caused the mirror to offer unsolicited advice.

He learnt that Sirius drank coffee until Remus replaced the third cup with tea, and that Remus read at breakfast and forgot to eat anything placed beside his book. Sirius could cook three meals competently: soup, sausages and toast. Harry's fourth attempt at eggs succeeded, making him the most qualified person in the house.

They went into the village for a few more clothes. Sirius bought enough shirts to make Narcissa declare the situation tolerable, along with a leather jacket nearly identical to his own.

"He is fourteen," Remus said when they returned.

"He gets cold."

"It is August."

"Preparedness."

Harry wore the jacket despite the heat, and the village noticed. Mrs Bell at the post office asked whether he had joined a motorbike gang. The butcher said the resemblance was unmistakable and gave Sirius credit for corrupting him efficiently. At the grocer's, a woman Harry had never met smiled at them both.

"Your son has your taste in clothes."

Sirius went still beside the peaches. Harry felt the mistake pass between them. It was not an unpleasant one, which almost made it worse.

"Godson," Sirius corrected at last, his voice rougher than the word deserved. "His parents had considerably better judgement."

The woman apologised, and Harry assured her it was fine. They escaped with their shopping and two orange ice lollies, which began melting before they reached the bridge. Sirius leaned on its low stone wall and watched the river move below.

"Sorry," he said.

Harry licked juice from his wrist. "For what?"

"That being strange."

"Everything about us is strange."

"Fair." Sirius turned the wooden stick between his fingers. "People may assume things. With you living at the cottage. With me being—"

"Old?"

"Devastatingly handsome."

"In photographs from 1979."

Sirius shoved his shoulder. Harry nearly lost the ice lolly and shoved him back.

"With me being responsible for you," Sirius finished. "I don't want you thinking you have to play along because it makes me happy."

Harry considered the river, where sunlight broke across the surface in white pieces. No one had ever worried that he might pretend to belong for an adult's benefit. The Dursleys had required the opposite.

"I didn't mind," he said.

Sirius studied him as though the answer were fragile and he needed to know where it might break.

"Good," he said quietly.

They carried the shopping home between them. Sirius took the heavier bag only after Harry permitted it, and Harry wore the jacket again the next day.

Some afternoons belonged to lessons. Remus taught shield variations beside the stream, while Sirius turned every exercise into a duel until Harry sent him backwards over the garden wall with a charm neither man expected. Padfoot emerged from the brambles carrying a rosebush and refused to transform until Harry stopped laughing.

Hermione visited with books about mental magic. Draconia arrived with none and asked better questions than most of them. They practised anchors in the orchard, sitting beneath the apple trees or walking where the stream could be heard through the branches. Hermione would interrupt without warning.

"Where are you?"

"Beside the second apple tree."

"What can you feel?"

"Grass, the key and Draconia's elbow."

Draconia did not move it.

"What do you know?"

"That you enjoy this too much."

"Harry."

"I'm not alone."

Draconia asked later, when the exercise no longer felt like one.

"Where are you?"

Once, Harry answered, "The kitchen," while they sat beside the stream. Hermione panicked. Draconia threw an apple at him.

The ordinary photographs returned from the chemist. Some were blurred; one contained only Hagrid's elbow. In another, Draconia lunged towards the camera while Hermione's hand covered half the lens. The final photograph was perfect. The three of them sat beneath Harry's windows, caught mid-argument, with Hermione facing Draconia and Harry laughing between them.

Unlike the magical picture downstairs, nobody repeated the moment. Harry placed it on his desk, where he could see it from the bed.

At night, he spoke to Sirius through open doors rather than mirrors. Nightmares continued, but most belonged to Harry: the Chamber, Dementors, a cupboard too small for the body he possessed now. He woke, named the room and found the key beneath his shirt. Voldemort did not return, and the absence became its own kind of pressure. Each painless morning felt like a held breath, though no one said so aloud.

The World Cup drew closer. Flags appeared in Diagon Alley, and newspapers printed team histories, predictions and photographs of Viktor Krum scowling beneath heavy eyebrows. Harry supported Ireland for its Chasers. Draconia declared Bulgaria's Seeker more decisive than Ireland's entire side. Hermione refused to choose.

"Neutrality," Draconia informed her, "is often fear wearing respectable clothes."

"It is a sporting event."

"Exactly. There are no moral consequences."

"Then why do you care which side I choose?"

"I care that you choose."

Hermione bought an Irish rosette the next morning and claimed it was coincidence. No one believed her.

On 22 August, Harry woke before the alarm.

The cottage was dark, and moonlight silvered the ceiling. The photograph on his desk existed only as three faint shapes. For several seconds, he listened for whatever had disturbed him, searching the quiet for a voice inside the walls. Nothing moved. His scar did not hurt.

Excitement had woken him. Harry smiled into the dark.

He dressed without lighting the lamp, pulling on jeans, the green jumper Narcissa had given him and the leather jacket Sirius had bought. The brass key disappeared beneath his collar; his wand slid into Draconia's holster. The clock showed 3:41. When a cupboard door opened downstairs, Harry drew his wand and left the room.

He found Sirius in the kitchen wearing boots, dark trousers and an Ireland scarf, his hair tied back. Several pieces of toast occupied his mouth while he searched a drawer.

"Morning," Harry said.

Sirius struck his head on the cupboard, and the toast fell.

"Excellent vigilance," Harry added.

Sirius straightened. "You are supposed to be asleep."

"So are you."

"I am responsible for departure."

"You've lost breakfast."

They looked at the toast on the floor. Padfoot would have eaten it, but Sirius picked it up and put it in the bin with visible regret.

"Coffee?"

"I'm fourteen."

"Tea?"

"That sounds worse."

Harry opened the icebox and took out orange juice. On the table lay a folded security plan, three emergency mirrors, tickets, maps and a list in Hermione's handwriting. Beside them stood the brass alarm clock that would become their Portkey. It had one hand and no numbers.

"That's it?"

"Ministry approved."

"It looks broken."

"That prevents Muggles wanting it."

"I don't want it."

"Proof of effectiveness."

Remus entered wearing a coat over his nightshirt.

"Why is no one asleep?"

"He started it," Harry and Sirius said together.

Remus closed his eyes.

By four-thirty, the cottage was awake. They ate standing at the kitchen table while Sirius checked the tickets twice and Remus checked the tent charms. Harry went through his mirror, wand, key and compass until Sirius caught him reaching for the mirror again.

"It remains there."

"You checked the tickets twice."

"Tickets move."

"Mirrors don't?"

Sirius looked at Remus. "He has become argumentative."

"Recent development," Remus replied.

They carried their packs through the garden, and Sirius put the alarm clock on the outdoor table. Dawn had not yet reached the valley. Mist lay across the orchard, turning the trees into dark islands, and the air smelled of wet grass. Above the wards, stars remained visible. The Portkey would activate at five.

Hermione and her parents arrived through the outer gate at 4:48. Mr Granger wore a waxed coat and hiking boots. Mrs Granger carried a handbag large enough to hold medical supplies for a small village. Hermione had plaited her hair tightly and attached the Irish rosette near her collar. Harry pointed to it.

"Coincidence?"

"Green suits me."

"That's not green."

"It has green."

Sirius greeted the Grangers while Harry took Hermione's pack. Its weight pulled his arm towards the ground.

"What's in this?"

"Essentials."

"Books?"

"Some."

"How many?"

"The number is not important."

Harry tried lifting it again. "The number is dangerous."

The Malfoys arrived at 4:53 in travelling cloaks too elegant for a damp field. Lucius carried nothing. Narcissa held a small silver bag, and Draconia wore a Bulgarian scarf around her neck, her luggage reduced to a case the size of a matchbox.

Hermione looked at it.

"Extension charm?"

"Professionally applied."

"Those are regulated."

"Yes."

"You cannot bring an entire wardrobe."

"I did not."

Narcissa glanced at her daughter. "She removed two cases."

Harry took the tiny case from Draconia.

"Heavy?"

"Only emotionally."

He slipped it into his pocket. Draconia's eyes rested on the leather jacket.

"Black?"

"Apparently."

"You look like him."

Harry glanced towards Sirius, who stood beside the alarm clock explaining Portkey travel to the Grangers with broad, unhelpful gestures.

"Is that terrible?"

Draconia's mouth curved. "Not today."

Her gaze remained on him after the answer, travelling briefly over the jacket before returning to his face. The leather suddenly felt warmer than the August morning accounted for.

The clock hand moved, and everyone formed a circle around the table.

"One finger," Lucius instructed. "The Portkey will do the rest."

Mr Granger examined the clock. "How?"

"Poorly," Sirius said.

"Ignore him," Remus replied.

"Has Harry travelled this way?" Narcissa asked.

All eyes moved to him.

"No."

Draconia changed position until she stood on his left. Hermione moved to his right.

"You don't need to—"

Each caught one of his sleeves, leaving him unable to complete the objection with much conviction.

"This seems excessive."

"You cannot steer," Hermione said.

"And you are likely to try," Draconia added.

The hand reached the top of the clock face. A hook caught Harry behind the navel, and the garden disappeared.

Wind became every direction at once. His shoes left the ground, colour stretched around him in long, indistinguishable lines, and Hermione's grip tightened on his right sleeve. Draconia vanished from sight but remained attached to the left. Someone's knee struck his pack, and Sirius shouted an instruction that reached him too late to understand.

Then the invisible hook released. Harry hit grass, rolled, lost one shoe and stopped against something solid enough to grunt.

"Morning," Cedric Diggory said.

Harry pushed himself upright. They had landed in a field crowded with people, luggage and Portkeys in various stages of arrival. Mist clung low to the grass, and the eastern sky had begun to pale behind a line of woods, though lanterns still burned on tall poles throughout the clearing. Cedric sat where Harry had knocked him down, one hand planted behind him, wearing a yellow-and-black scarf and an expression caught between concern and laughter.

"Sorry," Harry said.

"Better than the family from Norwich. They arrived with a wardrobe."

Hermione landed on both knees beside the alarm clock. Draconia remained upright for almost half a second before momentum pulled her backwards into Sirius, who caught her beneath the arms. Lucius appeared next and struck the ground shoulder-first. His daughter looked at him.

"Excellent technique."

Lucius rose with dignity assembled from limited materials.

"Portkeys are designed to punish elegance."

Narcissa landed beside him on both feet, steadied the silver bag on her arm and surveyed the field as though she had chosen the arrival herself. Lucius chose not to look at her. Mr Granger landed sitting down; Mrs Granger caught his coat, preventing him from falling and causing them both to rotate once before collapsing together.

"That," Mr Granger said from the grass, "was appalling."

His wife began laughing. Remus arrived last, stepped neatly away from the clock and picked up Harry's missing shoe.

"How?" Sirius demanded.

"Practice."

"You fell through a table in 1977."

"Practice since."

Harry pulled on his shoe and accepted Remus's hand. Cedric had climbed to his feet. He was taller than Harry remembered from the Quidditch pitch, broad-shouldered and brown-haired, with an open expression that made strangers seem to recognise him. A travelling cloak lay folded over one arm. His father stood several yards away, speaking to a Ministry witch in a green coat.

Amos Diggory saw Harry.

"Ced!"

The shout crossed the field, turning several heads. Amos waved with the enthusiasm of someone directing a rescue party.

"That's Harry Potter!"

Harry closed his eyes briefly. Cedric winced.

"He knows."

Amos came towards them, a broad man with a brown beard and the pleased confidence of someone whose morning had produced exactly the meeting he wanted.

"Harry!" He seized Harry's hand. "Amos Diggory. Department for the Regulation and Control of Magical Creatures."

"We met after the Quidditch match," Harry said.

"Of course! Cedric beat you."

Cedric stared at the mist. Sirius moved nearer.

"Dementors entered the grounds," Harry replied. "They did most of the beating."

"Still caught the Snitch, didn't he?"

"Dad," Cedric said.

Amos beamed at his son. "Modest. Always has been."

"Mr Diggory," Hermione said, stepping beside Harry, "a match compromised by more than a hundred Dark creatures is not statistically useful."

Amos's smile faltered. Draconia joined them.

"Nor socially useful, at present."

Cedric looked at both girls, then at Harry.

"You travel with a defence team?"

"They're usually less polite."

"We are adjusting for company," Draconia said.

Sirius held out his hand to Amos.

"Sirius Black."

Recognition moved across the older man's face. Enthusiasm stumbled into uncertainty before Ministry manners rescued it.

"Black. Yes. Heard about the hearing."

"Most people did."

"Terrible business."

"Being innocent usually is."

Amos shook the offered hand and released it quickly. Cedric intervened before either man could improve the conversation.

"We're at campsite three. Hufflepuff colours on the tallest tent."

"Subtle," Harry said.

"Dad."

Amos appeared proud of this too. Cedric lowered his voice.

"Come by later if you want. A few people from school are here."

"We will," Hermione answered.

Draconia looked at her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You made a face."

"Your authority over our schedule is developing rapidly."

Harry picked up the Ministry clock.

"We can add a procedure."

Hermione took the Portkey from him before he dropped it.

"You are not helping."

Cedric laughed, easing the last of the old Quidditch awkwardness.

"See you later, Harry."

Harry nodded. "See you."

As they left, Amos's voice carried behind them.

"Good lad, that Potter. Not as tall as the papers make him look."

Sirius turned round. Remus caught the back of his jacket.

"Campsite," he reminded him.

"I only wanted to discuss accurate reporting."

"Later."

The World Cup occupied the valley beyond the arrival field. Harry saw the flags first, rising above hedges, trees and tents in their thousands: Irish green, Bulgarian red and white, Swedish gold and blue, Japanese sunbursts, Kenyan lions, and countries he knew only from maps. Some flew from ordinary poles; others swam through the air like fish. A Welsh dragon banner breathed small flames whenever an Irish supporter passed beneath it.

The campsite spread across several farms. Muggle tents occupied the outer rows in disciplined lines, but deeper inside, magical attempts at concealment became increasingly unconvincing. One tent possessed a smoking chimney. Another had grown a second storey overnight and was attempting to disguise it with painted clouds. American wizards had built a canvas eagle whose wings covered three pitches. Ministry officials moved between them, correcting violations with exhausted expressions.

Harry had never seen so many magical people in one place. Diagon Alley felt private by comparison. Hogwarts held hundreds, but they wore uniforms and followed bells. Here, families from across the world crossed muddy paths in brilliant robes, speaking languages he could not identify and carrying magic as openly as umbrellas.

Children chased miniature broomsticks between tents. A witch in orange robes walked three silver foxes on leads. Somewhere beyond the nearest hill, a chorus began an Irish song and was answered by Bulgarian drums. Every path offered some new proof that the world was larger than Harry had been taught to imagine.

Beneath a Nigerian flag, a family cooked breakfast over blue fire without using wands. Two Japanese witches unfolded a paper pavilion until it stood higher than the surrounding tents, its white walls alive with painted cranes. Near the water queue, a Brazilian wizard coaxed rain from a copper bowl and laughed as children ran beneath it with their mouths open. Hermione attempted to identify each spell; Draconia tried to identify the families performing them. Harry wanted only to keep walking.

For years, magic had meant hidden things: letters forced through a slot, a platform behind a barrier, a castle protected from maps and Muggle eyes. Even at Hogwarts, wonder arrived inside boundaries. Staircases led somewhere, lessons ended, and the grounds stopped at a gate. Here, there seemed to be no edge at all.

A wizard in robes embroidered with constellations called a greeting to Lucius. Narcissa answered a French witch whose silver braids reached her waist. Mr Granger was drawn into a conversation with a South African Healer about Muggle dentistry after Hermione translated three technical words neither man had expected to share. Sirius stayed close enough for Harry to find without searching, but did not hurry him.

Harry passed a stall selling team chants in glass bottles and another where tiny clouds rained in national colours. A boy younger than him rode on his father's shoulders, both painted blue. Nearby, a girl in red robes slept across three pieces of luggage while her family argued cheerfully above her. Nobody lowered their voice to say magic, or behaved as though this life might be withdrawn if they enjoyed it too openly.

The happiness pressing beneath Harry's ribs grew sharp enough to resemble fear. He turned slowly, trying to see everything, and the movement pulled the key against his chest.

Where are you?

The World Cup.

What can you feel?

Wet grass. Leather jacket. The mirror in his pocket.

What do you know?

Hermione stood on his right, Draconia on his left. Sirius argued with a Ministry official ten feet ahead. Harry breathed, and excitement returned without tipping into panic.

Hermione watched him.

"Anchor?"

He nodded.

"Did it help?"

"Yes."

Draconia lowered the hand that had nearly reached for his wrist and pretended to adjust her scarf. They followed the adults towards the check-in field.

"Family tradition," Lucius replied.

Sirius coughed. Harry turned his own reaction into something resembling a sneeze.

"Malfoy. Granger. Black."

"Black," he repeated.

Sirius waited.

"You that fellow from the news?"

"Which news?"

"Your wife is also unusually accurate."

His attention wandered, and the pencil slipped from his fingers. A Ministry wizard in plus-fours emerged from behind the cottage and laid a hand on his shoulder.

Mr Roberts blinked.

"Morning," he said. "Three bookings?"

Hermione's face changed.

"You erased him."

"Ever."

"Yes."

"Also yes."

Her expression suggested that the Ministry would shortly regret giving her access to parchment.

"Shacklebolt."

"Yes."

Draconia nodded. Narcissa rested her hand briefly against the centre of her daughter's back.

"Not remotely."

Kingsley left them to the tents. The Grangers began with an ordinary green canvas model. "We spent six summers camping in Wales."

Hermione touched his arm. "Character-building?"

"We slept on the ground."

"Magical people have a flexible relationship with hardship," Sirius said.

"Remus packed."

"You moved it."

"Baseless accusation."

Harry removed the lamp and handed it to him.

"It can guard the entrance."

"That is not a tent."

"It is made of canvas," Draconia said.

"There is a chandelier."

"Camping need not be punitive."

Harry entered behind her. Portraits had been removed from the walls, leaving clean rectangles on green silk. The sitting room held four chairs, two sofas and a fireplace beneath a mantel wide enough to host a small orchestra.

"Subtle."

Draconia lifted her chin. "We removed the piano."

They unpacked quickly. The adults established wards, meal times and three meeting points. Hermione copied the map until everyone had one. Sirius paired mirrors, and Narcissa charmed each child's outer clothing to resist fire, water and Summoning Charms. Harry endured the precautions without complaint, understanding that every completed check brought them closer to being allowed to leave.

At ten, they were released into the campsite, with Remus keeping within sight at a distance that allowed them to feel almost unaccompanied.

"Central paths only," Sirius said.

"We know," Harry replied.

"Stay together."

"We know."

"Mirror every hour."

"You said two."

"I reconsidered."

Remus handed Harry a small bag of coins.

"Two."

Sirius looked betrayed. "Whose side are you on?"

"Harry's."

"That was my side."

"Then you should be pleased."

The trio left before the interval changed again.

By midmorning, the campsite had become another country. Smoke rose from cooking fires, music passed between rows of tents, and vendors carried trays of food, team ribbons and objects designed to make reasonable supporters unbearable.

Harry bought Omnioculars within five minutes. They resembled brass binoculars fitted with dials for replay, speed and commentary. The vendor demonstrated by focusing on a distant wizard tripping over a guy rope, then repeating the fall in slow motion until Hermione insisted that he stop.

"Cruel use of technology," she said.

Harry bought her a pair too.

"Why?"

"So you can analyse Krum's dive."

"I already told you—"

"He will explain it regardless," Draconia said. "The Omnioculars may reduce the duration."

Harry bought her a pair.

"I understand the dive."

"Then you can prove him wrong in slow motion," Hermione said.

Draconia accepted them.

The crowd thickened near the international stalls. Ireland's section glowed green beneath floating shamrocks, with miniature models of the team racing above the awnings. Every few seconds, the figure of Aidan Lynch performed a dive and collided with a basket of rosettes. Harry bought an Ireland scarf and wrapped it around his neck above the leather jacket.

Draconia stared.

"You look confused."

"I'm warm and supportive."

"You look like a tree."

Hermione bought a second rosette.

"For balance," she said when Draconia noticed.

The Bulgarian stalls stood opposite, their red flags snapping without wind. Viktor Krum's miniature likeness neither waved nor performed. It hovered on a tiny broom, scowled at anyone approaching and occasionally caught a gold paper Snitch. Draconia bought one. The small Krum landed on her shoulder, and Harry's miniature Irish Chaser flew up from his scarf to circle it. Krum ignored him.

"Accurate," Draconia said.

"There are three Chasers."

"One Krum."

"Ireland can win without catching the Snitch."

"A flaw in the scoring system."

"It's strategy."

"It rewards failure."

"Tell her," Harry said to Hermione.

"Krum has caught the Snitch in eighty-seven per cent of his international matches," Hermione said.

Draconia smiled.

"Ireland averages a hundred and seventy points before the catch."

Harry smiled. Hermione crossed both arms.

"Those facts do not require me to support either team."

"Your chest says Ireland," Draconia replied.

Hermione's two rosettes began singing. She attempted to silence them, and Harry laughed so hard that his Omnioculars slipped from his hand. Draconia caught the strap before they fell. Around them, a family in Finnish colours moved through one side of the crowd while Irish supporters filled the other, carrying them away from the clear edge of the path.

Draconia's fingers remained around the strap near Harry's chest. Hermione caught his hand, and he turned towards her.

"So we don't get separated," she said.

Draconia released the Omnioculars and took his other hand, fitting her fingers between his without hesitation.

"Procedure thirteen."

"We do not have—"

The rosettes reached their chorus. Hermione glared at them both, and Harry let the crowd carry them forwards.

People opened space when they recognised him. Some pointed after he passed; others said his name. A witch in Australian colours asked for a photograph, and an elderly wizard from Belgium clasped his shoulder and thanked him in a language Hermione believed was French.

The attention felt different from the birthday table. These people knew a story, and their happiness at seeing him carried old fear beneath it: the relief of recognising proof that Voldemort had once failed. Harry's grip tightened on both girls.

"Where are you?" Hermione asked near his shoulder.

"Surrounded by singing."

"Harry."

"International stalls."

"What can you feel?" Draconia asked.

Their joined hands had become the only still point in the crowd.

"Procedure thirteen."

Hermione's thumb moved once against his.

"What do you know?"

The answer came more easily.

"I can find you."

The crowd released them near the food stalls. Neither girl dropped his hand immediately. Then Draconia's miniature Krum caught the Snitch above Harry's shoulder, and she let go to retrieve it. Hermione released him one step later. Harry put both hands in his jacket pockets, noticing the new emptiness.

He bought them all ice cream.

They escaped the main path through a row of smaller stalls near the stream. Drums and singing remained audible, but canvas softened their edges. Artisans sat behind tables of hand-painted broom models, carved mascots and jewellery in team colours. A Romanian witch sold dragon-scale pins that warmed whenever the wearer's side scored. Two elderly wizards from Egypt argued over enchanted maps while the countries on them moved to improve their own borders.

Hermione stopped at every bookstall. Draconia pretended impatience and stopped beside her. Harry wandered between tables with three melting ice creams balanced in his hands.

"Which one is mine?" Hermione asked without looking away from a history of the Irish team.

"Vanilla."

She turned. "I asked for strawberry."

Harry gave her the pink one. "Test."

"Of what?"

"Your commitment."

Draconia took the pale lemon ice from his other hand.

"He forgot which was which."

"I did not."

"You tasted both."

Small marks showed along both edges.

"Quality control."

Hermione wiped a spot of strawberry from the corner of his mouth with her thumb. The movement was so automatic that none of them reacted immediately. Then she saw her own hand and withdrew it.

"Quality was adequate."

Harry forgot his reply. Draconia examined her ice cream with intense concentration, and they continued along the stream.

A stall beneath a striped awning displayed quieter team charms. No singing, sparks or miniature players assaulted its customers; enamel shamrocks, silver lions and broom-shaped clasps rested on dark cloth. Hermione's two rosettes began their chorus again. She pressed both hands against them.

"I regret everything."

The stallholder laughed. "First Cup?"

"Is it obvious?"

"You bought those before breakfast."

Hermione glanced at Harry. He was examining a green shamrock pin no larger than his thumbnail, with a charm that glowed gently whenever someone nearby said Ireland.

"That one does not sing?" he asked.

"Guaranteed."

Harry bought it and handed it to Hermione.

"You already bought the Omnioculars."

"Those were to protect me from explanations."

"And this?"

"Protects everyone from the rosettes."

Her face softened as she looked at the pin.

"Thank you."

She removed the singing decorations and fastened the shamrock near her collar. It gave one quiet pulse of green. Draconia watched, and Harry turned back to the stall.

"Bulgaria?"

The stallholder indicated a tray of red-and-silver pieces.

"I do not require anything," Draconia said.

"Your scarf is coming undone."

It had loosened near her shoulder. She reached for the knot and found the end slipping through. Harry chose a silver broom clasp set with a tiny red stone.

"That is not official Bulgarian merchandise," she said.

"It's red."

"So are many things."

"And a broom."

"Most teams use brooms."

Harry held it out. Draconia's argument stopped, but when she reached for the clasp, the loose end of her scarf slipped again.

"Hold still."

She became very still. Harry stepped closer and gathered the fabric at her shoulder, working the clasp into place. It resisted once before fastening with a quiet click. A strand of pale hair had caught beneath the silver, and he freed it carefully, his knuckles brushing the side of her neck.

Neither spoke. The market continued around them, full of singing rosettes, calling vendors and flags snapping above the awnings, but Draconia watched his face as though the noise had moved several streets away.

The red stone gave a pulse of light against her scarf.

"Acceptable," she decided, her voice not quite as even as usual.

"You're welcome."

Hermione looked between the pin and clasp.

"Did you plan that?"

"I plan several seconds ahead."

"On good days," Draconia added.

They found a quiet patch beside the water and sat on the grass to finish their ice creams. One boat predicted that a mascot would eat the referee and attracted more wagers than it deserved.

Harry and Draconia stared at her.

"A compromise," she said.

"Cowardice wearing arithmetic," Draconia replied.

They found Cedric near the Hufflepuff tents, his father's banner visible from two rows away.

DIGGORY — ENGLAND'S NEXT SEEKER

The words changed every few seconds.

CEDRIC CAUGHT IT FAIRLY

HUFFLEPUFF FOR IRELAND

"Take me with you."

"Can't you remove it?"

"It reappears."

"Your merchandise has opinions."

Cho shifted along the log to make room. Her dark hair had been cut shorter over the summer, and a silver eagle rosette sat near her collar.

Hermione's victory disappeared.

They stayed longer than planned. Conversation became easier once Amos left to argue with a Ministry official about the banner's height. CEDRIC CAUGHT IT FAIRLY

Harry laughed harder. Cedric covered his face with one arm.

"I am setting it on fire."

"Hermione says it reappears."

He stayed nearby while they ate a late lunch outside the tents. Conversation returned slowly. Mr Granger described Portkey travel as an assault disguised as public transport. Remus attempted to read the match programme until Hagrid arrived from another campsite wearing an Irish hat large enough to produce its own rainbow.

The afternoon grew bright and warm. Children raced toy broomsticks beside the stream, and music travelled from camp to camp. The Irish choir acquired drums from Ghana, Bulgarian pipes and French witches who knew none of the words. Gradually, Harry's shoulders loosened.

At four, Sirius took him to buy match programmes. They went alone only as far as the central path, where Remus followed at a distance with Hermione and Draconia, honouring the word alone more symbolically than practically. Sirius bought two programmes, another pair of Omnioculars and an Ireland hat shaped like a dancing shamrock.

"You aren't wearing that," Harry said.

"It is for Remus."

"He isn't wearing it."

"Not voluntarily."

They passed a stall selling commemorative photographs. One showed the Irish team above a green pitch; another contained Krum in mid-dive. Harry considered buying it for Draconia and decided that the miniature on her shoulder had already become unbearable. Sirius picked up a photograph of the stadium.

"James would have hated missing this."

He spoke lightly, with grief at the edges of the sentence instead of claiming all of it. Harry examined the image, where a hundred thousand tiny spectators filled gold seats and flags covered the upper rim.

"Who did he support?"

"Whoever was losing."

"Why?"

"He liked comebacks."

"That sounds inconvenient."

"Your mother supported whichever team's uniforms she preferred. She claimed tactical reasoning, but it changed whenever the robes did."

Harry smiled. "Bulgaria's are better."

Sirius looked at him. "Then why Ireland?"

"Their Chasers."

"Good answer."

They bought the photograph, and Sirius rolled it into the programme.

"Next Cup," he said, "we'll choose the teams before anyone else interferes."

The promise caught Harry more sharply than Sirius could have intended. Four years separated World Cups. Next meant eighteen. It meant summers no longer counted in twenty-one-night intervals, and Sirius expecting them both to be present beyond the immediate danger.

"You'll still choose Ireland," Harry said.

"Only if they deserve it."

"Their Chasers."

"Excellent Chasers."

They continued along the path, and Harry saw the ring outside a tent selling foreign books.

A man stood beneath the awning with his left side turned towards the crowd, wearing ordinary dark robes and gloves despite the warmth. Grey showed above one ear. His hand lifted, and black stone caught the sun. Something thin crossed an eight-pointed star.

Harry stopped. The man's head turned, giving him one glimpse of a narrow face and dark eyes. The other man from the dream.

Nott.

Harry reached for Sirius, but a group of Swedish supporters crossed the path carrying a floating ship. Gold sails filled his vision. He ducked beneath them, trying to keep the awning in sight, and when he straightened, the bookstall stood empty.

"Harry?"

Sirius's hand closed around his shoulder. Harry pointed.

"Nott."

Sirius drew his wand. "Where?"

"The stall. Black ring. Grey hair."

Sirius fired red sparks into the air, followed by gold. The response was immediate. Kingsley appeared from the opposite path with two Aurors, and Lucius reached them seconds later, with Narcissa and Remus behind him. People moved away as the officials sealed the row.

"Description," Kingsley said.

Harry gave it. He did not say that he might have imagined the engraving, or soften his certainty merely because adults were listening. Lucius entered the abandoned stall. Its owner returned carrying tea and objected loudly to Ministry interference. She had not seen the man, and the tent's record charm showed seventeen customers within the previous ten minutes, none registered under Nott's name.

"Disguise," Sirius said.

"Or Harry recognised someone similar," Kingsley replied.

"I know what I saw."

Kingsley met his gaze. "Then we proceed as though you're correct."

He sent Patronus messages throughout the grounds. The search lasted nearly an hour, and nobody found Nott. The security wards recorded no recognised suspect entering the campsite, and no one matching Harry's description appeared at an exit. Three wizards wore black signet rings; none bore the Nott device.

As the search widened, doubt entered through the spaces it left. Harry stood beside the bookstall while people stared, and the familiar questions returned. Had the dream shown him the ring clearly enough? Had Draconia's identification supplied details he later believed he remembered? Could any dark stone have become Nott's because he expected danger?

Sirius came back first.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing yet."

Harry looked at the unattended awning.

"Maybe I was wrong."

"Were you?"

"I don't know."

Sirius lowered his wand.

"Then say that. Do not decide you invented it because we failed to find him."

"Everyone left the campsite to search."

"That is their job."

"The match starts in two hours."

"It will begin without the Department of Magical Law Enforcement enjoying the first goal."

Harry pushed his hands into his jacket pockets.

"You believe me?"

Sirius did not answer immediately.

"I believe you saw a man who matched Nott," he said. "I believe you recognised the ring. I also believe fear and dreams can alter memory."

Harry studied him.

"That's not yes."

"It is the honest answer."

The honesty hurt less than reassurance would have. Sirius touched the back of his neck.

"You told me immediately. That was the right decision whether the man was Nott or a Bulgarian bookseller with unfortunate jewellery."

"Draconia will be pleased."

"She is unbearable."

"Did she say I was right?"

"Repeatedly."

Harry smiled faintly, and they returned to camp. Kingsley increased the patrols. Lucius added another ward around the tents, and Narcissa checked every mirror connection. Hermione recorded the sighting with the care of a witness statement. Draconia asked Harry to draw the ring again before discussing it, preventing the newer memory from replacing the original.

No one accused him of ruining the afternoon. That mattered almost as much as being believed.

At sunset, the stadium revealed itself. Trees had concealed it for most of the day; then thousands of lanterns ignited beyond the ridge, outlining a structure vast enough to contain Hogwarts several times over. Gold walls rose above the forest, towers climbed into the violet sky, and flags streamed from every point, their colours reflected on the clouds.

A roar crossed the valley, and the campsite answered. People emerged from every path in scarves, hats, robes and moving paint. Drums began. Fireworks shaped like shamrocks burst above the trees, chased by a red dragon made from sparks that dissolved into stars.

Harry stood outside the tents with his programme beneath one arm. For the first time since seeing the ring, excitement reached him without permission. Sirius came to stand beside him. He had persuaded Remus to wear the ridiculous hat, which Remus endured because resistance required more energy than he possessed.

"Still want to go?" Sirius asked.

The stadium blazed beyond them. Fear would continue to exist whether Harry entered its gates or remained beneath four layers of wards.

"Yes."

"So do I."

That helped. Hermione joined them with the quiet shamrock pin glowing near her collar. Draconia arrived in Bulgarian red with miniature Krum on her shoulder. He scowled at Harry's scarf.

"Your player is rude," Harry said.

"Focused."

"He's been glaring for nine hours."

"Preparation."

Hermione opened the programme.

"Ireland's starting Chasers are Mullet, Moran and Troy."

Harry stared. "You studied."

"I read the programme."

"That counts."

Draconia examined her. "Name Bulgaria's Beaters."

"Volkov and Vulchanov."

"You chose a side and still researched both?"

Hermione looked offended. "Support does not require ignorance."

Harry linked one arm through each of theirs before the argument could become stationary.

"Come on."

They joined the stream of people moving towards the stadium. Sirius, Remus and the Grangers followed close behind. Lucius and Narcissa walked on either side, watching the crowd with care that no longer pretended to be casual. Kingsley's Aurors remained visible along the path.

The trio stayed joined through the narrowest sections. Hermione complained that Harry pulled whenever he saw merchandise; Draconia accused Hermione of stopping to read every sign. Harry denied both while doing exactly what they described. Near the first gate, they passed Cedric and the Hufflepuffs, and Cho waved beneath a Bulgarian banner. Hagrid appeared farther ahead, unmistakable under his rainbow hat.

Near the Ministry entrance, Harry saw Winky. She climbed the stadium steps slowly, gripping the rail with both hands and glancing every few feet towards the empty space beside her. The boards creaked beneath weight he could not see.

He touched the mirror in his pocket. Sirius followed his attention.

"Kingsley knows," he said.

Harry nodded, and they continued.

The stadium swallowed the crowd. Staircases climbed through gold-lit passages, vendors shouted in a dozen languages, and a hundred thousand voices entered the stone until sound became something physical, vibrating beneath Harry's shoes and behind his ribs.

They climbed with the crowd winding around the immense outer wall. Through open arches, Harry caught fragments of the valley below: campfires between trees, rivers of lantern light along the paths, and the distant arrival field still flashing as late Portkeys struck the grass. Higher up, the air cooled. Wind caught the flags overhead and snapped them hard enough to sound like wings.

Every landing offered another impossible view. Hogwarts' moving staircases seemed almost domestic beside this, narrow pieces of stone serving a castle that understood its limits. Here, ramps crossed empty air and bridges hung between towers. Thousands of witches and wizards moved through them without slowing, each current separating and joining again according to the colours painted across the walls.

Hermione had stopped reading the programme. Draconia's miniature Krum had stopped scowling and stood on her shoulder with his tiny broom raised towards the noise. Harry turned to point this out, then found both girls looking upwards.

The ceiling showed an enchanted night sky, with stars in unfamiliar arrangements. A comet crossed the dark in silence and dissolved into the World Cup emblem.

"All right," Hermione breathed. "That is impressive."

Draconia recovered first. "Moderately."

"You walked into me."

"The crowd altered direction."

Harry grinned and stepped between them before either could pretend not to be astonished.

At the final stair, night opened above them. The pitch lay far below, brilliant green beneath floating lights, with goal hoops rising like golden needles at either end. Every tier blazed with colour. Ireland occupied one half of the world, Bulgaria the other.

Harry stopped at the railing. He had believed Hogwarts was the largest thing magic could build, and Quidditch belonged to school robes, cold stands and friends shouting above the wind. This was a world without ceilings.

Hermione reached the rail beside him, and Draconia appeared on his other side. Her shoulder settled against his as the crowd surged behind them. The red stone in her clasp caught the stadium lights, and when the first great roar rolled over the pitch, she looked at Harry before looking towards the players.

Sirius's hand rested briefly against his shoulder. The announcer's voice crossed the stadium, naming countries, champions and the match for which the magical world had waited four years.

Harry forgot Nott for one breath. Then another.

Above them, a thousand flags rose together.
